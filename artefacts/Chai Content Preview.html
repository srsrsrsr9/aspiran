Three illustrated Chai cards, one per tone

The same six zones the app already uses, with one small drawing and a 300 ms entry. Each card's tone tag sets its look: the light card gets marigold and one Chotu joke, the neutral card gets teal, and the serious card drops colour and moves in half the time.

  • Tap "Next card" or the dots. There is no auto-advance and no "load more".

Article 200 in 48 seconds

A tap-to-play motion explainer of one static concept: the Governor's four options for a state bill. It starts only when you press Play, can be paused at any point, and every line is on screen as a caption, so it works on mute.

  • The scene is drawn by the browser from a small script, the same way the app would draw it from a Lottie file. There is no video file.
  • The music bed is a soft tanpura drone. The real version adds a Hindi or English narrator.
  • It ends on the three-note chai motif, which also closes every reel.

Two soundscapes and the sonic logo

Study sound for Rooms and focus sessions, built from Indian acoustic textures. This preview synthesizes them live in the browser; the real ones are recorded and composed.

  • Off by default, never autoplays, mixed quietly so nothing jumps out at night.

Practice · Timeline story1 / 7

Making the Constitution, 1946 to 1950

An interactive timeline: drag the slider or tap a dot, and each step shows what happened and how long after the last step it came. The gap between dates is the thing aspirants mix up, so it is printed on every step.

  • Dots are evenly spaced so each is easy to tap; the line under each step says how long it took, which shows the long wait from 1947 to 1949.
  • The two dates that recur in exams, 26 November 1949 and 26 January 1950, are marked in teal.
  • The same format suits rivers by state, schemes by year, or Modern History.

Match the Article, a low-stakes game

Six Articles, six meanings, and no timer. A wrong pair gives a small nudge and a "try again"; a right pair locks in with a soft note. No score is ever shown to anyone else.

  • Each round draws 6 pairs from a pool of 14, so it can be played again.
  • Pairs you get wrong would come back in Today's revision at day 3, 10 and 30.
  • Sound is the same santoor note as the Chai cards, and only plays with Sound on.

Rooms · Friday quiz night1 / 5
The pod lead taps Reveal for the whole room.

A five-question pack for pod leads

A pack a pod lead runs in a Friday room. Everyone answers in their head or in chat, then the lead reveals. Every answer links back to the card or explainer it came from, so the quiz doubles as revision of the week.

  • This pack revises this week's samples: Article 200, GI tags, Sendai and the Constitution timeline.
  • The reveal is one 300 ms change with a soft note, and nothing is scored publicly.
  • For marketing, the same pack becomes a 5-slide Instagram carousel or a Telegram poll with the answer next day.

Map Chai: tap the pins, then find it

A vector map card for a story with a place in it. Tapping a pin reveals a layer, then "Find it" asks you to tap the right spot, with no timer. This sample is a schematic of the Strait of Hormuz, drawn without any national boundaries.

  • Recognition, not recall: Prelims map questions reward knowing where things sit.
  • Wrong taps get a hint, never a cross or a score.
  • Share version: the silhouette alone, "Where is this?", with the answer in the app.

Number Sense: one number, what it means

One figure a Mains answer can use, drawn once in 400 ms, then explained in plain words with a comparison and its source. This sample is India's sex ratio in Census 2011.

  • Bars start at 800 and the tick marks 1,000 (parity), so small gaps are visible; the axis start is stated under the bars.
  • One comparison from a different source (NFHS-5) teaches why surveys and censuses differ.
  • Share version: the number and its one-line meaning, with no app chrome.

Forward Buster1 / 3

Myth vs Fact: the Forward Buster

A forwarded claim, the kind that circulates in aspirant WhatsApp groups. You guess first, then the stamp lands with the correct version and its source. The claims here are about the Constitution, so each can be checked against its text.

  • Three verdicts: Fact, Myth, Half-true. Half-true teaches the most.
  • The tone never mocks the person who forwarded it.
  • Built to be forwarded back into the same groups, as a correction card.

Two Sides · Mains GS20 / 6 opened

Two Sides: a Mains debate in six taps

Three points for and three against, each hidden until you tap it, so you try to recall before you read. It ends with a three-part outline you could write in Practice. The sample is simultaneous elections, a recurring GS2 theme.

  • Amber for one side and teal for the other keeps the split calm, with no red or green.
  • The outline feeds the Mains answer task in Today's 3.
  • Balanced on purpose: examiners reward both sides and a way forward.

Lamp Off: a two-minute wind-down

An optional screen after Close the Day. A diya dims slowly over two minutes, with one breath cue and, if Sound is on, a low tanpura. There is no content, no feed and nothing below the last line.

  • It is always dark, whatever the theme, because it is meant for 23:30.
  • One slow change, not a loop, so it stays within the motion rules.
  • Private. It never has a share button and is never used in marketing.

### Card 1. Presidential Reference: no deadlines, no deemed assent

**Date of event:** 20 November 2025
**Lens chips:** GS2 · Polity · Both · Articles 143, 200, 201

**EN headline:** Supreme Court: no court-set deadline for Governors on bills (9 words)

**EN what happened:** On 20 November 2025, a five-judge Constitution Bench led by CJI B.R. Gavai answered a Presidential Reference under Article 143. It advised that courts cannot fix time limits for the Governor or the President to act on state bills, and that "deemed assent" has no place in the Constitution. It also said a Governor cannot simply withhold assent and keep a bill; withholding must go with returning it to the Assembly. Where a Governor sits on a bill for a long time without reason, a court can still order a decision within a reasonable time, without judging the decision itself.

**EN static link:** Article 200 (Governor's options on a state bill), Article 201 (bills reserved for the President), Article 143 (advisory jurisdiction), Article 142; State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (8 Apr 2025), which had set timelines and used Article 142 to treat ten bills as assented.

**EN MCQ:** In its November 2025 opinion on the Presidential Reference, the Supreme Court said that:
- (0) Courts may fix a three-month limit for the Governor to decide on a bill
- (1) A bill pending with the Governor beyond a reasonable time is deemed to have received assent
- (2) In cases of prolonged and unexplained inaction, a court may direct the Governor to decide within a reasonable time
- (3) The opinion binds the President in the same way as a judgment binds the parties

**Answer:** 2
- Why not 0: the Court said fixing timelines would rewrite the Constitution; the three-month limits came from the April 2025 two-judge ruling.
- Why not 1: "deemed assent" was rejected as alien to the Constitution.
- Why not 3: an Article 143 opinion is advisory; the President is not bound to accept it.

**EN Mains angle:** "Does the November 2025 advisory opinion strike the right balance between gubernatorial discretion and the will of the state legislature?" Examine. (15 marks)

**HI headline:** सुप्रीम कोर्ट: विधेयकों पर राज्यपाल के लिए अदालती समय-सीमा नहीं

**HI क्या हुआ:** 20 नवंबर 2025 को CJI बी.आर. गवई की अध्यक्षता वाली पाँच जजों की संविधान पीठ ने अनुच्छेद 143 के तहत राष्ट्रपति के संदर्भ (Presidential Reference) पर अपनी राय दी। पीठ ने कहा कि अदालतें राज्य के विधेयकों पर फ़ैसले के लिए राज्यपाल या राष्ट्रपति पर कोई समय-सीमा तय नहीं कर सकतीं, और "deemed assent" (मानी गई स्वीकृति) की संविधान में कोई जगह नहीं है। पीठ ने यह भी कहा कि राज्यपाल स्वीकृति रोककर विधेयक अपने पास नहीं रख सकते; स्वीकृति रोकने पर विधेयक विधानसभा को लौटाना होगा। अगर राज्यपाल बिना कारण लंबे समय तक कोई फ़ैसला न लें, तो अदालत उन्हें उचित समय में निर्णय लेने का निर्देश दे सकती है, पर निर्णय के गुण-दोष पर टिप्पणी नहीं करेगी।

**HI स्थायी संदर्भ (static link):** अनुच्छेद 200 (राज्य विधेयक पर राज्यपाल के विकल्प), अनुच्छेद 201 (राष्ट्रपति के लिए आरक्षित विधेयक), अनुच्छेद 143 (सलाहकारी क्षेत्राधिकार), अनुच्छेद 142; तमिलनाडु राज्य बनाम तमिलनाडु के राज्यपाल (8 अप्रैल 2025), जिसमें समय-सीमा तय की गई थी और अनुच्छेद 142 के तहत दस विधेयकों को स्वीकृत माना गया था।

**HI MCQ:** नवंबर 2025 में Presidential Reference पर अपनी राय में सुप्रीम कोर्ट ने कहा कि:
- (0) अदालतें विधेयक पर फ़ैसले के लिए राज्यपाल पर तीन महीने की सीमा तय कर सकती हैं
- (1) उचित समय से अधिक राज्यपाल के पास लंबित विधेयक को स्वीकृत माना जाएगा
- (2) लंबी और बिना कारण की निष्क्रियता के मामलों में अदालत राज्यपाल को उचित समय में निर्णय लेने का निर्देश दे सकती है
- (3) यह राय राष्ट्रपति पर उसी तरह बाध्यकारी है जैसे कोई फ़ैसला पक्षकारों पर

**उत्तर:** 2
- 0 क्यों नहीं: कोर्ट ने कहा कि समय-सीमा तय करना संविधान को दोबारा लिखने जैसा होगा; तीन महीने की सीमा अप्रैल 2025 के दो जजों वाले फ़ैसले में थी।

## 3. Essay (English)

**Topic:** "A river is strong because many streams feed it."
(Style match: UPSC essay topics are often aphorisms, e.g. 2023–25 papers. "Cooperative federalism: myth or reality" is believed to be a 2016 essay topic: **UNVERIFIED**, check the paper before citing it as a PYQ.)

### Snap 1: skeleton (core argument)

- **Thesis:** Unity that draws strength from diversity lasts longer than unity imposed by uniformity; India's federal design is a bet on this.
- **Dimensions:** (1) Political: states, languages and local self-government as the "streams" that give the Union legitimacy. (2) Economic: fiscal federalism (Finance Commission, GST Council) as pooled strength with a fair share for each contributor. (3) Social and cultural: languages, faiths and regions; linguistic states in 1956 calmed, not broke, the country. (4) Ecological and personal: a river and a person both grow by taking in, not by shutting out.
- **Counter-view:** Too many streams can flood: secessionism, regional chauvinism, inter-state water disputes, fiscal indiscipline; sometimes a strong channel (the Centre) is needed.
- **Conclusion:** The answer is banks, not dams: shared institutions that guide many streams without stopping them.

### Snap 2: outline (paragraph plan)

1. **Intro:** Image of the Ganga at Devprayag, where the Alaknanda and Bhagirathi meet and only then take the name Ganga. Thesis line.
2. **Meaning:** Unity as a whole that needs its parts; contrast "union of states" (Art 1) with a melting pot. Ambedkar's "not the result of an agreement".
3. **Political stream:** 1956 linguistic reorganisation; fears of Balkanisation proved wrong. 73rd and 74th Amendments: a third tier.
4. **Economic stream:** GST Council as pooled sovereignty (Art 279A); Finance Commission keeping 41% for states (16th FC, 2026). A Union that takes in more than it returns dries its tributaries.
5. **Social and cultural stream:** Eighth Schedule languages; festivals shared across regions; the Sixth Schedule's room for tribal self-rule.
6. **Beyond the state:** an individual, an institution or a science grows by many inputs (e.g. Indian classical music absorbing Persian forms).
7. **Counter-view, fairly stated:** Khalistan movement of the 1980s; Cauvery dispute; states that borrow beyond means; why Art 356 and emergency powers exist.
8. **Reply:** The flood came where streams were blocked or ignored, not where they were welcomed (Punjab accord, Mizo accord 1986 as examples of absorption).
9. **Present tests:** delimitation after Census 2027; Governors and bills (2025 opinions); a fair test of whether every stream still feels it feeds the river.
10. **Way forward:** "Banks not dams": Inter-State Council that meets, consultation on Governors, cesses inside the shareable pool, language choice for learners.
11. **Conclusion:** Return to Devprayag. The river keeps its name only because the streams keep coming.
12. (Word budget: about 1,000–1,200 words; 7 hours of the paper split evenly across two essays.)

**Introduction (about 150 words):**
At Devprayag, two rivers meet. The Bhagirathi comes down from Gangotri; the Alaknanda comes from the snows near Badrinath. Neither gives up its water, and yet from that point on there is only one name: the Ganga. No one would call the Ganga weaker because it was made of two rivers; it is the Ganga because of them. Nations are rivers of this kind. They draw on many languages, regions, faiths and ways of earning a living. A nation that welcomes these streams grows wider and deeper. A nation that tries to make every stream look the same finds that its streams turn into floods, or dry up. India's Constitution made its choice in its first line: "a Union of States". This essay argues that unity built on diversity lasts longer than unity built on sameness. It also argues that the streams need firm banks, not dams.

**Body paragraph, economic dimension (about 150 words):**
Money shows most plainly whether the streams feed the river or the river drains the streams. Under the Constitution, the Centre collects the most buoyant taxes while the states spend on schools, hospitals and police. The Finance Commission under Article 280 sends the water back: the 16th Commission kept 41 per cent of the divisible pool with the states for 2026–31. GST went further. States gave up their own taxes and sat at one table, the GST Council, where neither the Centre nor the states can decide alone. That is pooled strength, not surrender. But the river can also take more than it gives. Cesses and surcharges, which are not shared, now form close to a fifth of gross central tax revenue. When a state feels it puts in more than it gets back, it stops feeling like a tributary. The lesson: a fair share keeps every stream flowing.

### What an evaluator watches for

1. **Does the essay stay with the metaphor and go beyond politics?** A strong essay uses federalism as one stream, not the whole river: it should touch society, culture, the economy and the individual. A GS2 answer in disguise scores average marks.
2. **Is the counter-view argued, not just mentioned?** One honest paragraph on when diversity becomes division (and a reasoned reply) separates a 120+ essay from a 90.
3. **Are examples accurate and varied?** Articles, numbers and cases are fine, but evaluators reward a mix: a historical event, a lived example, a constitutional point, a global comparison. One wrong number (e.g. "42% under the 16th FC") damages trust in the whole essay.

### Typical aspirant flaws at each snap

**Snap 1 (skeleton):**
- Thesis restates the topic ("Diversity is our strength") instead of taking a position that can be argued against.
- Dimensions are all political-administrative (Centre, states, Governor, GST), so the essay becomes a GS2 answer.

**Snap 2 (outline):**
- The counter-view is pushed into one line just before the conclusion, with no reply, so the essay reads one-sided.
- Examples are stacked in one paragraph (a list of Articles) while the social and personal paragraphs have none.

**Snap 3 (writing):**
- The introduction opens with a dictionary definition or a long quote by a leader, then takes 200 words to reach the thesis.
- Facts are approximate or outdated (e.g. "the 16th Finance Commission will decide…", or calling the 2025 Reference a "judgment"), and the conclusion ends with a slogan ("Unity in diversity is the need of the hour") instead of returning to the image and the argument.

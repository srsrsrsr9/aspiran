# Testing and spacing top the evidence ranking

Of the 26 central ideas behind exam-help apps, books, courses and methods, only two are proven in the full sense: **practising recall (testing yourself) and spacing review over time**. Both rest on several large meta-analyses, show clear gains in real classrooms, and beat the habits most students fall back on: rereading, highlighting and cramming. Fifteen more ideas are **well supported but conditional**: they work only in a certain form, for certain learners, or on certain tests. Examples are tutoring, structured group work, explicit teaching, feedback, worked examples and protecting sleep. Four ideas are **thin or mixed**, including growth-mindset and habit messaging, memory palaces, rankings and leaderboards, and studying only past papers. Two have **almost no research behind them**: letting AI write your study material, and study rooms or "body doubling". Three are **researched and found not to work, or to do harm**: the default habits (rereading, cramming, multitasking), the popular quick fixes (learning styles, brain training, speed reading, the Mozart effect, sleep learning), and letting an app or AI hand you the answers. One finding cuts across the whole ranking. The evidence is about *ideas*, not *products*. Almost no named app, book or course has been tested in a randomised trial on a real exam, and the few brand studies that exist are observational, measure engagement rather than learning, or come from the maker.

This is Part 4 of a four-part project. It covers all exams and all countries. It gives no personal advice.

## How we graded

Each idea got two separate marks, so that "well researched" and "works" are not confused. The first mark is the **amount and quality of research**, from 5 (several meta-analyses plus large classroom or field trials, replicated across ages and countries) down to 0 (nothing found). The second mark is the **effect on learning or exam results**: ++ (clear benefit, usually an effect size of 0.4 or more), + (modest or conditional), ± (mixed or unclear), 0 (good studies find no benefit), − (harmful, or clearly worse than what it replaces) and n/a (too little research to say).

The two marks combine into five bands. **A, proven**: research 4–5 and a clear benefit. **B, well supported with conditions**: research 3–5 and a benefit with important limits. **C, promising but thin or mixed**. **D, little or no research**. **E, researched and found not to work, or harmful**. Within a band, ideas are ordered by research amount, then effect size, then how close the evidence is to a real exam (a course exam or national test beats a lab word list).

The scale has one gap. It has no band for an idea with **a lot of research (4–5) but a mixed or near-zero effect**. We used one rule everywhere: **such items go in band C and are marked "researched, mixed"**, unless good studies show no effect (0) or harm (−), in which case they go in band E. Growth mindset, background music, highlighting and smart drugs land in C under this rule. Grit, self-affirmation, learning styles and brain training land in E.

The notes behind this report were written by researchers who hit a shared web-search limit partway through. Some figures came from summaries rather than the papers themselves; these are marked **(secondary)** below and should be treated as provisional. Several papers could not be checked at all; they are listed in the final section, not used as evidence.

## Everything is measured against rereading, highlighting and cramming

The fairest test of an exam method is not "better than nothing" but "better than what the student would otherwise do". Most students reread, highlight and cram, which Dunlosky and colleagues rated **low utility** ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). The gap between the two kinds of comparison is large. In real classrooms, quizzing beats doing nothing by **g = 0.61** but beats restudying by only **g = 0.33** ([Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)). Headline effect sizes that use "no activity" controls overstate what a student gains by switching.

Against that baseline, the top two ideas win clearly. **Retrieval beats rereading**: g = 0.33 in classrooms, g = 0.51 across practice-test studies (as quoted in Yang), and d = 0.40 when the final test asks new, applied questions ([Pan & Rickard 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29733621/); [Adesope et al. 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306)). The transfer gain is conditional: after bias correction it often fell to zero unless feedback, decent first-try success or elaborated answers were present. **Spacing beats cramming** markedly, with spaced recall beating massed recall by g = 0.74 across 39 comparisons ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)). It helped in over 80% of verbal-learning studies but only about a third of maths-skill studies ([Cepeda et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). Combining the two, successive relearning beat spaced restudy by about a letter grade on a real course exam ([Rawson & Dunlosky 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484)), and weekly relearning beat restudying in a large course ([Higham et al. 2022](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/449677/1/Higham_et_al._in_press_JEdPsych.pdf)).

**Making the material yourself beats reading it** (d = 0.40) ([Bertsch et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)), and building a concept map beats studying a given one (0.72 vs 0.43) ([Schroeder et al. 2018](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789)). Highlighting itself is the weakest of the defaults, not the worst: self-made highlights help memory a little (0.36) but not understanding ([highlighting meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1)). That is why it sits in C while rereading and cramming sit in E. They are not useless; they lose to the method they displace.

One limit matters. **Active methods do not clearly beat each other.** In classrooms, quizzing beat summarising, note-taking and concept mapping by only g = 0.095, not a significant difference ([Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)). The big win is moving from passive to active, not choosing the perfect active technique. For most B-band ideas (tutoring, feedback, self-regulation training, interleaving) the comparison group was ordinary teaching or blocked practice, not rereading, so their effect sizes cannot be read directly as "gain over rereading".

## Almost no product has been tested on a real exam

Every grade above belongs to an **idea**. None belongs to a brand. The meta-analyses cover research-built systems, teacher-run classrooms and lab tasks, not the apps and books that sell those ideas. We found **no randomised trial of any named spaced-repetition app, flashcard app, AI study tool, focus app or study-room service on a real high-stakes exam**. The brand studies that do exist share three weaknesses: they are observational, they measure use rather than learning, or the maker wrote them.

For **Anki and similar flashcard apps**, the best study links roughly 1 extra US medical-licensing (Step 1) point to about 1,700 flashcards among 72 students at one school, after controls. It is observational, so keen students may simply use more cards ([Deng et al. 2015](https://reference-global.com/article/10.1007/S40037-015-0220-X)). Later studies split: daily use correlated with Step 1 but not Step 2 in 165 students, and a retrospective cohort found no significant link (223.71 vs 222.58) (secondary) ([Wothe et al. 2023](https://ijms.pitt.edu/IJMS/article/view/1549); [PMC10403443](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10403443)). A survey linking Anki to higher scores was only seen second-hand ([PMC10176558](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10176558)).

For **Duolingo**, the company-authored half-life-regression paper measured how well it *predicts* forgetting and reported a 12% rise in daily engagement. It measured no learning or exam outcome ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf)). Its streak A/B tests, reported second-hand, raised daily users about 1% and day-14 retention about 3%. Again, that is engagement, not learning ([Econsultancy](https://econsultancy.com/six-a-b-tests-used-by-duolingo-to-tap-into-habit-forming-behaviour/)).

For **one sec**, a pre-registered PNAS study found the app's 10-second delay cut target-app openings by 57% over six weeks. One author makes the app and another is listed as its lead scientist, and the outcome was app use, not grades ([Grüning et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409); [one sec research page](https://one-sec.app/research/)).

For **AI tutors**, the trials are newer and more rigorous but small and short. A Harvard physics crossover trial (194 students) found a step-by-step AI tutor beat in-class active learning on a post-test in less time; it was one course, short-term ([Kestin et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6)). A UK study in five schools (N = 165) with Google's LearnLM and Eedi found AI-guided students did at least as well as human-tutored ones; it is vendor-involved ([arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633v1)). The strongest independent trial is a warning: in about 1,000 Turkish students, unrestricted GPT-4 cut later unaided exam scores while a hint-only version largely avoided the harm ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html)). A second trial points the same way over a longer delay: business students who studied with ChatGPT remembered less on a surprise test 45 days later ([Barcaui 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssaho.2025.102287)). In a 5,831-student online coding course, simply offering GPT-4 chat lowered exam participation ([Nie et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09975)). No trial of a *guided* LLM tutor has measured long-term retention, and none has used a real high-stakes exam.

Other branded methods carry allegiance risks. The large **Direct Instruction** meta-analysis comes from authors linked to the National Institute for Direct Instruction ([UO NIFDI working paper](https://pages.uoregon.edu/jeans/nifwp15_3.pdf)). **Peer Instruction**'s headline data come from its originator's own institution ([Crouch & Mazur 2001](https://mazur.harvard.edu/publications/peer-instruction-ten-years-experience-and-results)). The older **cooperative learning** studies come from the method's developers. Multimedia **medians from Mayer's own lab** run larger than an independent meta-analysis. A **body-doubling** survey comes from a provider ([neurodiversity.org](https://neurodiversity.org/?p=11449)). Vendor claims of very large coaching gains are not supported by independent studies of admissions-test prep ([Briggs 2001](https://nepc.colorado.edu/files/Briggs_Theeffectofadmissionstestpreparation.pdf)).

A further warning applies to any tool judged by its own quizzes. Intelligent tutoring systems scored about 0.73 on locally made tests but about 0.13 on standardised tests (secondary) ([Kulik & Fletcher 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654315581420)). Strategy training shows the same pattern: the biggest effects come from tests the researchers designed ([Donker et al. 2014 summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/10/13-1)).

## Replications, retractions and conflicts reshape several famous claims

Several well-known findings look weaker than their reputation. **"Handwriting beats laptops"** failed a direct replication with twice the original sample ([Urry et al. 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797620965541)). The **phone "brain drain"** effect failed a pre-registered direct replication and a partial replication of 302 people ([FORRT](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1086/691462/); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/revisiting-brain-drain-effect)); a later meta-analysis of 22 studies found only a small harm, clear for memory but not attention ([Böttger et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs13090751)). **Reframing exam nerves as helpful** raised first-year exam scores in one course ([Brady et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000219)) but showed no effect across 12 science courses at 7 institutions ([Thormodsæter et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.25-04-0055)), and its pooled effect shrinks after correcting for publication bias ([Bosshard & Gomez 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-58408-w)). **Self-affirmation** failed a well-powered replication, and a reanalysis showed a famous physics result depended on covariate adjustments ([Hanselman et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403146); [Serra-Garcia et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620923587)). The **"Google effect"** on memory did not replicate in a large project ([PeerJ 10325](https://peerj.com/articles/10325)). **Bloom's "two sigma"** tutoring gain was never replicated; the tutored group also had a stricter mastery bar, and one reanalysis puts tutoring's own share near 0.85 SD ([arXiv 2504.16132](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16132v1)). Pre-exam **expressive writing** has reported replication problems, but no specific direct replication could be confirmed ([Learning and the Brain](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/6662422)).

**Publication bias and study quality** were checked unevenly. The retrieval meta-analysis passed five bias tests with open data ([Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)), and self-explanation looked robust on fail-safe tests ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)). By contrast, memory-palace studies show publication bias and 89% high risk of bias ([Ondřej 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12514325/)); gamification metas did not check for bias (secondary); binaural beats were not checked; and the active-learning literature faces reporting-bias critiques ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/tackle-reporting-bias-pedagogy-research-paper-warns)). Brain-training far transfer disappeared once placebo and bias were controlled, though all three of those metas come from one group ([Sala & Gobet 2019](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/near-and-far-transfer-in-cognitive-training-a-second-order-meta-a/)).

**Financial conflicts** matter most for growth mindset. Authors with a financial incentive were **2.5 times as likely** to report positive effects, and higher-quality studies were less likely to show benefit ([Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/2023-90931-004.pdf)). Other researchers reanalysed the same data and found meaningful effects for at-risk students, which the original authors dispute ([PMC commentary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10495100/)). This debate is why growth mindset sits in C as "researched, mixed", not in B or E.

**One retraction must be stated plainly.** The Wang & Fan (2025) meta-analysis of 51 ChatGPT studies, which reported a large positive effect on learning, was **retracted on 22 April 2026** over discrepancies in its analysis ([GovTech](https://www.govtech.com/education/nature-retracts-oft-cited-paper-on-positive-impact-of-chatgpt)). It is not used as evidence here. Another ChatGPT meta-analysis (35 experiments) was seen only second-hand, and most of its studies used ChatGPT as a supplement rather than a structured tutor ([arXiv 2509.22725](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.22725v2)). There is **no independent meta-analysis of structured AI tutors** yet.

Finally, **who was studied** limits every band. Classroom retrieval studies of adults outside school number only six ([Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)), and 94% of classroom retrieval experiments were run in the United States or Western Europe ([Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9)); almost all tutoring trials are in school-age children ([Nickow et al. 2020](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476)); the interleaving classroom trial is US school maths ([Rohrer et al. 2020](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770)); and evidence from Asian and other high-stakes national exam systems is sparse throughout. Adults preparing alone for large competitive or licensing exams are the least studied group of all.

## Second check with other AI tools (November 2026)

After the first version, we asked three free AI tools (Perplexity, ChatGPT and DeepSeek) to find the original papers behind our open questions and our second-hand figures. We then checked every answer ourselves against the paper, its abstract or an index record. Only what we could confirm is used above. The full item-by-item check is kept with the research notes.

**How reliable the tools were.** None invented a paper. All three were right about whether a paper exists and usually right about headline numbers in an abstract. Their errors were in detail. ChatGPT was the most accurate: every paper and headline number we checked was right. Perplexity was close behind; it put one classroom study in the wrong journal and gave pooled figures for one meta-analysis that we could not find. DeepSeek was careful on health topics (if anything it under-claimed), but on our list of second-hand numbers it got one study count wrong and gave one figure we could not find anywhere. One tool reported, correctly, an effect size of 2.03 from a 2026 herbal-supplement study; the number is in the paper but is not believable, and we flag it rather than use it.

**Confirmed.** Retrieval-spacing figures (0.74 spaced vs massed; 0.034 for expanding vs even gaps); the generation effect (0.40); elaborative interrogation (0.56); growth-mindset results for lower achievers (+0.10 grade points); context-dependent memory (about 0.28); self-affirmation (0.15 for identity-threatened students, 0.01 for others); Hattie's 0.27 for test-taking; the EEF's +8 months for metacognition; the production effect (0.37).

**Corrected.** The self-regulation training figure of 0.61 comes from Dignath & Büttner's 2008 paper (84 studies), not a 74-study paper, and applies to academic performance at primary school (0.54 at secondary). The phone–grades correlation, which one tool could not find, is r ≈ −0.16 in the paper's abstract. The often-quoted 0.52 for mastery learning could not be traced to the 1990 paper. A meta-analysis said to support the phone "brain drain" effect finds only a small harm, mostly to memory.

**Changed.** No central idea changed band, so the headline counts stand. Among sub-items: pretesting, caffeine and anxiety reappraisal gained research weight (reappraisal is now "researched, mixed"); keyword mnemonics, cram schools and leaderboards got new figures; commitment devices and test-taking strategy are no longer provisional; blocker apps moved from D to C after a randomised trial found no effect on grades; and four new rows were graded (hydration, herbal nootropics, reading aloud, photo-solver apps).

## Conclusion

The ranking shows that the biggest gains come from **cheap, unglamorous changes in how material is practised**: pulling it from memory, spacing it out, explaining it, and getting corrective feedback. The most heavily marketed features of exam products sit lower down. Streaks, leaderboards, mindset messages, study-room memberships, AI-written notes and speed tricks are untested, mixed, or known to fail. The pattern is consistent: an idea climbs the ranking when it makes the learner do more mental work at the right time, and falls when it does the work for them or only makes study feel productive.

The second lesson concerns the gap between ideas and products. An app built on retrieval and spacing inherits a band-A idea, not band-A proof that the app works. The same idea can help or harm depending on design, as the AI-tutor trials show most sharply: a guided tutor helped while an answer-giving one lowered exam scores. Until products are tested in independent randomised trials on real exams, with adult candidates and long delays, the honest claim for any of them is "built on well-supported ideas". No product has earned "proven to raise your score".

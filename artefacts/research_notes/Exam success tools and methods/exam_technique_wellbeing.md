# Exam technique, exam anxiety, motivation and wellbeing tools: catalogue (as of Nov 2026)

Scope: test-taking strategy, exam-anxiety interventions, motivation/goal tools, sleep/exercise advice, parent/teacher resources and social-media "exam hacks". Global, with India called out. Research done 1 Nov 2026 with 15 web searches; no full-text papers were fetched, so effect sizes come from publisher summaries and secondary coverage. **Entries marked [U] are named with their official URL, but their price and claims were NOT checked this session.** The report writer should treat [U] details as unverified.

## For each: name, type, URL, price model, what it does, and the core idea it relies on

### Takeaway
There are 36 entries below. They rest on about eight core ideas: (1) offloading worry (expressive writing); (2) reappraising arousal as helpful; (3) affirming one's values to protect self-integrity; (4) CBT (spotting thoughts, gradual exposure, coping skills); (5) mindfulness and slow breathing; (6) metacognitive reflection after an exam (wrappers, error logs); (7) mental contrasting plus if-then plans (WOOP); (8) "train the brain or boost the chemistry" (brain games, nootropics, study drugs), where the claims are weakest and most often regulated. Most items with real research behind them are free, short and low-tech. The paid apps are mostly general wellbeing subscriptions with an exam-season marketing layer.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Brief psychological interventions (research-derived, free)
1. **Expressive writing before an exam (Ramirez & Beilock 2011)**. Type: 10-minute classroom intervention. URL: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110113141605.htm. Price: free. What it does: students write freely about their worries for about 10 minutes just before a test. Core idea: writing offloads worry that would otherwise use up working memory. Cited evidence: published in *Science* 331:211–213 (DOI 10.1126/science.1199427). The trial covered 106 ninth graders taking a biology final, plus a lab study with 47 college students doing maths under pressure. Highly anxious students had scored about 6% below calmer peers on midterms, and writing erased that gap. There was no benefit for low-anxiety students — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110113141605.htm); [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/write-stuff-test-anxiety). Critique: a letter from Lang and Lang in *Science* argued the exercise could harm low-anxiety students, and Beilock and Ramirez replied in the same issue. Sources disagree on the size of the effect ("5%" vs "one grade point") — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/write-stuff-test-anxiety).
2. **Anxiety or arousal reappraisal (Jamieson et al. 2010)**. Type: brief message intervention. URL: https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/4214916. Price: free. What it does: students are told before a test that a racing heart and nerves can help performance. Core idea: how you interpret stress arousal changes your physiology and your performance ("knots into bows"). Cited evidence: in *J. Exp. Soc. Psych.* 2010, the reappraisal group scored higher on GRE-maths practice. One to three months later the same group scored higher on the real GRE maths section — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/4214916); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/were-only-human/having-heart-can-we-rethink-lifes-stresses.html). Mixed later results: Hangen, Elliot and Jamieson (2018) found more challenge-like cardiovascular responses but no effect on maths performance. A community-college classroom study reported lower maths anxiety and higher scores — [Columbia EPIC summary](https://epic.tc.columbia.edu/research/academic-alert/blog-posts/2023/can-stress-actually-be-beneficial-to-achievement/).
3. **Values self-affirmation writing (Cohen et al. 2006/2009)**. Type: 15-minute writing exercises at key moments in the school year. URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403146. Price: free. What it does: students write about a value that matters to them before stressful evaluations. Core idea: affirming a core value buffers the threat to self-integrity, including stereotype threat. Cited evidence: the original trial raised African-American students' GPA by about 0.24 points over two years. Replications are mixed. Hanselman et al. (2017), well powered and run in the same setting, found no effect. A district scale-up (Borman et al. 2016) reported positive effects. A UK medical-school replication with 348 students did not support the expected mechanism. The EEF funded a trial in England with low-SES pupils — [search summary of Durham/Russell Sage/PMC sources](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403146); [Russell Sage](https://www.russellsage.org/node/4198); [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1236625).
4. **WOOP / MCII (Oettingen & Gollwitzer)**. Type: method plus a free app and website. URL: https://woopmylife.org. Price: free. What it does: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan; the user names an inner obstacle and writes an if-then plan for it. Core idea: mental contrasting (positive future vs present obstacle) combined with implementation intentions. Cited evidence: the vendor cites more than 20 years of motivation research. Reviewers call the app "bare-bones". The University of Cape Town translated it into all 11 South African official languages in 2022 — [UCT](https://www.news.uct.ac.za/features/teachingandlearning/-article/2022-03-31-woop-goal-realisation-app-now-in-all-11-official-languages); [Healthify NZ](https://healthify.nz/apps/w/woop-app).

#### B. Test-taking technique and metacognition
5. **Exam wrappers (Marsha Lovett, Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center)**. Type: teacher handout. URL: https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/examwrappers/ [URL is the standard Eberly page; not fetched]. Price: free templates for physics, biology, chemistry, maths and languages. What it does: after results come back, students answer short questions on how they prepared, what kinds of errors they made, and what they will change. Core idea: metacognitive self-regulation. The standard reference is Lovett's 2013 chapter "Make exams worth more than the grade". Evidence: effects are "highly variable" when students do little metacognitive practice — [UVM CTL](https://blog.uvm.edu/ctl/2016/11/07/knowing-thyself-gets-a-hand-from-exam-wrappers/); [Waterloo CTE](https://uwaterloo.ca/centre-for-teaching-excellence/node/4521); [Temple EDvice](https://sites.temple.edu/edvice/?p=83).
6. **Error log / mistake journal**. Type: self-practice. Price: free. What it does: the student records every wrong answer with its cause (knowledge gap, misreading, time, careless slip) and reviews the log before the next mock. Core idea: the same metacognition as exam wrappers, plus retrieval practice. [Gap: no primary source was found this session; widely used by coaching institutes and the GMAT/CAT community.]
7. **In-exam strategy guides (triage, read the paper first, answer what you know first, leave time to check, slow breathing)**. Type: guidance from schools, NHS trusts and UCAS. Price: free. Core idea: allocate time and lower arousal during the paper. Cited advice: answer the question you are most confident about first, leave time to check, and take long slow breaths if you panic — [Humber NHS tips PDF](https://www.humber.nhs.uk/media/qzzbqk1i/top-tips-for-coping-with-exam-stress.pdf); [UCAS](https://ucas.com/money-and-student-life/student-life/mental-health-and-wellbeing/managing-exam-stress). [Gap: answer elimination and negative-marking guessing strategy were not sourced this session.]
8. **UK DfE "Resources for managing test-related anxiety"**. Type: government guidance for teachers and parents. URL: https://gov.uk/government/publications/resources-for-managing-assessment-related-anxiety/resources-for-managing-test-related-anxiety. Price: free. Core idea: normalise and prepare (help children with primary assessments) — [GOV.UK](https://gov.uk/government/publications/resources-for-managing-assessment-related-anxiety/resources-for-managing-test-related-anxiety).

#### H. Social-media exam "hacks" and their regulatory record
31. **Lumosity (Lumos Labs)**. Type: subscription brain-training games. URL: https://www.lumosity.com. **Regulatory action:** in Jan 2016 Lumos Labs agreed with the US FTC to pay US$2M in consumer redress. A US$50M judgment was suspended because of the company's financial condition. The FTC alleged deceptive claims that the games improve performance at school, at work and in sport, and delay cognitive decline, along with deceptive testimonials. Subscribers on auto-renew from 2009 to 2014 had to be offered an easy cancellation. The CEO and co-founder were also named. Lumosity said the case concerned discontinued marketing language — [FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/06/lumosity-brain-training-app-maker-to-pay-2-million-settlement-to-ftc/amp/); [FTC statement PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/903353/160104lumositystatement.pdf).
32. **Elevate** [U] and **Peak** (London) [U]. Type: freemium brain-training apps. Core idea: transfer from game practice to general cognition. No ASA or FTC ruling against either was found this session. ### Inferences
- The best-evidenced exam-day tools (expressive writing, reappraisal, values affirmation, WOOP, exam wrappers) are free, take 5–15 minutes and are easy to put inside an app. Their effects are moderated by context and anxiety level, and several have failed to replicate (affirmation, reappraisal on performance). They suit gentle, optional nudges and should not be promised as score boosters.
- Expressive writing helped only high-anxiety students, and critics warn it could harm low-anxiety ones. An app should offer it, not push it.
- For India, Tele-MANAS (14416) is the default referral line. The NTA and CBSE now push it every exam season. MANODARPAN's status in 2026 needs checking.
- The commercial "hack" category (brain games, nootropics, study drugs) has the clearest regulatory record (FTC 2016, MHRA, SA ASA). In India its harm signal comes through news reports, not regulator action.
- Several popular focus and goal apps rely on streaks, timers and leaderboards, which conflict with Aspiran's content rules. WOOP-style planning without streaks fits those rules.

### Gaps
- Primary full texts were not fetched; effect sizes come from summaries.
- [U] entries (Calm, Mind, YoungMinds, Kooth, Smiling Mind, .b, Elevate, Peak, Forest, Habitica, affirmation apps, nootropic syrups) have no verified 2026 pricing or vendor evidence.
- Whether MindShift was actually shut down after March 2025 is not confirmed.
- The Wysa 2026 NHS RCT (Moon et al.) was not found in a primary source.
- No ASA or FTC actions found for Elevate or Peak; the ASA rulings database was not searched directly.
- No CDSCO, FSSAI or ASCI actions found on modafinil or exam "memory boosters" in India; the legal status of OTC modafinil sales is contested.
- Not researched: UPSC-specific coaching-institute mentorship and wellbeing programmes, Kota (Rajasthan) coaching-centre guidelines (2023–24), US resources (APA test-anxiety pages, Khan Academy growth mindset), Headspace for Educators, Sleepio or CBT-I for students, and the answer-elimination and negative-marking strategy literature.

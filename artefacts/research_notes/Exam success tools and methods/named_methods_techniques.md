# Named study methods, techniques and popular ideas for exam success (proven, unproven, myths)

Researcher notes, 1 Nov 2026. Scope: global. 78 entries below. Full validation is a later step.

**How to read the source tags.**
- A plain [Source](URL) means I fetched that page, or saw its abstract or summary, in this pass.
- **[ref, not fetched]** means a standard reference page (mostly Wikipedia) that I did not open in this pass. The origin and description come from general knowledge and need checking before anything is published.
- Evidence tags: **STRONG** (high utility in Dunlosky 2013 or backed by several meta-analyses), **MODERATE**, **MIXED/SMALL**, **WEAK/LOW**, **MYTH**, **UNTESTED** (a productivity or organisation system with little or no controlled research on exam outcomes).

## Q1. What are the named methods, their aliases, origins, descriptions, how they are practised, and which apps or books use them?

### Takeaway
More than 70 named methods are in common use. Only a few have strong evidence: retrieval practice or practice testing, spaced or distributed practice, and the techniques built on them (successive relearning, Leitner boxes and spaced-repetition software, past papers). A second group has moderate evidence: interleaving, elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, worked examples, dual coding, concrete examples, pretesting and generation. Most branded systems (Feynman, Cornell, SQ3R, blurting, 2-3-5-7, Pomodoro, GTD) package one or more of these principles, and few have been tested as named systems in their own right. Popular habits such as rereading, highlighting and summarising are rated low. Learning styles, the Mozart effect, speed reading and sleep learning are myths or close to it.

### Cited Findings

#### A. Evidence-based learning strategies (cognitive science)

**Anchor citation.** Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham (2013), "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58, DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266. It rated 10 techniques. **High utility:** practice testing and distributed practice. **Moderate:** interleaved practice, elaborative interrogation and self-explanation. **Low:** summarisation, highlighting or underlining, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, and rereading. Utility was judged by how far the benefits generalise across learning conditions, students, materials and criterion tasks. — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [Kent State](https://www.kent.edu/node/295736)

**Second anchor.** The Learning Scientists (Yana Weinstein, Megan Sumeracki, Megan Smith) promote "Six Strategies for Effective Learning": spaced practice, interleaving, elaboration, concrete examples, dual coding and retrieval practice. They give away free posters and bookmarks, and their book is *Understanding How We Learn* (2018). One review notes that the evidence for spaced and retrieval practice is more extensive than for the other four. — [WashU CTL](https://ctl.wustl.edu/learningcenter/?p=767); [UTS book review](https://educationexpress.uts.edu.au/blog/2020/02/03/book-review-understanding-how-we-learn/); [Pamoja](https://pamojaeducation.com/blog/six-strategies-effective-learning)

1. **Retrieval practice** (aliases: active recall, the testing effect, test-enhanced learning). **STRONG.** You pull information *out* of memory instead of putting it back in by rereading. Popularised by Roediger and Karpicke (2006) and the Learning Scientists. *How:* close the book, write or say everything you can, then check against the source and fix the gaps. Flashcards, quizzes and blank-page recall all count. *Tools:* Anki, Quizlet, RemNote, Brainscape. *Book:* Brown, Roediger and McDaniel, *Make It Stick* (2014). — [Learning Scientists summary via WashU](https://ctl.wustl.edu/learningcenter/?p=767); Dunlosky 2013 rates practice testing high ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html))
2. **Practice testing** (aliases: self-testing, low-stakes quizzing). **STRONG**, rated high in Dunlosky 2013. This is retrieval practice done as tests: past questions, end-of-chapter questions, mock tests. *How:* take untimed or timed quizzes often, then review your errors with feedback. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
3. **Spaced practice** (aliases: distributed practice, spacing effect, spaced repetition). **STRONG.** You spread study over time instead of massing it. Origins: Ebbinghaus in the 1880s (forgetting curve). C. A. Mace (1932, *Psychology of Study*) proposed reviews at growing intervals of about 1, 2, 4 and 8 days. Pimsleur published graduated-interval recall in 1967. *How:* schedule reviews at expanding or fixed gaps, or let software set them. *Research:* Spitzer (1939) with more than 3,600 Iowa students; Karpicke and Roediger (2007), where expanding gaps helped short-term retention and equal gaps were better long-term; meta-analyses by Latimier et al. (2021) and Kim and Webb (2022). *Tools:* SuperMemo (Woźniak's SM algorithms, SM-0 to SM-18), Anki (the FSRS scheduler arrived in 23.10), RemNote, Pimsleur courses. — [Wikipedia: Spaced repetition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaced_repetition); Dunlosky 2013 rates it high ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html))
4. **Successive relearning.** **STRONG** (from Rawson and Dunlosky's research programme). Retrieval practice until you get each item right, repeated across several spaced sessions. *How:* in each session, keep testing an item until you recall it correctly (often to one correct recall), then return to it days later. [ref, not fetched: Rawson & Dunlosky papers]
5. **Leitner system / Leitner box.** **STRONG principle (spaced retrieval); the box schedule itself is untested.** Devised by Sebastian Leitner in 1973. *How:* flashcards sit in numbered boxes. A correct answer moves a card up one box, which is reviewed less often. A wrong answer sends it back to box 1. In the original design, the box sizes (1, 2, 5, 8 and 14 cm) set the schedule, and a box was reviewed when it was full. — [Wikipedia: Spaced repetition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaced_repetition)
6. **Spaced-repetition software (SRS).** Software that applies spacing and retrieval at scale: SuperMemo (Piotr Woźniak), Anki, FSRS (Junyao Ye; used in Anki and RemNote). — [Wikipedia: Spaced repetition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaced_repetition)
7. **Interleaving** (alias: interleaved or mixed practice). **MODERATE** in Dunlosky 2013. You mix problem types or topics within a session instead of blocking them (AAA BBB CCC becomes ABC BCA). This builds the ability to recognise which method a problem needs. Key researchers: Rohrer and Taylor. *How:* mix question sets from different chapters, or rotate subjects within a session. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [WashU](https://ctl.wustl.edu/learningcenter/?p=767)
8. **Elaborative interrogation.** **MODERATE** in Dunlosky 2013. You ask "why is this true?" and "how does it work?" of each fact and answer from what you already know. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
9. **Elaboration** (Learning Scientists' broader term). You generate extra detail and connections, linking ideas to each other and to experience. — [WashU](https://ctl.wustl.edu/learningcenter/?p=767)
10. **Self-explanation.** **MODERATE** in Dunlosky 2013. Building on Chi et al.'s work in 1989 and after, you explain each step of a text or solved problem to yourself: what it means and how it connects to what came before. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
11. **Concrete examples.** One of the Learning Scientists' six. You tie an abstract idea to several varied, specific examples and spot what they share. — [WashU](https://ctl.wustl.edu/learningcenter/?p=767)
12. **Dual coding.** One of the Learning Scientists' six, from Paivio's dual-coding theory. You combine words with visuals (diagrams, timelines, flowcharts) and explain each in terms of the other. Note that "imagery for text" was rated low in Dunlosky 2013, so the visuals need to carry meaning. — [WashU](https://ctl.wustl.edu/learningcenter/?p=767); [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
13. **Worked examples** (alias: the worked-example effect, from cognitive load theory, Sweller). Novices learn faster from studying fully solved problems than from solving problems alone. Support fades as expertise grows (the "expertise reversal" effect). *How:* study a worked example, then try a similar problem; alternate the two. [ref, not fetched: Sweller, cognitive load theory]
14. **Pretesting** (aliases: the pretesting effect, errorful generation, test-potentiated learning). **MODERATE.** You attempt questions *before* studying the material, even when you get them wrong. Richland, Kornell and Kao (2009, *J. Exp. Psych.: Applied*) found the benefit held even for items answered wrongly on the pretest, using a prose passage. Kornell, Hays and Bjork (2009) used word pairs. The benefit depends on corrective feedback. — [Richland et al. PDF](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-pluses-of-getting-it-wrong/); [Journal of Cognition](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/455); [Springer 2021 on terminology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-021-01218-6)
15. **Generation effect.** Information you generate yourself (fill in a blank, complete a word, produce an answer) is remembered better than information you only read. Slamecka and Graf (1978). [ref, not fetched]
16. **Desirable difficulties.** Robert and Elizabeth Bjork's umbrella idea: spacing, interleaving, testing and generation feel harder and slower but produce better long-term learning. [ref, not fetched]
17. **Keyword mnemonic.** **LOW** in Dunlosky 2013. You link a new word to a similar-sounding familiar word plus a vivid image. Widely used for vocabulary. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
18. **Imagery for text.** **LOW** in Dunlosky 2013. You form mental images while reading. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)

#### B. Named study, reading and note-taking systems

19. **Feynman technique.** **UNTESTED as a named system**, though it combines retrieval, self-explanation and the protégé effect. It is attributed to Richard Feynman's habits, but Feynman never set it out as steps. The name first appears in a September 2011 post by Scott H. Young, and a Farnam Street version from April 2012 was widely copied. *How:* (1) choose a concept; (2) explain it in plain words as if to a child or beginner; (3) find where you get stuck, go back to the source and fill the gap; (4) simplify and use analogies. — [learning.subwiki](https://learning.subwiki.org/wiki/Feynman_technique); [K-State handout](https://www.k-state.edu/aac/success-tools/collection/Feynman_Technique.pdf); [USC handout](https://dornsife.usc.edu/sergey-lototsky/wp-content/uploads/sites/211/2023/06/StudyLikeFeynman.pdf)
20. **Blurting** (alias: brain dump). Popular in UK GCSE and A-level revision culture. A blank-page version of retrieval practice. *How:* read a topic, close the book, write everything you remember on blank paper, then check and fill the gaps in another colour. Sources link it to the 2-3-5-7 schedule. Its evidence comes from retrieval practice; there are no studies of "blurting" by name. — [Save My Exams](https://savemyexams.com/learning-hub/revision-tips/create-a-spaced-repetition-schedule-to-boost-memory) (context); origin not found.
21. **Blank-page method / active-recall "blank page."** The same idea as blurting, often done as a mind map from memory. Same evidence status as blurting.
22. **2-3-5-7 revision method** (alias: 2/3/5:7). **UNTESTED schedule; the spacing principle behind it is STRONG.** A spaced schedule used two ways: (a) counting back from the exam, or (b) reviewing on days 2, 3, 5 and 7 after first learning a topic. No inventor, publication or study is found; it appears mostly in UK school guidance. — [Save My Exams](https://savemyexams.com/learning-hub/revision-tips/create-a-spaced-repetition-schedule-to-boost-memory); [Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/acrobat/resources/spaced-repetition.html); [Stanley High PDF](https://stanleyhigh.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Revision-Timetables.pdf); [BCU](https://bcu.ac.uk/exams-and-revision/best-ways-to-revise/spaced-repetition)
23. **SQ3R** (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review). From Francis P. Robinson, an Ohio State educational psychologist, in *Effective Study*. Sources date it to 1941 or 1946. *How:* skim the headings; turn them into questions; read to answer them; recite the answers from memory; review. **Some evidence**: the "Recite" step is retrieval practice. — [Wikipedia: SQ3R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQ3R)
24. **SQ4R / PQ4R** (Preview, Question, Read, Reflect, Recite, Review). PQ4R is usually credited to Thomas and Robinson (1972). It adds a "Reflect" (elaboration) step. [ref, not fetched]
25. **PQRST** (Preview, Question, Read, Summarise or Self-recitation, Test). A variant of the same family, attributed to Staton (1982) and Spache and Berg. [ref, not fetched; attribution uncertain]
26. **Cornell note-taking system.** Created by Walter Pauk at Cornell University in the 1950s, set out in *How to Study in College*. *How:* divide the page into a narrow cue column, a wide notes column and a summary strip at the bottom. Take notes during the lecture, write questions or cues afterwards, cover the notes and answer the cues (retrieval), then summarise. [ref, not fetched: Wikipedia "Cornell Notes"; origin not confirmed in this pass]
27. **Outline method.** Hierarchical indented notes (headings, subpoints, details). A long-standing method found in university learning-centre handouts. [ref, not fetched]
28. **Charting method.** Notes in a table, with columns for categories such as cause, effect and date. Suits comparisons. [ref, not fetched]
29. **Boxing method.** Each topic is grouped inside its own drawn box on the page, often on a tablet. A recent social-media and university-handout idea. [ref, not fetched]
30. **Sentence method / flow notes / mapping method.** Other note-taking formats taught by learning centres. [ref, not fetched]
31. **Mind mapping.** Popularised by Tony Buzan (BBC *Use Your Head*, 1974). A radial diagram that branches out from a central topic using keywords, colour and images. *Tools:* XMind, MindMeister, iMindMap. Evidence: MIXED/SMALL; it helps most when built from memory, which makes it retrieval practice. [ref, not fetched]
32. **Concept mapping.** Joseph Novak (Cornell, 1970s). Nodes joined by *labelled* links ("causes", "is part of"). [ref, not fetched]
33. **Zettelkasten** (slip box). Associated with sociologist Niklas Luhmann, who built a collection of about 90,000 linked index cards. Popularised by Sönke Ahrens, *How to Take Smart Notes* (2017). *How:* write atomic notes in your own words, give each an ID, link them to related notes, and build arguments from the links. *Tools:* Obsidian, Zettlr, Logseq, Roam. **UNTESTED** for exams; better suited to long-form research and writing. [ref, not fetched]
34. **Summarisation.** **LOW** in Dunlosky 2013. You write summaries of what you read. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
35. **Past-paper / PYQ method** (previous-year questions; Indian exams such as UPSC, JEE and NEET). **STRONG principle** (practice testing plus learning the exam's format). *How:* solve past papers under exam conditions; map the syllabus by how often topics appear; analyse your mistakes; revisit later with spacing. Its value as a separate "method" is not studied on its own; the evidence is from practice testing. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html) (practice testing)
36. **Mock tests / full-length simulations.** Practice testing plus rehearsal of exam stamina and time management. The same evidence status as above.
37. **Error log / mistake notebook.** You keep a record of every wrong answer, why it was wrong and the correct principle, and re-test those items. Widely used in JEE, UPSC and GMAT coaching. It rests on retrieval with feedback; no named-system studies found.

#### C. Memory techniques (mnemonics)

38. **Method of loci / memory palace.** Traditionally credited to Simonides of Ceos (classical legend); described in *Rhetorica ad Herennium* and by Cicero. *How:* pick a familiar route, place a vivid image for each item at each spot along it, then walk the route in your mind to recall. Used by memory champions. Popular books: Joshua Foer, *Moonwalking with Einstein* (2011). Good evidence for ordered lists; it takes training. [ref, not fetched]
39. **Major system** (phonetic number system). Developed from Aimé Paris (early 1800s) and Gregor von Feinaigle. Each digit maps to a consonant sound (1 = t/d, 2 = n, 3 = m ...). You turn numbers into words, then into images. Good for dates and numbers. [ref, not fetched]
40. **Peg system** (rhyming pegs, such as one-bun and two-shoe). You attach each item to a pre-learned peg image. [ref, not fetched]
41. **Chunking.** George Miller (1956), "The magical number seven, plus or minus two". Later work by Cowan suggests the limit is nearer 4 chunks. You group items into meaningful units (phone numbers, acronyms). [ref, not fetched]
42. **Acronyms and acrostics** (first-letter mnemonics such as ROYGBIV and "My Very Educated Mother ..."). Used everywhere; useful for order and lists. [ref, not fetched]
43. **Story or link method.** You join the items to remember into one vivid narrative. [ref, not fetched]

#### D. Time, planning and productivity systems

44. **Pomodoro technique.** Francesco Cirillo, late 1980s, named after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer; later a book, *The Pomodoro Technique*. *How:* 25 minutes of focused work, a 5-minute break, and a longer break after four rounds. *Tools:* Forest, Focus To-Do, Pomofocus, Toggl. **UNTESTED for exam outcomes** (a few small studies on focus and fatigue). [ref, not fetched; origin not confirmed in this pass]. *Note for Aspiran:* a visible countdown may conflict with the "no timers" content rule.
45. **Timeboxing / time blocking.** You give each task a fixed calendar slot (Cal Newport, *Deep Work*, popularised time blocking). **UNTESTED.** [ref, not fetched]
46. **Eisenhower matrix** (urgent/important matrix). Attributed to a 1954 Eisenhower quote; popularised by Stephen Covey, *The 7 Habits* (1989), as "Quadrant II". Four quadrants: do, schedule, delegate, delete. **UNTESTED.** [ref, not fetched]
47. **Ivy Lee method.** A productivity story said to date from around 1918, when consultant Ivy Lee advised Charles Schwab of Bethlehem Steel. *How:* at the end of each day, list the 6 most important tasks for tomorrow in priority order; work through them one at a time; carry over what is left. The story is anecdotal. **UNTESTED.** [ref, not fetched]
48. **Getting Things Done (GTD).** David Allen, 2001 book. Capture, clarify, organise, reflect (weekly review), engage. *Tools:* Todoist, OmniFocus, Things. **UNTESTED** for exams. [ref, not fetched]
49. **80/20 rule / Pareto principle.** From Vilfredo Pareto's observation about land ownership; generalised by Joseph Juran. In exam culture: focus on the 20% of topics that bring 80% of the marks, often worked out from PYQ frequency. A heuristic, not an evidence-based learning method. [ref, not fetched]
50. **Eat the frog.** Brian Tracy (2001), from a quote attributed to Mark Twain: do the hardest task first. **UNTESTED.** [ref, not fetched]
51. **Deep work / distraction blocking.** Cal Newport (2016). Long, single-task sessions with phones and sites blocked. Related evidence: multitasking costs (see 75). [ref, not fetched]
52. **Study timetable / backward planning.** You work back from the exam date to allocate syllabus coverage and revision rounds (common in UPSC and JEE culture). **UNTESTED** as a named method.

#### E. Social and mastery approaches

53. **Teach-back / protégé effect / learning by teaching.** Studying in order to teach, or actually teaching, improves learning (Nestojko et al. 2014; Fiorella and Mayer). Related to the "Feynman technique". [ref, not fetched]
54. **Study groups / peer instruction.** Peer instruction is Eric Mazur's method (Harvard, 1990s): a concept question, a vote, discussion with peers, then a revote. Unstructured study groups are MIXED and depend on structure (quizzing each other works better than chatting). [ref, not fetched]
55. **Reciprocal teaching.** Palincsar and Brown (1984): predict, question, clarify, summarise, taking turns in small groups. [ref, not fetched]
56. **Mastery learning.** Benjamin Bloom (1968); also Bloom's "2 sigma problem" (1984), which compared one-to-one tutoring with mastery learning. Learners must reach a set standard on each unit before moving on, with corrective loops. Used by Khan Academy. Evidence: MODERATE to strong in education meta-analyses. [ref, not fetched]
57. **Deliberate practice.** Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993). Effortful practice aimed at specific weaknesses, with feedback and expert-designed tasks. Popularised as the "10,000-hour rule" (Gladwell, 2008), a simplification Ericsson rejected. A 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara et al. found deliberate practice explained only a modest share of performance differences. [ref, not fetched]

#### F. Physiological, psychological and lifestyle factors

58. **Sleep (before and after study).** Sleep consolidates memories; all-nighters harm recall. **STRONG** in general. [ref, not fetched; broad literature]
59. **Naps.** Short daytime naps (about 10 to 90 minutes) can help memory consolidation. MODERATE. [ref, not fetched]
60. **Targeted memory reactivation (TMR) during sleep.** A cue (a sound or smell) present during learning is replayed during sleep. A meta-analysis by Hu et al. (2020, *Psychological Bulletin*) covered 91 experiments and 212 effect sizes, and found a small-to-moderate benefit across declarative memory and skill learning. This re-cues memories learned while awake; it is not learning new material while asleep. — [Hu et al. 2020 PDF](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/paller/PsychBulletin2020.pdf); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680)
61. **Exercise** (single bouts, and fitness over time). Aerobic exercise is linked to better executive function and memory. MODERATE. [ref, not fetched]
62. **Nutrition / breakfast.** MIXED. Evidence on breakfast and test scores varies by study. [ref, not fetched]
63. **Caffeine.** Improves alertness and reaction time at moderate doses; the effect on memory itself is mixed; it can disturb sleep. [ref, not fetched]
64. **Hydration.** Mild dehydration is linked to worse attention and mood. Small evidence, often confounded. [ref, not fetched]
65. **Mindfulness / meditation.** Small effects on attention and stress; small evidence on test anxiety. MIXED/SMALL. [ref, not fetched]
66. **Expressive writing before exams.** Ramirez and Beilock (2011), *Science* 331: 211–213. Ten minutes writing about your worries right before a test. In a high-school field study (106 ninth-grade biology students), it erased the roughly 6% score gap anxious students had shown on earlier midterms. There was no gain for students with low anxiety. Lang and Lang (2011) disputed it, and the authors replied; durability is unclear. MIXED/promising. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110113141605.htm); [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/write-stuff-test-anxiety)
67. **Reappraising anxiety / arousal reappraisal** ("get excited", "stress is fuel"). Jamieson, Mendes, Blackstock and Schmader (2010), *JESP* 46(1): 208–212. Students told that arousal helps performance scored higher on GRE-math practice and on the real GRE 1 to 3 months later. Follow-ups are mixed: a 2022 community-college study was positive; a maths-competition study found no effect on performance. MIXED. Related: Alison Wood Brooks (2014), "get excited". — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/4214916); [Columbia EPIC](https://epic.tc.columbia.edu/research/academic-alert/blog-posts/2023/can-stress-actually-be-beneficial-to-achievement/); [Fairfield / Hangen et al.](https://fairfield.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/stress-reappraisal-during-a-mathematics-competition-testing-effec/)
68. **Growth mindset.** Carol Dweck, *Mindset* (2006). The meta-analysis by Sisk et al. (2018, *Psychological Science*) found weak effects for both mindset–achievement correlations (k = 273, N ≈ 366k) and interventions (k = 43, N ≈ 57k), with signs of publication bias. Yeager et al. (2019, *Nature*) ran the National Study of Learning Mindsets with 12,490 ninth-graders in 65 schools and found about +0.10 GPA points, concentrated in lower achievers and supportive peer contexts. Researchers disagree on how to read the two together. SMALL/contested. — [Sisk et al. PDF](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf); [Penn State listing, Yeager 2019](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-national-experiment-reveals-where-a-growth-mindset-improves-ach/); [EdWeek](https://edweek.org/ew/articles/2019/08/07/national-study-shows-how-a-simple-growth.html)
69. **Self-affirmation / values affirmation.** Cohen et al. (2006, *Science*): a brief writing exercise about your personal values reduced racial achievement gaps. Replications are mixed. [ref, not fetched]
70. **Music while studying.** Music with lyrics generally hurts reading comprehension; effects of instrumental music depend on the person. MIXED. [ref, not fetched]
71. **Background noise / white, pink or brown noise.** MIXED; some evidence suggests a benefit for people with ADHD traits. [ref, not fetched]
72. **Binaural beats.** The meta-analysis by Garcia-Argibay, Santed and Reales (2019, *Psychological Research* 83: 357–372) pooled 22 studies and 35 effect sizes and found an overall g = 0.45 across memory, attention, anxiety and pain. The effect depended on frequency, exposure time and timing (exposure before the task did better). Later reviews (2023) report conflicting results for theta and beta on memory. MIXED; the studies are small and varied. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30073406/); [Springer 2021 study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00221-021-06132-6)
73. **Nootropics / "smart drugs"** (modafinil, methylphenidate used off-label, racetams, L-theanine plus caffeine). Healthy users see small effects at best, and there are health and legal risks. [ref, not fetched]. *Note for Aspiran:* do not recommend these.
74. **Chewing gum.** Onyper et al. (2011): about 5 minutes of chewing *before* testing gave short benefits (15 to 20 minutes) to working and episodic memory; there was no benefit from chewing during the test. A 2019 meta-analysis (*Physiology & Behavior*) found a weak but significant effect on sustained attention. Some studies found nothing. SMALL/MIXED. — [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/17520-chewing-gum-test-performance.html); [PubMed meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30611764/); [Learning and the Brain](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/does-chewing-gum-improve-memory-and-learning/)
75. **Context-dependent memory** (study where you will be tested; "encoding specificity", Tulving). Godden and Baddeley (1975): divers recalled words better in the same setting where they learned them (underwater or on land). Reported figures vary by source. A 1980 follow-up found no effect on *recognition*. Smith and Vela's (2001) meta-analysis found a small effect (d ≈ 0.28). Practical tip: vary where you study, or mentally rebuild the study setting during the exam. SMALL. Related: **state-dependent memory**. — [Wikipedia: Context-dependent memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context-dependent_memory); [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/context-and-state-dependent-memory.html)
76. **Handwriting vs typing notes.** Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014, *Psychological Science* 25: 1159–1168): longhand beat laptop on conceptual questions. A direct replication by Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019, *Educational Psychology Review*) found smaller, mostly non-significant differences. Sources disagree on whether the factual-recall advantage held; FLoRA lists the outcome as "mixed". MIXED. — [FLoRA atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1177/0956797614524581/); [Learning Scientists blog](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/2/21-1); [Morehead DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09468-2)

#### G. Myths and weak ideas

77. **Learning styles (VARK, VAK, Honey and Mumford, Kolb).** VARK comes from Neil Fleming (1987). **MYTH** (the meshing hypothesis). Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008, *PSPI*) found almost no studies that used the right design (randomly assigning students to matched or mismatched teaching). Those that did contradicted meshing. Aslaksen and Lorås (2018) found very low, non-significant effects. — [USF repository](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01538/full); [Deans for Impact](https://deansforimpact.org/learning-styles-what-does-the-research-say/)
78. **Rereading.** **LOW** in Dunlosky 2013. It creates an "illusion of fluency". — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
79. **Highlighting / underlining.** **LOW** in Dunlosky 2013. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
80. **Cramming / massed practice / all-nighters.** The opposite of spacing. It can work for the next day but is forgotten fast, and the sleep loss hurts recall. **WEAK.** — inferred from spacing evidence ([Wikipedia: Spaced repetition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaced_repetition)).
81. **Multitasking while studying.** Task-switching costs are well documented, and media multitasking is linked to lower grades. **WEAK/harmful.** [ref, not fetched]
82. **Brain Gym.** Paul and Gail Dennison (1980s). Movements claimed to "integrate the hemispheres". **MYTH**; no credible evidence. [ref, not fetched]
83. **"We use only 10% of our brain."** **MYTH.** [ref, not fetched]
84. **Left-brain / right-brain learners.** **MYTH.** Lateralisation exists but "dominance" types do not (Nielsen et al. 2013 brain-imaging study). [ref, not fetched]
85. **Speed reading** (RSVP apps such as Spritz; "eliminate subvocalisation"). **MYTH / trade-off.** Rayner et al. (2016, *PSPI* 17(1): 4–34): there is a speed-accuracy trade-off; readers are unlikely to double or triple their speed while keeping comprehension; eye movements take no more than about 10% of reading time; skimming can work for gist; practice gives modest gains. — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/speed-reading-promises-are-too-good-to-be-true-scientists-find.html); [APS publication page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/speed_reading.html)
86. **Subliminal learning** (hidden audio messages). **MYTH** for learning content. [ref, not fetched]
87. **Sleep learning / hypnopaedia** (playing new material during sleep to learn it). **MYTH** for acquiring new knowledge. Do not confuse it with TMR (item 60), which only re-cues material learned while awake. — [Hu et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680) (scope note)
88. **Mozart effect.** Rauscher, Shaw and Ky (1993, *Nature* 365: 611) reported a short-lived gain on spatial tasks. The meta-analysis by Pietschnig, Voracek and Formann (2010, *Intelligence* 38: 314–323; about 39 studies, more than 3,000 participants) found no specific Mozart effect, signs of publication bias, and similar effects from other music. **MYTH.** — [Univ. Vienna press release](https://medienportal.univie.ac.at/media/aktuelle-pressemeldungen/detailansicht/artikel/mozarts-music-does-not-make-you-smarter); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/05/100510075415.htm); [Wikipedia: Mozart effect](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Mozart_effect)
89. **Photographic (eidetic) memory training.** **MYTH.** There is no reliable evidence that adults have true photographic memory, or that it can be trained. Memory champions use mnemonics (items 38 to 40). [ref, not fetched]
90. **"10,000-hour rule"** (as a guarantee). A popular simplification of Ericsson's work (see 57). **WEAK as stated.** [ref, not fetched]
91. **Brain-training games for exam performance** (Lumosity and similar). In 2016 the US FTC fined Lumosity over its claims; transfer to untrained tasks is weak. [ref, not fetched]

### Inferences
- The branded systems are mostly wrappers around two strong ideas, retrieval and spacing. The strongest are blurting or blank-page recall, the Feynman technique, Cornell's cue-column recall, SQ3R's Recite step, Leitner boxes, 2-3-5-7 and PYQs. For a product such as Aspiran, it may be clearer to teach the two principles and present the named systems as "flavours" of them, instead of treating each as a separate proven method.
- Many popular productivity systems (Pomodoro, Eisenhower, Ivy Lee, GTD, 80/20, eat the frog) have essentially no controlled evidence on exam results. They should be presented as organisational habits, not learning science.
- Several lifestyle findings (expressive writing, reappraisal, growth mindset, gum, binaural beats, handwriting) rest on small or contested studies. They should carry "mixed evidence" labels.
- Some popular methods clash with Aspiran's content rules: Pomodoro countdown timers and "streak" spaced-repetition apps conflict with the "no timers, streaks or public scores" rule. Retrieval and spacing can be offered without either.
- Indian exam culture (UPSC, JEE, NEET) adds methods that are rarely covered in Western literature: PYQ analysis, error logs, multiple revision rounds, answer-writing practice. They map onto practice testing and spacing.

### Gaps
- I did not open the original Dunlosky 2013 paper, so the effect details for each technique come from press summaries only.
- The following origins are from general knowledge (marked [ref, not fetched]) and need checking: Cornell notes (Pauk), Pomodoro (Cirillo, late 1980s), PQ4R (Thomas & Robinson 1972), PQRST (attribution uncertain), Zettelkasten (Luhmann), mind mapping (Buzan), concept mapping (Novak), the major system (Paris/Feinaigle), method of loci (Simonides), Ivy Lee (around 1918, anecdotal), Eisenhower matrix (Covey), GTD (Allen 2001), mastery learning (Bloom 1968), deliberate practice (Ericsson 1993), peer instruction (Mazur), reciprocal teaching (Palincsar & Brown 1984), generation effect (Slamecka & Graf 1978), the Lumosity FTC case (2016).
- The origins of the 2-3-5-7 method and blurting were not found; they seem to be folk methods from UK revision culture.
- No sources were gathered in this pass for sleep, naps, exercise, nutrition, caffeine, hydration, mindfulness, music, noise, nootropics, self-affirmation or multitasking. These need their own validation pass.
- Figures for Godden & Baddeley and Smith & Vela vary across secondary sources; check the original papers.
- Named systems such as the boxing, charting and sentence methods are mostly described in university handouts with no research behind them.

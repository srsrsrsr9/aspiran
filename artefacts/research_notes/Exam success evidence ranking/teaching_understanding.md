# Part 4 evidence notes: teaching, tutoring, understanding, worked examples, notes (TCH, TUT, ELA, EXM, NOT)

Scope: the five central ideas TCH, TUT, ELA, EXM and NOT, as defined in `Exam success central ideas/taxonomy_v1.md`.
Graded with `grading_scale.md` (research amount 0–5, effect direction, band A–E). About 27 web searches, done 1 Nov 2026.
Where a number comes from a secondary summary rather than the paper itself, it is marked "(secondary)".
Numbers checked against the paper's own text are marked "(checked in full text)".

## TCH: Expert teaching and curated content (explicit / direct instruction, lectures vs active learning, video, star teachers, cram schools)

### Takeaway
Teaching that is explicit, structured and well sequenced has strong evidence: the Direct Instruction curricula meta-analysis (328 studies) finds moderate-to-large gains, and explicit teaching beats unguided discovery. But a *pure* lecture is beaten by lectures with active learning (Freeman 2014: +0.47 SD, failure rates 34% to 21%), and video helps most when it is *added* to teaching (g = 0.80) rather than replacing it (g = 0.28). The "star teacher / cram school / few standard books" part is far less clear: teacher quality matters a lot (Chetty), but paid shadow education gives mixed results.

### Cited Findings
**Explicit / Direct Instruction**
- Stockard, Wood, Coughlin & Khoury (2018), *Review of Educational Research* 88(4), 479–507: meta-analysis of Direct Instruction (DI) curricula, 1966–2016; 328 studies, 413 designs, almost 4,000 effects. All estimated effects positive and statistically significant (except affective outcomes in meta-regressions); described as "moderate to large" by traditional benchmarks; publication, method and sample features not systematically related to effect size; little decline during maintenance; larger effects with more exposure — [Stockard et al. 2018, DOI 10.3102/0034654317751919](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654317751919); [UO ScholarsBank record](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/9c5db747-52be-4bd8-8771-b05d0f7c0b01/full). Exact pooled d not retrieved (PDF host rejected the fetch).
- Note on conflict of interest: Stockard and co-authors are linked to the National Institute for Direct Instruction (UO working-paper series hosts a version) — [UO NIFDI working paper](https://pages.uoregon.edu/jeans/nifwp15_3.pdf). Weigh accordingly; this is the brand "DI" (Engelmann programmes), not all explicit teaching.
- Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich & Tenenbaum (2011), *Journal of Educational Psychology*: two meta-analyses, 164 studies. Unassisted discovery vs explicit instruction: 580 comparisons, d = −0.38 (favouring explicit teaching). Enhanced/guided discovery vs other conditions: d = 0.30 — [Alfieri et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf); [Texas LD Center summary](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=364). Critics note the "enhanced discovery" category mixes very different comparison conditions (opinion pieces, same search).

**Lecture vs active learning**
- Freeman et al. (2014), *PNAS* 111(23), 8410–8415: 225 studies of undergraduate STEM courses. Exam/concept-inventory scores rose by 0.47 SD under active learning (about 6% on exams); students in traditional lectures 1.5 times more likely to fail; failure 34% vs 21%; no significant difference by STEM discipline — [Freeman et al. 2014, PubMed 24821756](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24821756/); [PDF copy](https://cdn.serc.carleton.edu/files/nagt/profdev/twp/sanbernardino/freeman_et_al._2014.pdf).
- The paper found larger gains on concept inventories than on instructor-written course exams, so the exam-specific gain is smaller than 0.47 (secondary) — [UMN record and summaries](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/active-learning-increases-student-performance-in-science-engineer/).
- Critiques: Dawson (Studies in Higher Education) reports reporting bias in the lecture-vs-active-learning literature — [Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/tackle-reporting-bias-pedagogy-research-paper-warns). Martella and colleagues found no Freeman-included article met all 12 internal-validity controls they set — [NSF PAR, Martella](https://par.nsf.gov/search/author:"Martella,%20Ronald%20C."). Freeman's own trim-and-fill and fail-safe N tests suggested no publication bias.
- Important framing: "active learning" in Freeman is added inside a taught course; it is not the absence of expert teaching. Read together with Alfieri, the evidence favours *expert-led, structured teaching with student activity*, over both pure lecturing and unguided discovery (inference).

**Video lectures**
- Noetel et al. (2021), *Review of Educational Research* 91(2), 204–236: 105 randomised trials, N = 7,776 higher-education students. Swapping video for existing teaching: g = 0.28; adding video to existing teaching: g = 0.80. Authors flag possible experimental and publication biases — [Noetel et al. 2021, DOI 10.3102/0034654321990713](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654321990713); [AERA press release](https://www.aera.net/tabid/19323/mid/50326/default.aspx).
- A 2024 preprint critiques the review for not modelling learner demographics and giving little context detail; not peer-reviewed — [Preprints.org 202401.0247](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202401.0247).

**Star teachers / teacher quality**
- Chetty, Friedman & Rockoff (2014), *American Economic Review* 104(9), 2633–2679: >1 million US children linked to tax records; high value-added teachers raise college attendance and earnings; 1 SD higher teacher value-added in one grade raises earnings at 28 by about 1%; replacing a bottom-5% teacher with an average one ≈ $250,000 more lifetime classroom income — [NBER w19424](https://www.nber.org/papers/19424); [NBER w17699](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17699). Disputed by Rothstein; authors' 2017 AER reply and an LA replication (Bacher-Hicks, Kane & Staiger) support the original — [Brown Annenberg: reply to Rothstein](https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/measuring-impacts-teachers-reply-rothstein).

**Shadow education / cram schools / private tuition (Asia)**
- Bray (2014), *Asia Pacific Education Review* 15(3), 381–389: the research on shadow education's impact on achievement is "inconclusive" — [HKU CERC record](https://cerc.edu.hku.hk/publications/cerc-monograph-series/shadow-education/attachment/review-jero-2014-2).
- Zhang, Bray & Jiang (2021, longitudinal, China): no clear significant positive effect of regular private tutoring, even with tutoring through summer and term (secondary, via later review) — [Springer, APER](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12564-021-09671-3).
- A 2026 PRISMA-style review reports positive effects of private tutoring on Korean CSAT scores but mixed results in China, mediated by modality, school quality and family SES — [HRMARS systematic review](https://hrmars.com/papers_submitted/28006/shadow-education-and-academic-achievement-a-systematic-review-of-global-efficacy-and-educational-equity.pdf). Low-tier journal; treat as a pointer only.

### Inferences
- The strong evidence is for *how* teaching is done (explicit, structured, with checks and student activity), not for "attending a famous coaching centre" or "reading only standard books". Shadow education studies are mostly observational and confounded by family background and motivation.
- No study found that tests the "few standard sources" idea (for example, NCERT-only vs many sources) directly.
- For exams: Freeman and Noetel use real course exams and university outcomes; DI and Chetty use standardised tests and long-run outcomes. This is real-world, not lab, evidence.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled d values in Stockard 2018 and the Freeman exam vs concept-inventory split could not be read from the full text (fetch failed).
- No meta-analysis of cram-school (juku, hagwon, kota coaching) effects on high-stakes exam scores was found; no rigorous study of curated digests or "standard book" strategies.
- No direct evidence on adult competitive-exam aspirants (e.g. civil-service exams).

### Grade
- **Research: 5. Effect: + (++ for explicit, structured teaching; ± for cram schools and star-teacher branding). Band: B (well supported, with conditions).**
- Justification: several large meta-analyses (Stockard k = 328; Freeman k = 225; Noetel k = 105; Alfieri k = 164) and a very large field study (Chetty) all show that good, structured teaching raises real exam and life outcomes. But the benefit depends on the form: passive lecturing alone is clearly worse than teaching with active elements, video works best as an add-on, and the "pay for coaching" or "star teacher" variants have inconclusive, confounded evidence (Bray). So the idea as a whole is B, not A; the explicit-instruction core would be A on its own.

## TUT: Tutoring and mentoring (human and AI)

### Takeaway
Human tutoring is one of the best-tested interventions in education: about 0.29–0.37 SD across ~90 randomised trials in school, on real tests, largest when given by trained tutors, often (≥3 times a week) and in school time. Bloom's "two sigma" is not replicated. Step-based intelligent tutoring systems come close to human tutors (VanLehn), but gains shrink on standardised tests. LLM (ChatGPT-style) tutoring is new: a few promising RCTs when the AI is designed to guide rather than give answers, and clear evidence that an unguarded chatbot can *harm* exam performance. Mentoring has small effects.

### Cited Findings
**Human tutoring**
- Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan (2020), NBER w27476: meta-analysis of RCTs of PreK–12 tutoring; pooled effect 0.37 SD; larger for teacher and paraprofessional tutors than volunteers or parents; larger in earlier grades and in-school than after-school — [NBER w27476](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476); [EdWorkingPapers ai20-267](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai20-267).
- Updated version (2024): 89 RCTs, pooled 0.288 SD (SE 0.029); literacy 0.290, maths 0.268; largest for teachers/paraprofessionals, earlier grades, ≥3 days a week, during school — [Stanford NSSA, "The Promise of Tutoring"](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/promise-tutoring-prek-12-learning-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis-experimental-evidence).
- Bloom (1984): tutoring plus mastery learning gave ~2 SD over conventional classes, from two University of Chicago dissertations — [Bloom 1984 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1984-bloom.pdf). Critics note the tutored group also had a higher mastery criterion (90% vs 80%) and short, narrow tests; one reanalysis puts tutoring's net effect near 0.85 SD after removing the mastery-learning part — [arXiv 2504.16132](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16132v1); [Nintil essay (blog)](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/).

**Intelligent (computer) tutoring**
- VanLehn (2011), *Educational Psychologist* 46(4), 197–221: human tutoring ≈ 0.79 vs no tutoring; step-based tutoring systems ≈ 0.76; substep-based ≈ 0.40; did not confirm Bloom's 2.0 (secondary figures) — [VanLehn slides, MIT](https://quality.mit.edu/files/2012/01/Quality-Symposium-Kurt-VanLehn.pdf); [record](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2051339053).
- Kulik & Fletcher (2016), *Review of Educational Research* 86(1), 42–78: 50 evaluations; median ITS effect 0.66 SD; much larger on locally developed tests (≈0.73) than standardised tests (≈0.13, secondary) — [Kulik & Fletcher 2016, DOI 10.3102/0034654315581420](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654315581420).

**AI (LLM) tutoring**
- Kestin et al. (2025), *Scientific Reports* 15, 17458: crossover RCT, 194 Harvard intro-physics students; AI tutor designed to give one step at a time vs in-class active learning; median post-test 4.5 vs 3.5, in about 49 vs 75 minutes; higher engagement. Single course, short-term — [Kestin et al. 2025, DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6); [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-ai-tutor-harvard-physics/).
- Bastani et al. (2025), *PNAS* 122, e2422633122 ("Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning"): high-school maths in Turkey (~1,000 students per summaries). GPT Base raised practice scores 48%, GPT Tutor (hints only) 127%; but on the unassisted exam, GPT Base students did 17% worse than controls; the guarded tutor largely removed the harm — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486); [Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education).
- Fischer & Rau (IZA DP 18338): 334 university students preparing for an incentivised exam; AI tutor access +0.23 SD; most help for lower-knowledge, more self-regulated students — [IZA DP18338](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp18338/1/citations/ris).
- Exploratory RCT in 5 UK secondary schools (N = 165; Google LearnLM with Eedi): AI-guided students did at least as well as students with human tutors; scaled trial registered April 2026 — [arXiv 2512.23633](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23633v1); [AEA registry](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/18079). Vendor-involved; weigh lightly.
- A meta-analysis of 35 ChatGPT experiments (Wu et al., 4,193 participants) reported g = 0.67, but most studies used ChatGPT as a supplement, not a structured tutor (secondary, seen via a citing paper) — [arXiv 2509.22725](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.22725v2).

**Mentoring**
- Eby, Allen, Evans, Ng & DuBois (2008), *Journal of Vocational Behavior* 72, 254–267: multidisciplinary meta-analysis; mentoring has small but favourable associations with many outcomes; academic and workplace mentoring larger than youth mentoring — [USF record](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/23/); DOI 10.1016/j.jvb.2007.04.005. Mostly correlational studies.

### Inferences
- Tutoring benefit comes from frequent, structured, curriculum-linked sessions by trained tutors, not from one-to-one contact as such.
- For AI tutors, *design* decides the sign of the effect: guided, step-by-step tutors help; answer-giving chatbots can lower unassisted exam scores. This matters directly for any "AI study buddy".
- The gap between ITS gains on local tests (0.73) and standardised tests (0.13) is a warning for any tool judged on its own quizzes.

### Gaps
- Almost all human-tutoring RCTs are PreK–12; little rigorous evidence for adults preparing for competitive exams.
- No LLM tutoring trial measured long-term retention or a real high-stakes exam; no independent meta-analysis of structured LLM tutors yet.
- Exact mentoring effect size on academic performance in Eby 2008 not retrieved.

### Grade
- **Human tutoring — Research: 5. Effect: ++ (≈0.3 SD on real tests in ~90 RCTs; robust practical gains). Band: A.**
- **AI (LLM) tutoring — Research: 2–3. Effect: ± (+ when guided; − when unguarded). Band: C.**
- **Mentoring — Research: 3. Effect: + (small). Band: B/C.**
- **TUT overall: Band B** (human tutoring is A, but the idea as used in exam-prep tools is mostly AI tutoring and mentoring, which are thinner).
- Justification: Nickow et al. pool ~90 randomised trials with real test outcomes and consistent gains; VanLehn and Kulik & Fletcher add many ITS studies. The effect size (~0.3 SD) is below the 0.4 rule of thumb but is large for field RCTs on standardised tests, so it counts as robust practical gain. AI tutoring has only a handful of short trials and a clear harm case (Bastani), so it sits lower.

## ELA: Understanding and elaboration (self-explanation, elaborative interrogation, concept maps, dual coding / multimedia)

### Takeaway
Explaining to yourself why something is true reliably helps (self-explanation g = 0.55 across 64 reports; concept maps g = 0.58; elaborative interrogation d ≈ 0.56). Pairing words with relevant pictures (Mayer's multimedia principles) also has large lab effects. But most studies are short lab or single-lesson studies, effects depend on prior knowledge and prompt quality, and Dunlosky et al. rated these techniques only "moderate utility" for lack of classroom testing.

### Cited Findings
- Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018), *Educational Psychology Review* 30(3), 703–725: 69 effect sizes from 64 reports (~6,000 participants); overall g = .55 (95% CI .45–.65); self-explanation beat instructor-provided explanation (g = .35); vs no extra explanation g = .67; multiple-choice prompts g = .24 (not significant, k = 2); classic and Orwin fail-safe N suggest robustness to publication bias (checked in full text) — [Bisra et al. 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x); [PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf).
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58: rated elaborative interrogation and self-explanation "moderate utility", partly because they "have not been adequately evaluated in educational contexts"; elaborative interrogation may help less for low-knowledge learners — [Dunlosky et al. 2013, DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/improving-students-learning-with-effective-learning-techniques); [Duke record](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/pn/faculty/emarsh/publications/252743).
- Elaborative interrogation: Donoghue & Hattie (2021) meta-analysis reports d ≈ 0.56 over 254 comparisons and 2,138 learners, more for surface than deep learning (secondary) — [Structural Learning briefing](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/ask-why-and-how). Older text studies show benefits rise with prior knowledge — [Alberta Journal of Educational Research](https://journalhosting.ucalgary.ca/index.php/ajer/article/view/54666).
- Concept maps: Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope (2018), *Educational Psychology Review* 30(2), 431–455: 142 effect sizes, n = 11,814; g = .58 overall; constructing maps g = .72, studying given maps g = .43; works in STEM and non-STEM — [Schroeder et al. 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789). Earlier Nesbit & Adesope (2006), *Review of Educational Research*: 55 studies / 67 effect sizes, positive — [Nesbit & Adesope 2006](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/nesbitadesope2006.htm).
- Multimedia / dual coding: Mayer's own tallies — coherence principle median d = 0.86 (23 of 23 tests), signalling median 0.41–0.69 depending on edition; multimedia principle median above 1 — [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E); [EdTech Books](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349). An independent signalling meta-analysis (29 studies, 44 effects): d = .38 — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842). A meta-analysis of 92 Mayer-authored articles (591 effects) is also listed — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/search/award_ids:1661231).

### Inferences
- Medians from the author's own lab (Mayer) are larger than the independent meta-analysis (signalling 0.41–0.69 vs 0.38): expect real-world effects nearer the lower figures.
- Self-explanation works best with open prompts and is weaker with multiple-choice prompts; this matters for app design.
- Most outcomes are immediate or short-delay tests on single lessons, not end-of-course exams.

### Gaps
- Donoghue & Hattie (2021) not read in full; figure is secondary.
- Little evidence on long-term retention or on high-stakes exams for any ELA technique; no evidence on SQ3R / PQ4R / Feynman-technique branding as such.
- No independent meta-analysis of the "multimedia principle" itself was retrieved.

### Grade
- **Research: 4. Effect: + to ++ (g ≈ 0.4–0.7, conditional on prior knowledge and prompt quality). Band: B.**
- Justification: three independent meta-analyses (self-explanation, concept maps, elaborative interrogation) agree on moderate positive effects, and publication-bias checks for self-explanation look reassuring. But most studies are short and lab- or lesson-based, effects shrink for low-knowledge learners, and Dunlosky's review flags thin classroom testing. Well supported, with conditions.

## EXM: Worked examples and model answers

### Takeaway
Studying worked examples beats solving equivalent problems unaided for novices (maths meta-analysis g = 0.48), but the benefit reverses as learners gain expertise (expertise reversal), so examples should fade into practice. Evidence is mostly maths and science procedures; studying "topper copies" or model essays is essentially untested.

### Cited Findings
- Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023), *Educational Psychology Review*: worked-examples effect on maths performance, elementary to postsecondary; average g = 0.48 (robust variance estimation); correct-only examples better than incorrect or mixed; adding self-explanation prompts *reduced* the effect relative to examples alone; effect similar for practice and skill acquisition — [Barbieri et al. 2023, DOI 10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance). Preliminary poster: g = 0.43, 185 effect sizes, I² = 94.5% (very high heterogeneity) — [UD poster PDF](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf).
- Expertise reversal: Kalyuga (2007), *Educational Psychology Review*, reviews evidence that instructional methods that help novices (like worked examples) can become ineffective or harmful for more knowledgeable learners; faded examples and completion problems recommended — [Kalyuga 2007, DOI 10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect). Some replications find it for transfer but not retention — [Instructional Science 2012](https://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s11251-012-9237-2).
- Self-explaining worked examples is one of the main settings in the Bisra 2018 self-explanation meta-analysis (see ELA) — [Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x).

### Inferences
- Barbieri and Bisra appear to disagree on whether self-explanation prompts add to worked examples; likely depends on comparison condition (Barbieri compares to examples without prompts; Bisra compares to no self-explanation).
- For exam prep, studying solved papers is likely most useful early in a topic and should give way to timed practice; for essay-type exams the evidence base is a stretch.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of model answers or exemplars for essay writing was found within the search budget; no evidence on "topper copies" or topper strategy books.
- Barbieri's k (number of studies) and N not retrieved from the published paper.

### Grade
- **Research: 4. Effect: ++ for novices in procedural domains; − for advanced learners (expertise reversal); n/a for essays. Band: B.**
- Justification: a recent meta-analysis (g ≈ 0.48) plus a long cognitive-load literature support worked examples, but heterogeneity is very high, the evidence is mostly maths and science, and benefit reverses with expertise. "Model answers / topper copies" on their own would be Band D.

## NOT: Note-making and organising knowledge (note-taking, longhand vs laptop, summarising, concept/mind maps as notes)

### Takeaway
Taking notes and especially reviewing them helps, and teaching students how to take notes helps (g ≈ 0.54, 9 studies). The famous "longhand beats laptop" finding did not replicate (Urry 2021); later meta-analyses are split between a small handwriting advantage and no difference. Summarising was rated low utility. Building concept maps yourself (g = .72) is the best-supported note format. Systems like Zettelkasten, PARA or "second brain" have no outcome research found.

### Cited Findings
- Kobayashi (2005), *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 30, 242–262: meta-analysis of the encoding effect of note-taking — [Kobayashi 2005, DOI 10.1016/j.cedpsych.2004.10.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2004.10.001). A later Kobayashi (2006) meta-analysis is cited for a small encoding effect without review, d = 0.22, with bigger effects when notes are reviewed (secondary) — [Liu & Uesaka 2022, Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.893237/pdf).
- Jansen, Lakens & IJsselsteijn (2017), *Educational Research Review* 22, 223–233: integrative review of the cognitive costs and benefits of note-taking (working-memory load) — [Jansen et al. 2017, DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2017.10.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.10.001). Findings not retrieved (closed access).
- Reed, Rimel & Hallett (2016), *Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness*: note-taking instruction for college students; 9 group-design studies, weighted mean effect 0.54 (95% CI 0.47–0.62); individual g from −0.35 to 2.11 — [U Iowa record](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Note-Taking-Interventions-for-College-Students-A/9983993201102771).
- Longhand vs laptop: Urry et al. (2021), *Psychological Science* 32(3), 326–339: direct replication of Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) Study 1 with 145 students (original 67); no effect of medium on factual or conceptual recall; mini meta-analysis also non-significant — [Urry et al. 2021, DOI 10.1177/0956797620965541](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797620965541); [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/no-benefit-handwriting-notes.html). Flanigan et al. (2024), *Educational Psychology Review*: 24 studies; taking *and reviewing* handwritten notes linked to higher achievement, g = 0.248; Voyer et al. (2022) found medium has little influence — [Georgia Southern record](https://scholars.georgiasouthern.edu/en/publications/typed-versus-handwritten-lecture-notes-and-college-student-achiev/).
- Summarisation: Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated it low utility, mainly because benefits depend heavily on training and task; not harmful — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/improving-students-learning-with-effective-learning-techniques); [Kaneb Center summary](https://sites.nd.edu/kaneb/?p=1307).
- Concept maps as notes: constructing maps g = .72 vs studying given maps g = .43 — [Schroeder et al. 2018](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/17789).
- Note-taking while learning a second language: Jin & Webb meta-analysis g = 0.56 (95% CI 0.24–0.88) — [Cambridge PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C5CA68C9A1B1E873754EFCFEFF55A005/S0272263123000529a.pdf/the-effectiveness-of-note-taking-through-exposure-to-l2-input-a-meta-analysis.pdf).

### Inferences
- The benefit of notes comes mainly from *reviewing* them (and from generating structure), not from the act of writing; this overlaps with retrieval and ELA.
- The laptop vs pen debate is a small effect at most; distraction on laptops (not tested here) may matter more than the medium.

### Gaps
- Overall effect sizes in Kobayashi 2005 not retrieved; Jansen 2017 findings not read.
- No outcome research found for Cornell notes as a named method, Zettelkasten, PARA, "second brain" or note-compression routines.
- Few studies measure long-term retention or real exams.

### Grade
- **Research: 3–4. Effect: + (modest; depends on reviewing notes and on training). Band: B (take and review notes; build concept maps); C for longhand-vs-laptop (±) and summarising; D for PKM systems (Zettelkasten, second brain).**
- Justification: several meta-analyses support note-taking with review and note-taking training, but effects are modest, many studies are short lecture-recall tasks, the best-known sub-claim (handwriting) failed replication, and summarising is rated low utility. Overall NOT sits at the lower end of B.

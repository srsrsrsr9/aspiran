# Evidence for six "feedback and steering" ideas: ADP, FBK, TST, TGT, PLN, LHL (Part 4)

Scope: published evidence for working on weak spots (ADP), feedback and error correction (FBK), exam rehearsal and technique (TST), exam targeting (TGT), planning and self-monitoring (PLN) and learning how to learn (LHL). Definitions come from `Exam success central ideas/taxonomy_v1.md`. Grades use `grading_scale.md` exactly.

Method note: 21 web searches, then the shared session search budget ran out. After that I fetched pages directly from publisher sites only (PLOS ONE worked; APA PsycNet and EEF blocked access). So some candidate sources could not be checked: Richardson, Abraham & Bond 2012; the EEF metacognition strand; Duckworth et al. on MCII/WOOP; Becker 1990 (only seen through a secondary source); Kulik, Kulik & Bangert 1984 (findings not retrieved); Dignath & Büttner 2008 (separate figures not retrieved). These are listed under Gaps. No effect size below comes from memory: each has a source link, and a figure is called secondary where it came from a secondary source.

## ADP: Working on weak spots (adaptive learning / ITS, mastery learning, deliberate practice)

### Takeaway
Intelligent tutoring systems and mastery learning both have large meta-analyses showing real gains on tests. The gains are biggest against ordinary whole-class teaching and disappear when ITS is compared with small groups or human tutors. Deliberate practice, the idea that "practise what you can't do" explains expertise, explains only about 4% of performance differences in education. **Grade: research 4, effect +, band B.**

### Cited Findings
- **Kulik & Fletcher (2016), *Review of Educational Research* 86(1), 42–78.** Meta-analysis of 50 controlled evaluations of intelligent tutoring systems. The median effect raised test scores by 0.66 SD. Ten studies were excluded for non-conventional control groups (6) or flawed ITS implementations (4). The authors say results depend on the control treatment and on how well the program was implemented; some effects were small. The work came out of US defence research (Institute for Defense Analyses). — [SAGE, doi 10.3102/0034654315581420](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654315581420); [IDA report](https://www.ida.org/research-and-publications/publications/all/e/ef/effectiveness-of-intelligent-tutoring-systems-a-meta-analytic-review)
- **Ma, Adesope, Nesbit & Liu (2014), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 106(4), 901–918.** ITS meta-analysis. ITS beat large-group teacher-led teaching (g = .44). Against small-group teaching (g = .05) and one-to-one human tutoring (g = −.11) there was no significant difference. The benefit held from primary school to university and in both lab and classroom studies. The authors judged the result consistent with their publication-bias analysis. The overall pooled g was not retrieved in my searches. — [Author PDF](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/ma2014intelligent.pdf); [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Intelligent-Tutoring-Systems-and-Learning-Outcomes/99900601053801842)
- Caveat on Ma et al.: systems were mostly tested under the conditions they were designed for, so results may not generalise. — [Author PDF](https://www.cs.uky.edu/~sgware/reading/papers/ma2014intelligent.pdf)
- A 2025 systematic review describes ITS effectiveness as lacking scientific consensus. — [PMC12078640](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12078640/)
- **Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), *Review of Educational Research* 60(2), 265–299.** 108 controlled evaluations of mastery learning programs, with positive effects on examination performance in colleges, high schools and upper primary grades. The paper also criticises Slavin's (1987) much less favourable review for its study selection. I did not retrieve the pooled effect size. A secondary summary claims gains are larger for weaker students and that completion rates drop; both are unverified. — [SAGE TOC / record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2190/FG7X-7Q9V-JX8M-RDJP); [Academia abstract](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis); [Slavin 1987](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf)
- **Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), *Psychological Science*.** Deliberate practice explained about 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions. The analysis covered 157 effect sizes and N > 11,000. — [PMC4101876 ("Facing facts")](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4101876); [record](https://stafforini.com/works/macnamara-2014-deliberate-practice-and/)
- **Ericsson's side.** Critics say many of the effect sizes measured ordinary practice rather than individualised deliberate practice. A 2021 reanalysis argues the meta-analyses underestimate deliberate practice. Macnamara and colleagues replied by reporting both r and r². A 2016 sports meta-analysis by the same group followed. — [Frontiers commentary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00751/full); [2021 reanalysis, Current Psychology](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s12144-021-02326-x); [Macnamara et al. 2016 reply](https://scottbarrykaufman.com///wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Macnamara-et-al.-2016-Reply-to-Ericsson.pdf); [2016 sports meta](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/macnamara-et-al-2016-the-relationship-between-deliberate-practice-and-performance-in-sports-a-meta-analysis.pdf)

### Inferences
- The strong part of ADP is "diagnose, then practise the gap until mastered", the approach shared by ITS and mastery learning. It mostly beats untargeted whole-class teaching and adds little over a good human tutor. For a self-studying exam candidate, the alternative is usually untargeted self-study, which is the comparison where ITS shows gains.
- Most outcomes are course or researcher tests, not national exams. That is why the grade is B rather than A.
- Deliberate practice as a general theory of exam success is weakly supported in education (4% of variance; the estimate rests on few education studies). It should not carry the ADP grade.
- Brand note: vendor claims for adaptive apps are not the same as this evidence. The meta-analyses cover research-grade systems.

### Gaps
- Pooled g for Ma et al. 2014 and for Kulik et al. 1990 (not in the retrieved text).
- Number of education studies behind the 4% figure in Macnamara et al. 2014.
- Kulik & Fletcher's separate figure for standardised versus local tests was not confirmed. The search summary only said some effects were "small".

## FBK: Feedback and error correction (error logs, exam wrappers)

### Takeaway
Feedback is among the most-studied interventions in education. It has a medium average effect (d ≈ 0.4–0.5), but results vary enormously: over a third of feedback interventions make performance worse. Corrective, task-focused feedback works; praise and self-focused feedback do not. Error logs and exam wrappers as standalone tools have thin and partly null evidence. **Grade for feedback overall: research 5, effect +, band B. Sub-grade for exam wrappers and mistake notebooks: research 2, effect ±, band C.**

### Cited Findings
- **Kluger & DeNisi (1996), *Psychological Bulletin*.** 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations. Feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41), but over one-third reduced it. A secondary count gives 231 of 607 as negative. Sampling error, feedback sign and existing theories do not explain this. The authors' Feedback Intervention Theory: feedback gets less effective as attention moves from the task to the self. — [Abstract, Hebrew University](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc); [record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/); [secondary summary](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/kluger-denisi-1996/)
- **Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), *Frontiers in Psychology* (doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087).** 435 studies, k = 994 effect sizes, N > 61,000. Medium overall effect, d = 0.48, with significant heterogeneity, so "feedback" is not one consistent treatment. Effects are larger for cognitive and motor outcomes than for motivation and behaviour. The information content of the feedback matters most. Corrective feedback was highly effective; praise, punishment and rewards had low effects. — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full); [PMC6987456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456)
- **Hattie & Timperley (2007), "The Power of Feedback".** Not retrieved in my searches. Wisniewski et al. (2020) describe their meta-analysis as replicating and extending the Visible Learning research. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
- **Metcalfe (2017), "Learning from Errors", *Annual Review of Psychology* 68, 465–489 (doi 10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022).** A review of behavioural and neural evidence. For typical learners, making and engaging with errors helps when corrective feedback follows: errors strengthen later memory for the correct answer and direct attention. Errorless learning came from work with amnesic patients and does not carry over to typical students. — [Full-text PDF (Wesleyan)](https://wesleyan.edu/ofcd/resources/OFCD%20Resource%20Docs/Learning%20from%20Errors.pdf)
- **Soicher & Gurung (2017), *Psychology Learning & Teaching* 16(1), 64–73.** A single-course randomised study (N = 86) comparing exam wrappers, sham wrappers and no wrapper. There were no differences in final grades, in any exam, or in Metacognitive Awareness Inventory scores, even after controlling for baseline metacognition. Earlier work by Lovett reported gains but lacked statistical analysis of performance. — [Oregon State record](https://liberalarts.oregonstate.edu/biblio/do-exam-wrappers-increase-metacognition-and-performance-single-course-intervention); [Academia PDF](https://www.academia.edu/32154890/Soicher_and_Gurung_pdf)
- Commentary on wrappers: they may work better when instructors teach metacognitive strategies alongside them, or when used across several courses. As a standalone activity, their impact varies widely. — [Faculty Focus](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/educational-assessment/strategy-work-look-exam-wrappers/); [Improve with Metacognition](https://www.improvewithmetacognition.com/2019/06/11/)

### Inferences
- The research amount is extensive (two meta-analyses with hundreds of studies), but "it depends" is the core finding. Band B fits better than A: the average effect is ≥ 0.4, but over a third of interventions harm performance and heterogeneity is high.
- For exam takers, the active ingredient is corrective information about what was wrong and why, followed by re-attempting. Metcalfe supports this.
- Mistake notebooks (错题本, 오답노트) are widely used in East Asia but I found no controlled trial of them. Their support is indirect, via corrective feedback and retrieval.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of error logs or mistake notebooks found. Lovett's original exam-wrapper data were not retrieved.
- Hattie & Timperley 2007 was not directly retrieved.
- Publication-bias figures for Wisniewski et al. were not checked.

## TST: Exam rehearsal and test-taking technique (practice tests, test-wiseness, coaching)

### Takeaway
Taking the same kind of test again reliably raises scores by about a quarter of an SD, more with coaching or identical forms. Commercial coaching for admissions tests like the SAT and ACT gives only small gains (about 30 of 1600 SAT points, under 1 of 36 ACT points). Test-wiseness training has a meta-analysis (Samson 1985), but I could not retrieve its effect size. The retrieval benefit of practice tests belongs to the RET idea. **Grade: research 4, effect +, band B.**

### Cited Findings
- **Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard (2007), *Journal of Applied Psychology*.** 50 studies, 107 samples, N = 134,436. The adjusted overall retest (practice) effect on cognitive ability tests is d = .26. Gains are largest when practice comes with coaching and when identical forms are used. 84% of the studies were in educational settings; the median sample was 91. — [DePaul record](https://depaul.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/retesting-in-selection-a-meta-analysis-of-practice-effects-for-te/); [Cornell eCommons PDF](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/4fbba765-0d9c-4940-8492-66fecaca5227/content)
- **Scharfen, Peters & Holling (2018) update.** 122 studies, 174 samples, 786 test outcomes, N = 153,185. Retest effects were confirmed; I did not retrieve the pooled value. — [PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/iq/2018-scharfen.pdf)
- **Kulik, Kulik & Bangert (1984).** An earlier review of practice effects on aptitude and achievement tests, cited by Hausknecht et al. as the previous quantitative review. Its findings were not retrieved. — [Cornell eCommons PDF (Hausknecht et al.)](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/4fbba765-0d9c-4940-8492-66fecaca5227/content)
- **Briggs (2009), NACAC discussion paper "Preparation for College Admission Exams".** Coaching gives about +30 points on the SAT (out of 1600) and under 1 point on the ACT (out of 36). Above-average scorers may gain most. A third of colleges said a 20-point SAT-math gain would "significantly improve" admission chances, so small gains can still matter where cut-offs are tight. From press coverage; the PDF was not retrieved. — [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/value-of-college-admissions-test-prep-classes-unclear/2011/02); [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/index%2Ephp/node/78564)
- **Briggs (2001), NELS:88.** Coaching raises SAT math by about 14–15 points and verbal by 6–8 points. Gains cannot all be credited to coaching because of self-selection. — [NEPC PDF](https://nepc.colorado.edu/files/Briggs_Theeffectofadmissionstestpreparation.pdf)
- **Becker (1990) and Laird (1983) meta-analyses, as reported by Briggs.** Commercial courses gave about 9–25 points on SAT verbal and 15–25 on math. — [NEPC PDF](https://nepc.colorado.edu/files/Briggs_Theeffectofadmissionstestpreparation.pdf)
- **Samson (1985), "Effects of training in test-taking skills on achievement test performance: a quantitative synthesis", *Journal of Educational Research* 78(5), 261–266.** Exists, but the effect size was not retrieved. — [U. Michigan Deep Blue (citing reference)](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/a0b14fb1-12e7-43bd-9f98-38058023f75f)
- A single-study example: test-taking skills training raised one subtest score for students with learning disabilities. — [SAGE, doi 10.2466/pms.1985.60.3.847](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2466/pms.1985.60.3.847)

### Inferences
- The evidence is large and the outcomes are real high-stakes tests (SAT, ACT, selection tests), which is rare. The effects are modest, so the grade is +.
- Retest gains partly reflect familiarity with the test format rather than more ability. For a candidate that still counts, since it is a real exam score.
- Very large claimed coaching gains (common in vendor marketing) are not supported by independent studies.

### Gaps
- Samson 1985 and Kulik et al. 1984 effect sizes.
- No good studies found on full timed mock-test series for high-stakes national exams (UPSC, gaokao, 수능 and similar), beyond the retrieval-practice literature.
- Briggs 2009 numbers come from press coverage, not the report itself.

## TGT: Exam targeting (past papers and syllabus-driven selection; teaching to the test)

### Takeaway
At the school-system level, teaching to the test raises scores on the targeted test, but those gains often do not show up on other tests of the same subject. Jacob's Chicago study and Koretz's work on score inflation document this. I found no controlled studies of individual candidates choosing topics from past papers. **Grade for targeting as a way to raise the targeted exam score: research 2, effect +. Grade as real learning: ±. Overall band C.**

### Cited Findings
- **Jacob (2005), *Journal of Public Economics* (NBER w8968).** Chicago's 1996–97 high-stakes accountability raised math and reading scores relative to prior trends and to other Midwest urban districts. Item-level analysis shows the gains were driven by test-specific skills and student effort. For younger pupils, there was no gain on a low-stakes state exam. Teachers moved time away from untested subjects (science, social studies) and increased special-education placements and grade retention. — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8968); [NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8968.pdf)
- **Koretz.** High stakes lead to teaching to the test, which inflates scores. State-test gains have often exceeded NAEP gains. Examples: Kentucky grade 4 reading rose sharply on the state test (1992–94) while slipping on NAEP; Texas minority students' large TAAS gains were much smaller on NAEP. Koretz notes inflation varies by place. — [RAND brief](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_briefs/RB8008.html); [Koretz, World Bank slides](https://www.worldbank.org/content/dam/Worldbank/Event/education/Daniel%20Koretz.pdf); [Education Week](https://edweek.org/policy-politics/testing-expert-sees-illusions-of-progress-under-nclb/2008/09)
- **Critique of Koretz.** The early Cannell evidence involved mostly no-stakes tests with lax security, so it does not cleanly test the high-stakes claim. This comes from an advocacy source. — [Nonpartisan Education Review](https://nonpartisaneducation.org/Foundation/KoretzReview.htm)

### Inferences
- These studies show targeting works for the stated goal of a higher score on that specific exam. Gains do not transfer, and the cost is narrower learning. For a candidate whose aim is the exam itself, that makes TGT effective but narrow.
- The research is about schools and systems, not individual learners analysing previous-year questions (PYQs). Applying it to individual "80/20 from past papers" strategies is indirect, hence research 2.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found of individual past-paper or syllabus-mapping strategies against unstructured study.
- No independent cross-state study comparing state-test and NAEP gains was retrieved, only Koretz's examples.

## PLN: Planning and self-monitoring (SRL, metacognition, goal setting, implementation intentions, WOOP, time management)

### Takeaway
Training school pupils in self-regulated learning has moderate effects (about 0.5–0.6 SD). If-then plans (implementation intentions) reliably help people act on their goals (d = .65 across 94 tests). Time management correlates moderately with academic achievement, but the evidence is mostly cross-sectional. **Grade: research 4, effect +, band B.**

### Cited Findings
- **Dignath, Büttner & Langfeldt (2008), *Educational Research Review* 3, 101–129.** 74 studies (1992–2006) of self-regulation training in primary and secondary schools. Average effect about +0.61 (secondary report). Effects were larger in earlier grades; Donker et al. 2014 found no age difference. — [Asia-Pacific Forum (secondary)](https://www.eduhk.hk/apfslt/v21_issue1/yuzie/page2.htm); [Frontiers 2022 (secondary)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.889201/pdf)
- Reviews summarise the Dignath 2008, Donker 2014 and de Boer 2018 meta-analyses as showing moderate, significant effects of about 0.50–0.60. — [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.889201/pdf)
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69–119 (doi 10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1).** 94 independent tests; implementation intentions had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = .65, 95% CI about .6–.7). The plans helped people start, avoid distraction, give up failing courses of action and save effort for later goals. Outcomes are general goals (health and so on), not mainly exams. — [Konstanz repository](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f); [Frontiers 2021 citing CI](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/pdf)
- **Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2024) update.** 642 tests, d from 0.27 to 0.66 across outcomes. Plans work better in explicit if-then form, with high motivation and with rehearsal. From a secondary source. — [Goals and Progress summary](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-research/)
- **Aeon, Faber & Panaccio (2021), *PLOS ONE* 16(1), e0245066.** 158 studies, 490 effect sizes, N = 53,957. Time management has a moderate relationship with academic achievement (76 studies), slightly stronger than with job performance (r ≈ .25). It relates more strongly to wellbeing; with distress, r = −.358. Most studies are cross-sectional, so causation is not established. Heterogeneity is high and publication bias is judged low. Experiments show time-management training raises felt control and wellbeing, sometimes without performance gains. The exact r for achievement was not visible in the fetched text. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0245066)

### Inferences
- The strongest causal evidence is for teaching SRL and metacognitive strategies in schools, which overlaps heavily with LHL, and for if-then planning. Timetables and time management per se are mainly correlational.
- WOOP/MCII with students is plausible given the implementation-intention evidence, but I could not verify specific student trials (see Gaps).
- Most SRL trials use researcher-made tests (see LHL), which inflates effects.

### Gaps
- Duckworth et al. MCII/WOOP student trials (for example the PSAT practice study) could not be searched; the budget ran out.
- Richardson, Abraham & Bond (2012), the meta-analysis of correlates of university GPA (effort regulation, performance self-efficacy, grade goal): APA PsycNet did not load, so no figures are reported.
- EEF Toolkit metacognition strand: the page returned 403, so its months-of-progress and evidence-strength figures were not retrieved.

## LHL: Learning how to learn (study-skills and learning-strategy instruction)

### Takeaway
Teaching students how to learn, especially with metacognitive components, has moderate positive effects in several meta-analyses. Effects are larger on researcher-made tests than on independent or standardised ones and fade somewhat over time. Strategy courses that include retrieval practice do best. **Grade: research 4, effect +, band B.**

### Cited Findings
- **Hattie, Biggs & Purdie (1996), *Review of Educational Research* 66(2).** 51 studies of study-skills interventions, grouped by type (task-related skills, self-management, affective) and by near versus far transfer. Secondary sources say interventions involving metacognition had about double the effect of those without; this figure is unverified. One index lists pp. 87–97; check page numbers against the journal before citing. — [Melbourne record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/968643-effects-of-learning-skills-interventions-on-student-learning--a-meta-analysis); [SAGE issue TOC](https://journals.sagepub.com/toc/rera/66/2); [practitioner summary](https://www.teacherstoolbox.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/effectivestudyskills.doc)
- **Purdie & Hattie (1999).** A separate meta-analysis that found low correlations between self-reported study skills and learning outcomes. — [Avondale repository](https://research.avondale.edu.au/bitstreams/2145dc0b-852f-4dcc-b972-de7ec054d367/download)
- **Donker, de Boer, Kostons, Dignath-van Ewijk & van der Werf (2014), *Educational Research Review* 11, 1–26.** 58 studies (2000–2012), 95 interventions, in primary and secondary education, covering cognitive, metacognitive, management and motivational strategy instruction. The interventions were moderately successful. Those that included retrieval practice were most effective. The largest effects came from tests designed by the researchers who ran the intervention; effects on independent tests were smaller but still positive. The authors call for standardised outcome measures. The overall g was not retrieved. — [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/10/13-1); [TUM Clearinghouse review](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/CHU_KR-14_Donker-2014-Selbstreguliertes-Lernen.pdf)
- **de Boer, Donker, Kostons & van der Werf (2018), *Educational Research Review*.** On the long-term effects of metacognitive strategy instruction on academic performance. I could not fetch the PDF, so effect sizes are not reported. — [PDF link (connection refused)](https://www.dane.Ac-versailleS.fr/IMG/pdf/deboer2018.pdf)
- Do not confuse this with **de Boer, Donker & van der Werf (2014), *Review of Educational Research* 84, 509–545**, on intervention attributes. There, collaborative work slightly reduced effectiveness. — [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/10/13-1)

### Inferences
- LHL works mainly through its contents. Teaching strategies that work (retrieval, spacing, planning and monitoring) helps; teaching "study skills" in general does not guarantee use. Low correlations between self-reported study skills and outcomes (Purdie & Hattie 1999) support this.
- The bias toward researcher-made tests means the true effect on real exams is probably at the lower end of the moderate range, hence + rather than ++.
- Most evidence is from school pupils. University and adult exam-candidate evidence is thinner in what I retrieved.

### Gaps
- Overall g for Hattie et al. 1996 and Donker et al. 2014, and the follow-up effect sizes for de Boer et al. 2018: full texts not retrieved.
- No independent trial found of popular MOOCs such as "Learning How to Learn" measuring exam outcomes.

# Part 4 evidence notes: FOC, HAB, SPC, GRP, CMP (focus, habits, study spaces, groups, comparison)

Researched 1 Nov 2026. Grading uses `grading_scale.md` in this folder (research amount 0–5, effect ++/+/±/0/−/n/a, band A–E).
Method note: the shared web-search budget ran out after about 25 searches, so a few candidate papers could not be

## FOC: Attention and distraction control (phones, multitasking, Pomodoro and breaks, blockers and friction)

### Takeaway
Phone use and media multitasking are consistently linked with somewhat lower grades, but the links are small and mostly
correlational, the famous "mere presence" (brain drain) effect has failed direct replication, and school phone bans show
mixed results. Scheduled (Pomodoro-style) breaks improve mood and efficiency but not measured performance; friction apps
cut phone use but have no exam-outcome data. Grade: **phones/multitasking 4, +, band B; breaks/Pomodoro 2, ±, band C;
blockers/friction 2, + for use (n/a for learning), band C/D.**

### Cited Findings
**Phones and academic performance (correlational metas and reviews)**
- Kates, Wu & Coryn (2018), "The effects of mobile phone use on academic performance: A meta-analysis," *Computers & Education* 127, 107–112: small negative association between phone use and academic outcomes. A 2024 review reports the problematic-use subgroup as r = −0.12 from only k = 7 effect sizes and questions its precision (secondary) — [PMC review citing Kates](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11894741/); [UK evidence summary](https://ormistonacademiestrust.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/mobile-phones-evidence-summary.pdf). DOI to check: 10.1016/j.compedu.2018.08.012 (not opened).
- Amez & Baert (2020), "Smartphone use and academic performance: A literature review," *International Journal of Educational Research* 103: most studies in higher education find a negative association between smartphone use frequency and academic success, but associations "cannot be given a causal interpretation"; size varies with how use and performance are measured; most studies rely on self-reported use — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3483961); [GLO summary](https://glabor.org/does-smartphone-use-reduce-the-academic-success-of-students-evidence-reviewed-in-a-new-glo-discussion-paper/).
- May & Elder (2018), "Efficient, helpful, or distracting? A literature review of media multitasking in relation to academic performance," *International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education* (published 27 Feb 2018): reviews 38 articles (2003–2017); heavier multitasking linked to lower exam scores and less efficient study; evidence mostly supports the "scattered attention" view though some results fit a "trained attention" view (secondary) — [Lumen summary](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/learning-hacks/?p=40).

**"Brain drain" (mere presence of one's phone)**
- Ward et al. (2017), *Journal of the Association for Consumer Research*: phone in another room outperformed phone on desk on working-memory tests — [journal](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdfplus/10.1086/691462).
- Ruiz Pardo & Minda (2022), *Acta Psychologica*, pre-registered direct replication of Ward's Experiment 2: no difference between phone-location conditions on operation span or go/no-go. DOI 10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103717 — [FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1086/691462/).
- Hartmann et al. (2020/2021), *Consciousness and Cognition*, N = 302: partial replication found no overall effect of phone presence on short-term or prospective memory; a small prospective-memory effect only in low-dependence users — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/revisiting-brain-drain-effect).
- A CogSci 2025 study using smartphone abstinence also found no brain-drain effect and notes most replication attempts are null — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/61s0x8ks).
- Conflict: Böttger, Poschik & Zierer (2023) meta-analysis is reported to find 22 studies confirming Ward et al. (secondary; not checked) — [Univ. Augsburg news](https://uni-augsburg.de/en/campusleben/neuigkeiten/2023/09/19/the-smartphone-brain-drain-effect).

**School phone bans (real-exam outcomes)**
- Beland & Murphy (2016, working paper; later *Labour Economics*): about 91 English high schools; bans associated with +6.41% of a SD in test scores, about 14% SD for the lowest achievers, little effect for top achievers; authors flag possible student sorting — [LSU working paper](https://lsuonline.lsu.edu/business/economics/files/workingpapers/pap15_03.pdf); critique: [The Conversation / Western Sydney](https://theconversation.com/no-education-minister-we-dont-have-enough-evidence-to-support-banning-mobile-phones-in-schools-151574).
- Goodyear et al. (2025), SMART Schools, *Lancet Regional Health – Europe*: 30 English secondary schools (20 restrictive, 10 permissive), 1,227 pupils; no notable difference in attainment, wellbeing, sleep, activity or classroom disruption; restrictive policies reduced phone time only slightly — [University of Birmingham](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news/2025/school-bans-alone-not-enough-to-tackle-negative-impacts-of-phone-and-social-media-use).

**Breaks and Pomodoro**
- Biwer, Wiradhany, oude Egbrink & de Bruin (2023), *British Journal of Educational Psychology*, DOI 10.1111/bjep.12593: 87 Dutch university students in a real self-study session; self-regulated breaks vs 24 min work / 6 min break (Pomodoro-style) vs 12/3. Systematic breaks gave less fatigue and distraction and more concentration and motivation; **no difference in task completion or mental effort**, so the gain was mood and efficiency (same work in less time) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/); [Maastricht](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel). Note: some later secondary write-ups report the opposite mood direction; the original paper is the authority.
- Albulescu et al. (2022), "Give me a break!", *PLOS ONE*: 22 samples, N = 2,335; micro-breaks (≤10 min) raised vigour (d = 0.36) and lowered fatigue (d = 0.35); performance effect small and non-significant overall (d = 0.16, p = .116), significant only for low-demand tasks; longer breaks helped more — [DOAJ abstract](https://doaj.org/article/c6f37b6ba159469296b2cb968b65505f).

**Friction and blockers**
- Grüning, Riedel & Lorenz-Spreen (2023), *PNAS* 120(8), DOI 10.1073/pnas.2213114120: one sec app (10-second delay, deliberation message, option to back out). Six-week field study, N = 280: 36% of attempts to open a target app were abandoned; target-app openings down 57% after six weeks. Pre-registered online study (N = 500): the dismiss option did most, the delay helped, the message alone did nothing — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409). **Conflict of interest:** Riedel is the app's maker and Grüning is listed as one sec's lead scientist — [one sec research page](https://one-sec.app/research/). Outcome is app use, not learning.

### Inferences
- Phone and multitasking evidence is broad (several reviews, real grades) but mainly correlational; the plausible causal mechanism (task switching) is well supported in lab work, so "keep the phone away while studying" is a sensible low-cost recommendation, but expected gains on exams are small.
- Pomodoro's real evidence is about feeling better and studying efficiently, not scoring higher. No meta-analysis of Pomodoro on exam results was found.
- Friction reliably reduces phone use (one field study, COI); its link to exam scores is an inference, not measured.

### Gaps
- Could not open Kates et al. (2018) itself for the overall pooled r and k; Amez & Baert has no pooled effect (narrative review).
- Böttger et al. (2023) meta-analysis not checked; it conflicts with the direct replications.
- No randomized trial found of site-blocker apps (Freedom, Forest, Cold Turkey) on exam results.

## HAB: Motivation, habits and mindset (gamification, streaks, rewards, habit formation, growth mindset, grit)

### Takeaway
Gamification has several meta-analyses showing small-to-moderate learning gains (g ≈ 0.36–0.49) with quality caveats;
streaks specifically have no learning-outcome research; habit-formation science is real but not tested on exams. Growth
mindset is the most researched idea here and its average effect is tiny and contested; grit adds little beyond
conscientiousness. Grades: **gamification 4, +, B; streaks 1, n/a, D; habit formation 2, n/a, D; growth mindset 5,
± (tiny average; small gains for lower achievers in supportive schools), C (scale gap, see Inferences); grit 4, 0 as a
lever, E.**

### Cited Findings
**Gamification**
- Sailer & Homner (2020; online 2019), "The gamification of learning: a meta-analysis," *Educational Psychology Review* 32, 77–112, DOI 10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w: cognitive g = 0.49 [0.30, 0.69] (k = 19, N = 1,686); motivational g = 0.36 [0.18, 0.54] (k = 16, N = 2,246); behavioural g = 0.25 [0.04, 0.46] (k = 9, N = 951). Cognitive effect held in high-rigour studies; motivational and behavioural effects did not. Game fiction and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes; competition combined with collaboration looked best — [ERIC record via Fachportal](https://www.fachportal-paedagogik.de/literatur/vollanzeige.html?FId=eric_ej1245270). Authors note small samples and no publication-bias assessment (secondary) — [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056).
- Huang, Ritzhaupt, Sommer et al. (2020), *Educational Technology Research and Development* 68(4), 1875–1901, DOI 10.1007/s11423-020-09807-z: g = 0.464 [0.244, 0.684], 30 studies, N = 3,083, formal education; 14 design elements (incl. leaderboards) had differing effects — [ECNU repository](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/the-impact-of-gamification-in-educational-settings-on-student-lea/).
- Bai, Hew & Huang (2020), *Educational Research Review*: g = 0.504 across 30 interventions from 24 studies (secondary) — [UZH reference](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020)).

**Streaks**
- No controlled study isolating streaks' effect on learning was found. Duolingo's own A/B test (reported second-hand) showed streak emphasis raised daily users ~1% and day-14 retention ~3% — engagement, not learning — [Econsultancy](https://econsultancy.com/six-a-b-tests-used-by-duolingo-to-tap-into-habit-forming-behaviour/). A Lund thesis found streak continuance was not driven by perceived learning value — [Lund](https://lup.lub.lu.se/student-papers/record/9238652/file/9238707.pdf).

**Habit formation**
- Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology*, DOI 10.1002/ejsp.674: 96 people, one daily health behaviour for 84 days; median about 66 days to plateau of automaticity, range 18 to 254 days (upper end extrapolated); good model fits for only part of the sample — [UCL news](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [critical read](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit). Not a study of study habits or exam outcomes.

**Growth mindset**
- Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara (2018), *Psychological Science* 29(4), 549–571: Meta-analysis 1 (k = 273, N = 365,915) found a weak mindset–achievement correlation; Meta-analysis 2 of interventions (k = 43, N = 57,155) found d ≈ 0.08, somewhat larger for low-SES / at-risk students — [authors' PDF](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf); [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/should-taxpayers-and-schools-invest-in-growth-mindset-programs/).
- Yeager et al. (2019), *Nature* 573, 364–369, DOI 10.1038/s41586-019-1466-y: national RCT, US 9th graders, under one hour online; raised grades of lower-achieving students (about +0.10 GPA points on a 4-point scale, secondary), larger (+0.15 core, +0.17 STEM) in schools whose peer norms supported challenge; raised advanced-maths enrolment — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v573y2019i7774d10.1038_s41586-019-1466-y.html); [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/national-study-bolsters-case-for-teaching-growth-mindset/2019/08); [ASU](https://news.asu.edu/20190807-solutions-prepping-students-long-term-success).
- Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023), *Psychological Bulletin* 149 (target article DOI 10.1037/bul0000352): 11 pre-registered moderators; 94% of interventions had confounds; authors with a financial incentive were 2.5 times as likely to report positive effects; higher-quality studies less likely to show benefit; conclusion that apparent effects "may be attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias" — [authors' reply, Psych. Bulletin, DOI 10.1037/bul0000394](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/2023-90931-004.pdf).
- Contrary view: Burnette et al. (2023, same journal) found effects ranging from negative to positive, and Tipton et al. (2023) reanalysed the data with multilevel methods and found meaningful effects in focal (at-risk) groups; Macnamara & Burgoyne dispute the reanalysis — [PMC commentary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10495100/); [reply PDF](https://hhs.purdue.edu/skill-learning-and-performance-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2024/08/2023-90931-004.pdf).

**Grit**
- Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017), "Much ado about grit," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 113, 492–511, DOI 10.1037/pspp0000102: 584 effect sizes, 88 samples, N = 66,807; grit only moderately correlated with performance and retention (r ≈ .18 for performance, secondary), very strongly correlated with conscientiousness, higher-order structure not confirmed; interventions may have only weak effects — [Iowa State news](https://news.iastate.edu/news/2016/05/18/grit-analysis); [author PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2016-crede.pdf).

### Inferences
- Gamification's average effects look moderate but come from small, heterogeneous studies without bias checks; the parts most relevant to an exam app (points, streaks) are not separated out. Gains on high-stakes exams are untested.
- Growth mindset: research amount is extensive (5) but the average effect is near zero and its positive subgroup result is contested. The grading scale has no band for "research 4–5, effect ±"; band C ("promising but thin or mixed") fits the effect but understates the research volume. A defensible alternative is E for general students. The report writer should pick one rule and apply it consistently.
- Grit is a measured trait, not an intervention; no evidence that training grit raises exam scores.

### Gaps
- Burnette et al. (2023) pooled figures not checked directly.
- No study of streaks or study-habit apps on exam scores; Lally (2010) is about health behaviours.
- Sailer & Homner's publication-bias and small-sample caveat taken from a repository summary, not the full paper.

## SPC: Study spaces and co-presence (environment, context-dependent memory, body doubling, virtual co-working, study rooms)

### Takeaway
Context-dependent memory is a reliable but modest lab effect that shrinks for recognition tests and when other cues are
strong; it does not justify a dedicated study room for exam gains. Body doubling, "study with me" and paid study rooms
have almost no outcome research. Grades: **context/environment 3, + (small, conditional), C; body doubling / virtual
co-working 1, n/a, D; paid study rooms 0–1, n/a, D.**

### Cited Findings
- Smith & Vela (2001), "Environmental context-dependent memory: A review and meta-analysis," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 8(2), 203–220, DOI 10.3758/BF03196157: effects are reliable overall but modest; reduced by "outshining" and "overshadowing" (strong non-context cues); larger for free recall, smaller for cued recall, smallest for recognition; mentally reinstating the context also helps — [Wikipedia summary citing the paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context-dependent_memory); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03196157). A blog figure of 93 experiments and d ≈ 0.29 for free recall could not be confirmed against the paper.
- Body doubling: a 2025 VR study with 12 adults with ADHD found faster task completion with a human or AI body double and notes prior work found no conclusive effectiveness — [arXiv 2509.12153](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12153). A 117-user survey of virtual body doubling comes from a provider (FLOWN), self-report — [neurodiversity.org](https://neurodiversity.org/?p=11449). A Penn ADHD programme co-director says there does not appear to be extensive research, and ADDA notes the lack of research proving effectiveness — [summary of expert views](https://admin.environment.sa.gov.au/?p=2087); [Smithsonian on virtual co-working](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/can-virtual-coworking-platforms-make-us-more-productive-180984439/).

### Inferences
- For exams, the useful lesson from context research is the opposite of "one sacred room": exam halls differ from study places, so studying in varied places or mentally recalling the study setting may help slightly; this relies on lab word-list studies.
- Co-presence probably works, if at all, through commitment and reduced distraction (overlapping with FOC), but no trial measured exam outcomes.

### Gaps
- Could not check Smith, Glenberg & Bjork (1978, *Memory & Cognition*) on studying in varied rooms (search budget exhausted).
- No research found on paid study rooms (독서실, 自习室, Indian reading rooms) or "study with me" videos and exam results.
- CHADD's own statement on body doubling not opened directly.

## GRP: Study groups and teaching others (cooperative learning, peer tutoring, peer instruction, jigsaw, learning by teaching)

### Takeaway
Structured cooperative learning is among the best-supported ideas in education (classroom effects around 0.4–0.5),
peer instruction shows large concept gains in university physics, and peer tutoring helps both tutors and tutees. Learning
by teaching works but effects are smaller and depend on actually explaining, not just preparing. Grades: **cooperative
learning 5, ++ (if structured), A/B; peer tutoring 4, +, B; peer instruction 4, ++ in STEM concept tests, B; learning by
teaching 3, +, B/C.**

### Cited Findings
- Johnson, Johnson & Stanne (2000), "Cooperative learning methods: A meta-analysis": 164 studies, 194 effect sizes, eight methods all positive vs competitive and individualistic learning; "Learning Together" strongest, Jigsaw lower in the ranking; one secondary abstract gives an overall 0.44 — [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/36554686/Cooperative-Learning-Methods); [EduCaixa summary](https://educaixa.org/es/-/collaborative-learning). Not peer-reviewed journal article (conference / university report), and studies are older.
- Kyndt, Raes, Lismont, Timmers, Cascallar & Dochy (2013), *Educational Research Review* 10, 133–149, DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2013.02.002: 59 (quasi-)experimental classroom studies; achievement ES = 0.54, attitudes ES = 0.15; bigger in science and maths than social sciences/languages (difference g = 0.32); smaller in secondary than primary (−0.20) or university (−0.18); smaller in Western than non-Western cultures (−0.38); group vs individual reward made no difference — [Clearing House Unterricht review](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/CHU_KR4_ENG_Kyndt_2013.pdf); [Antwerp repository](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/link/irua/107136).
- Leung (2015), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 107(2), 558–579, DOI 10.1037/a0037698: peer tutoring meta-analysis (overall figure not confirmed); a later Leung (2019) meta on tutors' own achievement gives weighted mean ES = 0.43 (16 articles); Leung (2016) found quasi-experiments report the largest effects — [EdUHK repository](https://repository.eduhk.hk/en/publications/an-updated-meta-analysis-on-the-effect-of-peer-tutoring-on-tutors/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0143034318808832); [QUB](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/the-influence-of-experimental-design-on-the-magnitude-of-the-effe/).
- Crouch & Mazur (2001), *American Journal of Physics* 69(9), 970–977, DOI 10.1119/1.1374249: ten years of Peer Instruction at Harvard; conceptual and quantitative problem-solving both improved; Force Concept Inventory normalised gain rose from 0.25 (traditional lecture) to 0.49 in the first PI semester and up to 0.74 later (secondary); five different instructors — [Mazur group](https://mazur.harvard.edu/publications/peer-instruction-ten-years-experience-and-results); [Derek Bruff commentary](https://derekbruff.org/2009/10/23/article-crouch-mazur-2001). Single institution, by the method's originator, pre/post comparison.
- Kobayashi (2019), "Learning by preparing-to-teach and teaching: A meta-analysis," *Japanese Psychological Research* 61, 192–203: learners who prepared to teach and actually taught outperformed those who only studied (figures not seen) — [newsletter citing it](https://upkeeping-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/what-you-can-learn-by-teaching).
- Ribosa & Duran (2022), *Educational Research Review* 37: generating teaching materials for others; 23 studies, 62 comparisons; small but significant effect (reported range 0.04 to 0.31); Kobayashi (2023, *Frontiers in Psychology*) argues mixed comparison types mean results need "the greatest caution" — [Frontiers critique](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1095285/full); [UAB record](https://ddd.uab.cat/record/288558).
- Fiorella & Mayer: expecting to teach changes how students study, and actually generating an explanation adds benefit beyond preparing (Fiorella's comments; 2013/2014 papers not opened) — [UGA research news](https://research.uga.edu/news/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/19114).

### Inferences
- Classroom cooperative learning effects are robust but rely on teacher-designed structure (shared goal plus individual accountability). Self-organised study groups or Telegram communities are not what was tested; their effect is unknown.
- For an exam app, "explain to a peer (or an AI) after studying" is the best-supported group mechanism; passive membership of a large community is not.
- Older cooperative-learning studies are pre-1995, lab-heavy and from Johnson & Johnson's own group (developers of the method), a possible allegiance bias.

### Gaps
- Could not verify Cohen, Kulik & Kulik (1982) figures (search budget exhausted).
- Leung (2015) and Kobayashi (2019) pooled effect sizes not seen in primary text.
- No meta-analysis specific to jigsaw was checked; Johnson et al. rank it below other methods.
- No studies found on online/self-organised study communities and exam outcomes.

## CMP: Public comparison and rankings (leaderboards, social comparison, competition, rank feedback)

### Takeaway
Telling students where they stand can raise performance (Azmat & Iriberri 2010: about +5% grades) but other field
experiments find drops in performance after rank feedback, especially for students who learn they are lower than they
thought; class rank itself shapes later confidence and scores. Leaderboards raise time-on-task in some studies but can make
leaders complacent. Grade: **3, ± with wellbeing harms for low-ranked students, band C (leaning E for public rankings of
low performers).**

### Cited Findings
- Azmat & Iriberri (2010), *Journal of Public Economics*: natural experiment in a Spanish high school; for one year students were told whether they were above or below the class average; grades rose about 5% across the distribution, confirmed on externally graded national exams; effect disappeared when the information stopped — [CEP abstract](https://cep.lse.ac.uk/_NEW/PUBLICATIONS/abstract.asp?index=8847); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:qmw:qmwecw:788).
- Azmat, Bagues, Cabrales & Iriberri (2019), *Management Science*: university field experiment giving students their rank; treated students' performance dropped relative to controls, concentrated among students who had **underestimated** their position (so the feedback told them they were doing better than they thought, and they eased off), while overestimators, if anything, responded positively; effects faded by graduation (same GPA and graduation rates) — [field experiment paper](https://www.eco.uc3m.es/~acabrales/research/field_experiment.pdf); [IZA DP 9853](https://repec.iza.org/dp9853.pdf).
- Brade, Himmler & Jäckle: randomized relative-feedback experiment with first-year students; feedback raised performance only when it said the student was above average — [CESifo WP 10346](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_10346.html).
- Goulas & Megalokonomou (2021), "Knowing who you actually are: The effect of feedback on short- and longer-term outcomes," *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 183, 589–615 (Greek students; findings not seen) — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/f/pgo717.html).
- Murphy & Weinhardt (2020), "Top of the Class: The importance of ordinal rank," *Review of Economic Studies* 87(6), 2777–2826, DOI 10.1093/restud/rdaa020: holding ability fixed, a higher class rank in primary school has "large effects on test scores, confidence and subject choice" in secondary school; larger for boys; low-income students gain more from being top — [NBER WP 24958](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24958).
- Landers & Landers (2014), "An empirical test of the theory of gamified learning: The effect of leaderboards on time-on-task and academic performance," *Simulation & Gaming* 45(6), 769–785, DOI 10.1177/1046878114563662: leaderboard increased time-on-task, which in turn related to performance (small online-course sample; details secondary) — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1046878114563662); [author blog](https://neoacademic.com/2015/02/25/examining-evidence-for-leaderboards-and-learning/). A later study found high leaderboard positions led to complacency while low positions spurred effort, and a review warns of side effects of excessive competition — [Emerald, Internet Research](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/intr-12-2021-0897/full/html).

### Inferences
- Ranking feedback has real but two-sided effects: it can motivate some students and lead others to ease off (Azmat 2019: those who learned they were better placed than they thought; Brade et al.: only "above average" feedback helped). Results are from a handful of field studies in Spain, Germany, Greece and England, not meta-analysed.
- Murphy & Weinhardt imply that being low in a visible ranking may itself lower confidence and later scores; this is a harm signal for public all-India-style ranks shown to everyone.
- Private, self-referenced progress is the safer design; public leaderboards are better framed as optional and team-based (Sailer & Homner found competition paired with collaboration worked best).

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of rank feedback or leaderboards on exam scores was found; leaderboard-specific results from Huang et al. (2020) were not visible in the abstract.
- Social-comparison and wellbeing evidence (anxiety, self-esteem) could not be searched after the budget ran out; harm is inferred from performance studies, not measured wellbeing outcomes.
- The direction wording of Azmat et al. (2019) should be checked against the paper.

## Grades summary (per grading_scale.md)

### Takeaway
Best supported in this group: structured cooperative learning and peer explanation (A/B). Moderately supported: keeping
phones away and gamification (B, small effects). Mixed or thin: Pomodoro, friction apps, growth mindset, context, rank
feedback (C). Little or no research: streaks, body doubling, study rooms (D). Grit as a lever: E.

### Inferences
- The scale lacks a band for "much research, mixed/near-zero effect" (growth mindset, rank feedback). Suggest the final report treat these as C with a note, or define E to include "researched, average benefit negligible."
- Several FOC and HAB ideas are popular in exam culture (Pomodoro, streaks, study rooms, body doubling) but their evidence is about mood, engagement or app use, not exam scores.

### Gaps
- Searches not run because the budget was exhausted: Cohen, Kulik & Kulik (1982); Smith, Glenberg & Bjork (1978); Böttger et al. (2023); Burnette et al. (2023); social-comparison wellbeing research; leaderboard-specific metas. A follow-up pass could close these.

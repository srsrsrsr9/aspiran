## a. Thacher (2008), all-nighters and university GPA

**Citation:** Thacher, P. V. (2008). University students and “the all nighter”: Correlates and patterns of students’ engagement in a single night of total sleep deprivation. *Behavioral Sleep Medicine, 6*(1), 16–31.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/15402000701796114

**Design:** Cross-sectional self-report survey with registrar-obtained GPA.  
**k / N:** Single study; **N = 120** university students at a 4-year liberal arts college.  
**Main result:** 60% reported at least one all-nighter since starting college. All-nighter engagement was associated with later bedtimes, evening preference, and **poorer academic achievement as measured by GPA**. The abstract does not report a numeric correlation or effect size.  
**Caveat:** Cross-sectional and self-report; cannot establish that all-nighters caused lower GPA. Single institution, and effect size not reported in the abstract.  
**Status:** Confirmed citation and design; numeric effect size not confirmed.

## b. Meta-analysis of caffeine on memory or learning in students

**Exact item: not found.** No meta-analysis specifically testing caffeine on **memory or learning in students** with a poolable memory/learning outcome was located.

**Closest linked meta-analysis, but it is not memory-specific:**  
Niu, J., Li, H., Wang, Z., Xu, L., Liu, L., Liu, J., Li, J., & Cao, Y. (2026). Acute caffeine intervention and cognitive performance in populations aged 20 years and younger: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Frontiers in Nutrition*.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2026.1888658

**Design:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of acute caffeine intervention studies.  
**k / N:** **k = 14 studies**, **N = 250** participants aged ≤20.  
**Main result:** Acute caffeine had a **small positive effect on broad cognitive task performance** versus placebo: **SMD = 0.41, 95% CI 0.28–0.55**. This is not a memory- or exam-specific effect.  
**Caveat:** Small N; broad cognitive outcome; population aged ≤20, not university students generally; no exam-score outcome.  
**Status:** Exact memory/learning-in-students meta-analysis **not found**; closest meta-analysis confirmed but mismatched outcome.

A narrative review often cited for caffeine and memory is:  
**Citation:** Lorenzo Calvo, J., Fei, X., Domínguez, R., & Pareja-Galeano, H. (2021). Caffeine and cognitive functions in sports: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Nutrients, 13*(3), 868.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13030868  
But this is about sports/cognition and is not a student memory/learning meta-analysis.

## c. Hydration / drinking water and exam performance

**Citation:** Pawson, C., Gardner, M. R., Doherty, S., Martin, L., Soares, R., & Edmonds, C. J. (2013). Drink availability is associated with enhanced examination performance in adults. *Psychology Teaching Review, 19*(1), 57–66.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.53841/bpsptr.2013.19.1.57

**Design:** Quasi-experimental / observational comparison of exam performance between students who brought a drink into exams and those who did not, with coursework marks used as a covariate for prior ability. Three undergraduate cohorts.  
**k / N:** Single study; **N = 447** undergraduates.  
**Main result:** Students who brought water/drinks achieved higher exam marks. Examples: foundation-level MCQ difference about **12%, Cohen’s d = 0.82**; first-year MCQ difference about **7%, d = 0.51**; ANCOVA-adjusted mean difference at foundation level **10.6%, partial η² = 0.135**.  
**Caveat:** Not randomized; students self-selected whether to bring a drink; no direct hydration biomarker; correlational rather than causal. The authors themselves call for further research.  
**Status:** Confirmed, with caveats.

A randomized crossover trial in Malian schoolchildren found **no statistically significant** cognitive-test benefit of supplementary water, with results masked by practice effects:  
Chard, A. N., Trinies, V., Edmonds, C. J., Sogore, A., & Freeman, M. C. (2019). The impact of water consumption on hydration and cognition among schoolchildren: Methods and results from a crossover trial in rural Mali. *PLoS ONE, 14*(1), e0210568.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0210568  
But this measured cognitive tests, not exam performance.

## d. Curcio, Ferrara & De Gennaro (2006), sleep loss and learning

**Citation:** Curcio, G., Ferrara, M., & De Gennaro, L. (2006). Sleep loss, learning capacity and academic performance. *Sleep Medicine Reviews, 10*(5), 323–337.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2005.11.001

**Design:** Review of sleep-loss studies across educational levels, including manipulated sleep and neurocognitive/behavioral outcomes.  
**k / N:** Not a meta-analysis; no pooled **k** or **N** in the abstract.  
**Main result:** The review reports that sleep loss is associated with impaired learning, memory capacity, and school performance, and that sleep manipulation studies support neurocognitive and behavioral consequences. It does **not** provide a single pooled effect size.  
**Caveat:** Narrative review, not a meta-analysis; heterogeneous designs and outcomes; no single quantitative estimate.  
**Status:** Confirmed citation and main conclusion; k/N/effect size not pooled/confirmed.

## e. Donnelly et al. (2016), physical activity and academic achievement position stand

**Citation:** Donnelly, J. E., Hillman, C. H., Castelli, D., Etnier, J. L., Lee, S., Tomporowski, P., Lambourne, K., & Szabo-Reed, A. N. (2016). Physical activity, fitness, cognitive function, and academic achievement in children: A systematic review. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise, 48*(6), 1197–1222.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000901

**Design:** Systematic review / position stand, grouping studies as cross-sectional, longitudinal, acute, or intervention trials.  
**k / N:** **64 studies** met inclusion criteria for cognition/learning/brain outcomes; **73 studies** met criteria for academic achievement/concentration outcomes. Participant **N was not pooled**.  
**Main conclusions:**  
- A majority of research supports benefits of physical fitness, single bouts of physical activity, and physical-activity interventions on children’s **cognitive functioning**.  
- Evidence for physical activity effects on **learning** was limited: only one cross-sectional study met criteria.  
- Cross-sectional and longitudinal studies often showed favorable academic-achievement results, but **controlled experimental evidence was mixed**, and better-designed studies were needed.  
**Caveat:** Considerable heterogeneity; lack of RCTs; many studies high risk of bias; limited blinding; lack of adequately powered trials.  
**Status:** Confirmed.

## f. Meta-analysis of reappraisal / “reframing nerves as excitement” on test performance, 2018 or later

**Citation:** Bosshard, M., & Gomez, P. (2024). Effectiveness of stress arousal reappraisal and stress-is-enhancing mindset interventions on task performance outcomes: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. *Scientific Reports, 14*, 7923.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-58408-w

**Design:** Meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.  
**k / N:** **k = 35 studies**, **44 effect sizes**. Total participant **N was not reported in the accessible abstract/HTML**; therefore N is unconfirmed here.  
**Main result:** Overall small significant improvement in task performance: **d = 0.23, p < 0.001**. After trim-and-fill correction for publication bias, **d = 0.14, 95% CI 0.04–0.24**. Subgroups: mixed interventions **d = 0.45**; stress-arousal reappraisal alone **d = 0.22**; stress-is-enhancing mindset alone **d = 0.18** (only SAR significant).  
**Who / outcome:** Participants in varied performance domains, including cognitive/written tasks such as academic exams and public-performance tasks.  
**Caveat:** Moderate heterogeneity; clear funnel-plot asymmetry and evidence of publication bias; trim-and-fill reduces the pooled effect; not exclusively test/exam performance.  
**Status:** Confirmed citation, k, and effect sizes; total N unconfirmed.

A relevant primary RCT—not a meta-analysis—is Brady et al. (2018), which found reappraising test anxiety improved first-year college students’ exam performance:  
**Citation:** Brady, S. T., Hard, B. M., & Gross, J. J. (2018). Reappraising test anxiety increases academic performance of first-year college students. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 110*(3), 395–406.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000219

## g. Trial of a CBT or breathing-exercise app for test anxiety that also measured exam scores

**Exact item: not found.** No trial was located that both (a) tested a CBT or breathing-exercise **app/device** for test anxiety and (b) reported **exam scores** as an outcome.

**Closest linked trial, but it measured self-reported test anxiety, not exam scores:**  
Rosenberg, A., et al. (2021). Reducing test anxiety and related symptoms using a biofeedback respiratory practice device: A randomized control trial. *Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback, 46*(1), 69–82.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-020-09494-9  
**Design:** RCT; **N = 34** students assigned to biofeedback device, self-directed breathing exercise, or psychoeducation.  
**Main result:** Only the biofeedback device group showed significant reductions in self-reported test anxiety; no exam-score outcome was reported.  
**Caveat:** Very small N; no exam-score outcome; not an “app” in the smartphone sense.

A device-guided breathing pilot is also linked, but again without exam-score outcomes:  
Ovadia-Blechman, Z., Tarrasch, R., Velicki, M., & Chalutz Ben-Gal, H. (2022). Reducing test anxiety by device-guided breathing: A pilot study. *Frontiers in Psychology, 13*, 678098.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.678098  
**Status:** Exact item **not found**.

## h. Callender & McDaniel (2009), the limited benefits of rereading

**Citation:** Callender, A. A., & McDaniel, M. A. (2009). The limited benefits of rereading educational texts. *Contemporary Educational Psychology, 34*(1), 30–41.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2008.07.001

**Design:** Review of research on rereading educational texts; not a new primary experiment in the citation itself.  
**k / N:** Not pooled in the accessible abstract/record.  
**Main result:** The paper concludes that rereading produces **limited benefits** for learning from educational texts and should not be assumed to be an effective durable-learning strategy. It does not report a single pooled effect size in the accessible metadata.  
**Caveat:** Review, not meta-analysis; no single quantitative estimate available in the accessible record.  
**Status:** Confirmed citation and qualitative conclusion; k/N/effect size not confirmed.

## i. Cuevas (2015), “Is learning styles-based instruction effective?”

**Citation:** Cuevas, J. (2015). Is learning styles-based instruction effective? A comprehensive analysis of recent research on learning styles. *Theory and Research in Education, 13*(3), 308–333.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1477878515606621

**Design:** Comprehensive review of recent research testing the learning-styles matching hypothesis.  
**k / N:** The paper is a review; a secondary summary states it reviewed **13 studies** explicitly testing the matching hypothesis. Participant **N is not pooled** in the accessible record.  
**Main result:** The review concludes that the majority of research evidence does **not** support learning-styles-based instruction as benefiting student learning; matching instruction to style is not supported.  
**Caveat:** Review rather than meta-analysis; no pooled effect size; “learning styles” is a heterogeneous construct.  
**Status:** Confirmed citation and conclusion; k/N and effect size partly confirmed.

A linked primary test is:  
Cuevas, J., & Dawson, B. L. (2018). A test of two alternative cognitive processing models: Learning styles and dual coding. *Theory and Research in Education, 16*(1), 40–64.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1477878517731450  
**N = 204** university students; **no significant learning-style × condition interaction**; the basic learning-styles prediction was rejected.

## j. Fawcett (2013), production effect meta-analysis

**Citation:** Fawcett, J. M. (2013). The production effect benefits performance in between-subject designs: A meta-analysis. *Acta Psychologica, 142*(1), 1–5.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2012.10.001

**Design:** Meta-analysis of between-subject production-effect experiments.  
**k / N:** Not reported in the accessible abstract/record.  
**Main result:** Reading aloud produces a **small but consistent memory advantage** over silent reading in between-subject designs; the effect is reliable for recognition memory. The accessible abstract does not give an exact **g** value.  
**Caveat:** Laboratory memory studies, not exam performance; between-subject designs only; exact pooled effect size not confirmed here.  
**Status:** Confirmed citation and direction; k/N/exact g not confirmed.

A later meta-analysis reports that the between-subject production effect is small and consistent, but its numeric **g** was not verified from the accessible record:  
Bailey, L. M., et al. (2022). Production improves recognition and reduces intrusions in between-subject designs: An updated meta-analysis. *Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology*.  
**DOI:** not captured in the accessible record; therefore **unconfirmed**.

## k. Meta-analysis of non-prescription nootropics (Bacopa/Brahmi, ginkgo) on memory in healthy young adults

**Citation:** Comparative effects of *Bacopa monnieri* and *Ginkgo biloba* on cognitive functions: A systematic review and network meta-analysis. *Phytomedicine* (2026).  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.phymed.2026.157915  
**Author list:** Not displayed in the accessible Europe PMC record; author metadata unconfirmed here.

**Design:** Systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials in healthy adults.  
**k / N:** **k = 29 RCTs**, **N = 2,107** healthy adults.  
**Main result:** High-dose Brahmi (≥600 mg/day) significantly improved **working memory** versus placebo: **SMD = 2.03, 95% CI 1.28–2.78**. It also improved short-term memory. Low-dose Brahmi improved delayed memory versus low-dose ginkgo and placebo. No significant differences for sustained attention, selective attention, or processing speed.  
**Caveat:** No direct head-to-head Brahmi-vs-ginkgo comparisons in the network; heterogeneous preparations/doses; not an exam-performance outcome; healthy adults rather than students specifically; author list unconfirmed in the accessible record.  
**Status:** Confirmed DOI, design, k, N, and effect sizes; author metadata partly unconfirmed.

A Bacopa-specific meta-analysis is:  
Kongkeaw, C., Dilokthornsakul, P., Thanarangsarit, P., Limpeanchob, N., & Norman Scholfield, C. (2014). Meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials on cognitive effects of *Bacopa monnieri* extract. *Journal of Ethnopharmacology, 151*(1), 528–535.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jep.2013.11.008  
It reported **k = 9** trials and **N = 437**, with the strongest evidence for **speed of attention** rather than broad memory improvement.

## l. Photo-solver apps (Photomath, Gauthmath, Zuoyebang, QANDA, etc.) and later exam scores

**Exact item: not found.** No study was located that credibly estimates the effect of photo-solver-app use on **later exam scores** with a confirmable design and DOI.

**Closest linked study, but it measured answer submission speed, not exam scores:**  
Sloan-Lynch, J., Gay, N., & Watkins, R. (2022). Too fast for their own good: Analyzing a decade of student exercise responses to explore the impact of math solving photo apps. *LAK22: 12th International Learning Analytics and Knowledge Conference*.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3506860.3506868  
**Design:** Large-scale exploratory analysis of math-exercise response times.  
**Data:** **700 unique math exercises** and **over 82 million student submissions**.  
**Main result:** Submission speeds shifted dramatically with the proliferation of math-solving photo apps, consistent with increased reliance on these tools on homework/exams. **No later exam-score outcome was reported.**  
**Caveat:** Response-time proxy, not exam achievement; cannot establish learning or later exam effects.

A preliminary Photomath algebra study reports a **36.25% improvement in algebra test scores**, but its design, sample size, and whether it measured later exam scores could not be verified:  
“Photomath application for learning algebra: Preliminary study on a school in border area” (2025). *Indonesian Journal for the Study of Mathematics and Education*.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.24042/ijsme.v8i1.24297  
**Status:** Exact item **not found**; closest studies are indirect and do not answer the later-exam-score question.

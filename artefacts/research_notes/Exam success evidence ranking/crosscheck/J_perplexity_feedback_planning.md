## a. Samson (1985)

- **Citation:** Samson, G. E. (1985). *Effects of training in test-taking skills on achievement test performance: A quantitative synthesis.* **Journal of Educational Research, 78**(5), 261–266.
- **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1080/00220671.1985.10885613](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220671.1985.10885613)
- **Design:** Meta-analysis.
- **Size:** **k = 24 studies**; participant N not stated in the abstract/source excerpts I could verify.
- **Main effect:** Mean study-weighted effect size **ES = .33** (Glass-type standardized mean difference). This corresponds to moving an average control-group score from the 50th to about the 63rd percentile.
- **Population/outcome:** Elementary and secondary students; achievement-test performance.
- **Caveat:** The paper reports that programmes lasting **five weeks or longer** produced significantly larger gains than shorter programmes. A later reanalysis using slightly different inclusion criteria obtained **ES = .34**, and noted that Samson appears to have included delayed-test and aptitude-measure outcomes.[^1_1][^1_2]


## b. Other test-wiseness / test-taking-skills meta-analysis

**Not found** within the evidence I could verify and link here. The reanalysis discussed above is:

- **Link:** [https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED339743.pdf](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED339743.pdf)
- **Caveat:** This is a reanalysis/report document rather than a cleanly identified standalone journal meta-analysis; its inclusion criteria differed from Samson’s.[^1_2]


## c. Ma, Adesope, Nesbit \& Liu (2014)

- **Citation:** Ma, W., Adesope, O. O., Nesbit, J. C., \& Liu, Q. (2014). *Intelligent tutoring systems and learning outcomes: A meta-analysis.* **Journal of Educational Psychology, 106**(4), 901–918.
- **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037123](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037123)
- **Design:** Meta-analysis.
- **Size:** **107 effect sizes**; **N = 14,321 participants**.
- **Main effects:** There is **no single unconditional overall g** in the verified abstract. The key comparison-specific estimates are:
    - vs. teacher-led large-group instruction: **g = .42**
    - vs. non-ITS computer-based instruction: **g = .57**
    - vs. textbooks/workbooks: **g = .35**
    - vs. small-group instruction: **g = .05** (not significant)
    - vs. individual human tutoring: **g = −.11** (not significant)
    - APA’s summary reports **g = 1.23** versus no instruction and **g = .41** across the 107 results where the comparison condition received instruction.
- **Population/outcome:** Learners from elementary through postsecondary education; achievement/learning outcomes, including standardized and researcher-developed tests.
- **Caveat:** Effects depend heavily on the comparison condition; ITS does not outperform one-to-one human tutoring.[^1_3][^1_4]


## d. Kulik, Kulik \& Bangert-Drowns (1990)

- **Citation:** Kulik, C.-L. C., Kulik, J. A., \& Bangert-Drowns, R. L. (1990). *Effectiveness of mastery learning programs: A meta-analysis.* **Review of Educational Research, 60**(2), 265–299.
- **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.2307/1170612](https://doi.org/10.2307/1170612)
- **Design:** Meta-analysis of controlled evaluations.
- **Size:** **k = 108 controlled evaluations**; **103 reported end-of-instruction examination results**. Overall participant N not stated in the accessible source.
- **Main effect:** Mean effect size **d = .52** for examination performance across the 103 examination studies; **SE = .033**. No confidence interval was stated in the accessible text.
- **Population/outcome:** Students in colleges, high schools, and upper elementary grades; end-of-instruction examinations.
- **Caveat:** Effects varied by mastery procedure, study design, and course content; effects were larger for less-able students (**M = .61**) than more-able students (**M = .40**) in the 13 studies reporting ability subgroup results. A later critique argues the overall estimate is heavily influenced by experimenter-made rather than standardized tests.[^1_5][^1_6]


## e. Hattie \& Timperley (2007)

- **Citation:** Hattie, J., \& Timperley, H. (2007). *The power of feedback.* **Review of Educational Research, 77**(1), 81–112.
- **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487)
- **Design:** Research review / meta-synthesis, not a conventional random-effects meta-analysis with a clearly reported k and N in the accessible source.
- **Size:** **Not found** in the sources I could verify.
- **Main effect:** It reports an average feedback effect of **d = .79**.
- **Population/outcome:** Educational settings and student learning outcomes; the paper’s model distinguishes feedback at task, process, self-regulation, and self levels.
- **Caveat:** Later work explicitly notes that this **.79** is much higher than a subsequent random-effects meta-analysis estimate of **d = .48** after removing extreme values (**d = .55** before removal; 435 studies, 994 effect sizes, about 61,000 participants; 17% of effects negative). Feedback effects are highly heterogeneous and should not be treated as one uniform intervention.[^1_7][^1_8]

## h. Richardson, Abraham \& Bond (2012)

- **Citation:** Richardson, M., Abraham, C., \& Bond, R. (2012). *Psychological correlates of university students’ academic performance: A systematic review and meta-analysis.* **Psychological Bulletin, 138**(2), 353–387.
- **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026838](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026838)
- **Design:** Systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of correlations.
- **Size:** **241 data sets**, **1,105 independent correlations**, covering **50 conceptually distinct correlates**; overall participant N not stated in the accessible abstract.
- **Main correlations with university GPA** reported in a later summary of the same meta-analysis:
    - Performance self-efficacy: strongest correlate; exact r **not found** in the accessible source.
    - High-school GPA, ACT, SAT/A-level scores, academic self-efficacy, grade goal, and effort regulation: medium-sized correlations; exact values for most were not available in the verified excerpts.
    - Effort regulation: **r = .32**.
    - Time/study management: **r = .22**.
    - Metacognition: **r = .18**.
    - Elaboration: **r = .18**.
    - Concentration: **r = .16**.
    - Critical thinking: **r = .15**.
    - Help seeking: **r = .15**.
    - Peer learning: **r = .13**.
    - Organisation and rehearsal: not significantly associated with GPA.
- **Population/outcome:** University students; cumulative GPA.
- **Caveat:** These are **correlational** estimates, not causal intervention effects.[^1_9][^1_10]


## i. Donker et al. (2014)

- **Citation:** Donker, A. S., de Boer, H., Kostons, D., Dignath van Ewijk, C. C., \& van der Werf, M. P. C. (2014). *Effectiveness of learning strategy instruction on academic performance: A meta-analysis.* **Educational Research Review, 11**, 1–26.
- **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2013.11.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2013.11.002)
- **Design:** Meta-analysis of intervention studies.
- **Size:** **58 studies**, **95 interventions**, **180 effect sizes**; overall participant N not stated in the accessible source.
- **Main effect:** Mean weighted **Hedges’ g = .66**; **SE = .05**; **95% CI .56–.76**.
- **Population/outcome:** Primary and secondary students; academic performance. Domain results included writing **g = 1.25**, science **g = .73**, mathematics **g = .66**, and reading **g = .36**.
- **Caveat:** Effects varied substantially by domain and by intervention features; interventions that included general metacognitive knowledge, planning instruction, and task-value components were reported as more effective.[^1_11][^1_12]

## l. EEF: Metacognition and self-regulation

- **Source:** Education Endowment Foundation, *Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Metacognition and self-regulation*, updated 21 May 2025.
- **Link:** [https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation)
- **Headline impact:** **+8 months’ additional progress** over a school year, on average.
- **Evidence base:** The EEF describes this as based on **355 individual studies** in its Toolkit synthesis.
- **Population/outcome:** School pupils; attainment across literacy, mathematics, and science, in primary and secondary settings.
- **Caveat:** The EEF states that the impact can be difficult to realise in practice and that effective approaches embed metacognitive strategies in challenging curriculum content rather than teaching generic study tips in isolation. The estimate rose from **+7 months** in the prior version to **+8 months** after the 2025 update.[^1_13][^1_14][^1_15][^1_16]

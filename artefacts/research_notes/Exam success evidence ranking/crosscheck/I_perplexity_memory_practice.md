# I am checking the research evidence for study techniques (any exam, any country). For each item

Below is what I could verify from accessible primary sources. Where an exact figure was not visible in a retrievable source, I have marked it “not verified” rather than inferring it.

## a. Agarwal, Nunes \& Blunt (2021)

1. **Citation:** Agarwal, P. K., Nunes, L. D., \& Blunt, J. R. (2021). Retrieval practice consistently benefits student learning: A systematic review of applied research in schools and classrooms. *Educational Psychology Review, 33*(4), 1409–1453.[^1_1]
2. **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9)[^1_1]
3. **Design:** Systematic review of applied classroom/school experiments; not a formal pooled meta-analysis.[^1_1]
4. **Size:** 50 experiments across 37 publications; 49 effect sizes; total $N = 5{,}374$.[^1_2][^1_1]
5. **Main effect:** No single pooled effect size is reported in the accessible abstract; 57% of the 49 effect sizes showed medium or large benefits of retrieval practice.[^1_1]
6. **Who/outcome:** Students from K-12 through medical school, using real course material; outcomes were course-related tests, with varying final-test delays.[^1_1]
7. **Caveat:** Only 6% of experiments were conducted in non-WEIRD countries, limiting generalisability.[^1_1]

## b. Adesope, Trevisan \& Sundararajan (2017)

1. **Citation:** Adesope, O. O., Trevisan, D. A., \& Sundararajan, N. (2017). Rethinking the use of tests: A meta-analysis of practice testing. *Review of Educational Research, 87*(3), 659–701.[^1_3]
2. **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306)[^1_3]
3. **Design:** Meta-analysis of practice-testing versus non-testing conditions.[^1_4]
4. **Size:** The exact overall $k$ and total $N$ were not verifiable from the accessible records retrieved here.
5. **Main effect:** The accessible summary reports $g = 0.51$ versus restudying and $g = 0.93$ versus filler/no-activity comparisons; the single overall pooled $g$ and its CI were not verifiable here.[^1_5]
6. **Who/outcome:** Students across educational levels; outcomes included learning and retention measures, with moderators including test format, participants, outcomes, and methodology.[^1_4]
7. **Caveat:** The authors report that effects were moderated by practice-test features and methodological characteristics; no publication-bias result was verifiable from the retrieved material.[^1_4]

## c. Latimier, Peyre \& Ramus (2021)

1. **Citation:** Latimier, A., Peyre, H., \& Ramus, F. (2021). A meta-analytic review of the benefit of spacing out retrieval practice episodes on retention. *Educational Psychology Review, 33*(3), 959–987.[^1_6]
2. **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)[^1_6]
3. **Design:** Meta-analysis.[^1_6]
4. **Size:** 29 studies; 39 effect sizes for spaced versus massed retrieval and 54 for expanding versus uniform gaps. Total participant $N$: not verified here.[^1_6]
5. **Main effect:** Spaced versus massed retrieval: $g = 0.74$. Expanding versus equal gaps: $g = 0.034$, not significant.[^1_6]
6. **Who/outcome:** Learners tested on retention after spaced versus massed retrieval practice; the retrieved summary does not specify the full range of educational levels or delay distribution.[^1_6]
7. **Caveat:** The key practical caveat is that expanding retrieval gaps did not outperform equal gaps in this synthesis.[^1_6]

## d. Pretesting meta-analysis

1. **Citation:** Chan, J. C. K., Meissner, C. A., \& Davis, S. D. (2018). Retrieval potentiates new learning: A theoretical and meta-analytic review. *Psychological Bulletin, 144*(11), 1111–1146.[^1_7]
2. **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000166](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000166)[^1_7]
3. **Design:** Theoretical and meta-analytic review of pretesting/prequestions and subsequent learning.[^1_7]
4. **Size:** $k = 192$ effect sizes in the overall dataset; total $N$: not verified here.[^1_8]
5. **Main effect:** Overall random-effects $d = 0.40$, 95% CI $[0.31, 0.50]$.[^1_8]
6. **Who/outcome:** Mainly laboratory/experimental learning studies; outcome was later recall or learning of new material after an initial test or prequestion phase.[^1_8][^1_7]
7. **Caveat:** A later correction to the article is indexed by APA, so the published correction should be checked when quoting precise figures. A separate 2023 preregistered meta-analysis found a large *specific* prequestion effect—benefit for pretested content—but almost no *general* transfer effect: $g = 0.54$, 95% CI $[0.42, 0.66]$, $k = 97$, versus $g = 0.04$, 95% CI $[-0.04, 0.11]$, $k = 91$.[^1_9][^1_10]

## e. Bertsch et al. (2007)

1. **Citation:** Bertsch, S., Pesta, B. J., Wiscott, R., \& McDaniel, M. A. (2007). The generation effect: A meta-analytic review. *Memory \& Cognition, 35*(2), 201–210.[^1_11]
2. **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441)[^1_11]
3. **Design:** Meta-analysis.[^1_12]
4. **Size:** 86 studies and 445 effect sizes.[^1_12]
5. **Main effect:** Overall $d = 0.40$ for generated versus read material.[^1_12]
6. **Who/outcome:** Predominantly laboratory memory studies; outcome was memory for generated versus read information.[^1_12]
7. **Caveat:** The authors report substantial variability across moderators; effects were larger in within-subject designs ($d = 0.50$) than between-subject designs ($d = 0.28$), and larger for incidental than intentional learning.[^1_13][^1_14]

## f. Keyword mnemonic meta-analysis

1. **Citation:** Donoghue, G. M., \& Hattie, J. A. C. (2021). A meta-analysis of ten learning techniques. *Frontiers in Education, 6*, 581216.[^1_15]
2. **Link:** [https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216)[^1_15]
3. **Design:** Meta-analysis of learning techniques, including keyword mnemonics.[^1_15]
4. **Size:** For mnemonics: 107 cases/effects and 580 unique participants.[^1_15]
5. **Main effect:** $d = 0.50$ for mnemonics; $SE = 0.104$; $I^2 = 89\%$.[^1_15]
6. **Who/outcome:** The retrieved table does not break the mnemonic subset down by school versus university or by delay; outcomes were learning-strategy study outcomes pooled across the included cases.[^1_15]
7. **Caveat:** Very high heterogeneity ($I^2 = 89\%$) means the pooled $d = 0.50$ should not be treated as a stable, one-size-fits-all estimate.[^1_15]

## g. Spaced-repetition app and high-stakes exam

**Not found.** I could not verify a randomised trial of Anki, Quizlet, Duolingo, or a comparable app that used a real high-stakes examination score as its primary outcome. A 2026 systematic review found mostly small controlled studies, observational/self-directed app-use studies, and mixed findings; for example, an Anki cohort study found no overall benefit for test-score improvement, while a retrospective study found flashcard review predicted USMLE Step 1 score. That evidence base does not meet the requested randomised, high-stakes-exam standard.[^1_16]

## h. Replication of Rohrer et al. (2020)

1. **Citation:** Sana, F., et al. (2022). Interleaving retrieval practice promotes science learning. *Journal of Educational Psychology*. The exact author list, volume, and pages were not verifiable from the accessible record retrieved here.[^1_17]
2. **Link:** [https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2022-sana.pdf](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2022-sana.pdf)[^1_17]
3. **Design:** Classroom quasi-experimental/within-course study; a conceptual replication and extension of interleaving, not a direct replication of Rohrer et al.’s mathematics trial.[^1_17]
4. **Size:** $N = 155$ students in grades 9–12.[^1_17]
5. **Main effect:** Interleaved quizzes beat blocked quizzes, $d = 0.35$; interleaved quizzes beat no-quiz control with $d = 0.71$ on the final test.[^1_17]
6. **Who/outcome:** Secondary-school science students; outcome was a test one month after the final quiz.[^1_17]
7. **Caveat:** This study changed both subject matter and the manipulation—interleaved *retrieval practice* rather than Rohrer et al.’s interleaved mathematics worksheets—so it supports the mechanism but is not a direct replication.[^1_17]

## i. Kang (2016)

**Not fully verified.** Kang’s “Spaced repetition promotes efficient and effective learning” is best treated as a narrative/policy-oriented review rather than a meta-analysis or randomised trial. I could not retrieve a source in this session that verified its full journal citation, volume/pages, DOI, or any pooled effect size, so I am not reporting numbers for it.

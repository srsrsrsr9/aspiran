## LIF: Body basics (sleep, naps, all-nighters, exercise, breakfast, hydration, caffeine)

### Takeaway
Sleep has the strongest body-basics evidence: experiments show a week of short sleep steadily damages attention and
memory, and real-course studies link better sleep over the weeks before a test with better grades. Sleep the night before
alone mattered less. Exercise helps maths and does not harm grades, but study quality is low. Breakfast and caffeine
evidence is mostly short-term lab cognition, not exam scores.

### Cited Findings
**Sleep and exam results**
- Okano, Kaczmarzyk, Dave, Gabrieli & Grossman (2019), *npj Science of Learning* 4:16. 88 MIT intro-chemistry students
  wore activity trackers for a term. Better sleep quality, longer sleep and more consistent sleep went with better quiz
  and exam grades, and sleep measures explained nearly 25% of the variance in performance. Sleep the night before a test
  was not related to that test's score; sleep in the week and month before was. Students going to bed after the median
  bedtime (1:47 am) scored lower. Correlational, one course. — [DOI 10.1038/s41539-019-0055-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-019-0055-z); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/127257)
- Dewald, Meijer, Oort, Kerkhof & Bögels (2010), *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 14(3):179–189. Three meta-analyses of children
  and adolescents: sleep quality (k = 16, N = 13,631), sleep duration (k = 17, N = 15,199), sleepiness (k = 17, N = 19,530).
  Each was linked to school performance with small but significant effects; sleepiness was the strongest correlate, then
  quality, then duration. Effects were larger in younger participants. Exact pooled r values were not found in my search. — [UvA repository](https://dare.uva.nl/id/69e7fde3-de6d-4f5f-b3c7-6b1a45988314); secondary summary [Frontiers in Psychology 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00038/full)
- Lo et al. (2016), *SLEEP* ("Need for Sleep" study). 56 Singapore students aged 15–19 from top schools, randomised to
  7 nights of 5 h in bed vs 9 h. The 5 h group showed cumulative decline in sustained attention, working memory,
  executive function and processing speed; effects on sustained attention and sleepiness persisted after 2 nights of
  recovery sleep. The 9 h group held steady or improved. Small, elite sample, severe restriction. — [PMC4763362](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4763362); [commentary PMC4763363](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4763363)

**Naps**
- Leong, Lo & Chee (2022), *Sleep Medicine Reviews* 65:101666, meta-analysis of 60 experimental studies: afternoon naps
  gave significant benefits, strongest for vigilance and memory, consistent in teens and young to middle-aged adults.
  Effect sizes were not in what I found. — [NUS Medicine summary](https://medicine.nus.edu.sg/newsletters/issue45/science-of-life/how-naps-can-be-beneficial-for-you)
- A 2025 Chinese meta-analysis (42 studies, k = 130) found a moderate positive effect of an afternoon nap on cognitive
  function, g = 0.59 (general cognition, not exam scores). — [Psychological Development and Education 41(2):173–184](https://devpsy.bnu.edu.cn/CN/Y2025/V41/I2/173)

**Exercise**
- Singh et al. (2019), *British Journal of Sports Medicine* 53:640–647. Systematic review of 58 intervention studies in
  children and adolescents plus a Delphi expert panel. 15 of 25 academic-performance analyses found a significant benefit;
  only 11 of 58 studies (19%) were rated high quality. Panel: strong evidence for benefit on maths; evidence for overall
  academic and cognitive performance inconclusive. — [DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2017-098136](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2017-098136); [Amsterdam UMC](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/en/publications/effects-of-physical-activity-interventions-on-cognitive-and-acade/)
- Donnelly et al. 2016 was not reached in my searches (see Gaps).

**Breakfast**
- Adolphus, Lawton, Champ & Dye (2016), *Advances in Nutrition* 7(3):590S–612S. Systematic review of 45 studies (43
  articles) on breakfast and cognition in children and adolescents; 34 tested a single breakfast's acute effect. Findings
  matched their 2009 review with more evidence, but the authors flag few adolescent studies, few natural settings, small
  samples and insensitive tests. — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/100495)
- Their earlier review on academic performance (Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2013) reported that habitual breakfast is
  positively related to academic performance, but few good-quality studies existed, so the effect cannot be attributed
  directly to breakfast. (Reported via search summary; full text not opened.) — [Adolphus group, White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/100495)

**Caffeine**
- Borota et al. (2014), *Nature Neuroscience* 17(2):201–203. Caffeine-naive adults took 200 mg caffeine or placebo
  5 minutes after studying images. Next day, the caffeine group was better at telling similar new images from old ones
  (inverted-U dose curve: 200 mg beat 100 mg; 300 mg no better). No benefit when taken just before the test. The difference
  was modest (reported t42 = 2.0, p ≈ 0.05). Brief communication, single lab study. — [DOI 10.1038/nn.3623](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3623); [Yassa lab page](https://faculty.sites.uci.edu/myassa/research/caffeine-and-memory-consolidation/)

### Inferences
- **Sleep: research 4, effect +, band B.** Real-course data (Okano), large correlational meta-analyses (Dewald) and
  randomised restriction experiments (Lo) point the same way. Effects on grades are small to moderate and mostly
  correlational; the causal evidence is on cognition, not exam scores. The practical message: protect sleep over the weeks
  before an exam; one night is not the lever. Chronic short sleep (5 h) is clearly harmful.
- **All-nighters** fall under the same evidence: experimental restriction harms attention and memory, and Okano found late
  bedtimes linked to lower scores. Direct studies of single all-nighters on grades were not searched (budget).
- **Naps: research 3, effect +, band B (conditions).** Good meta-analytic support from lab studies; little real-exam data.
- **Exercise: research 4, effect + (maths) / ± (general), band B at the low end.** Not harmful; overall academic benefit
  unclear because most studies are weak.
- **Breakfast: research 3, effect ±, band C.** Mostly short-term cognition, small studies, confounded with family background.
- **Caffeine: research 2, effect + small, band C.** One small, often-cited lab study; no exam outcomes found. Caffeine
  late in the day could cut sleep (not researched here).
- **LIF overall: band B**, driven by sleep.

### Gaps
- Hydration (e.g. water in exams; dehydration and cognition meta-analyses) not searched: budget ran out.
- Direct studies of all-nighters and GPA (e.g. Thacher 2008) and caffeine meta-analyses not searched.
- Curcio et al. 2006 review and Donnelly et al. 2016 position stand not reached.
- No pooled effect sizes found for Dewald 2010 or Leong 2022 (full texts not opened).
- Very little from India, China or other high-stakes exam systems in what I found.

## ANX: Exam nerves and mental wellbeing

### Takeaway
Structured test-anxiety programmes reliably lower anxiety (a medium effect) but only weakly raise grades. Mindfulness
reduces stress and distress, including during an exam term, but no grade gain was shown. The famous quick pre-exam tricks
are shakier: expressive writing has mixed follow-ups, reappraisal has a few positive real-test results, and
self-affirmation largely failed in large replications.

### Cited Findings
**Structured test-anxiety interventions**
- Huntley et al. (2019), *Journal of Anxiety Disorders* 63:36–50. Meta-analysis of RCTs in test-anxious university
  students. Medium reduction in test anxiety (Hedges' g = −0.64) and weak effects on academic performance (figures as
  reported by a later paper from the same group). Most interventions were behavioural (e.g. relaxation) targeting body
  symptoms; none directly targeted worry. — [Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3032501); secondary source for g: [Frontiers in Education 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2023.1051304/pdf)
- von der Embse, Barterian & Segool (2013), *Psychology in the Schools* 50(1):57–71. A systematic review (not a
  meta-analysis) of child and adolescent test-anxiety treatments 2000–2010: only 10 usable studies, 3 with RCT designs.
  The school-age evidence base is thin. — [DOI 10.1002/pits.21660](https://doi.org/10.1002/pits.21660); [LJMU summary of the review](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/7562/)

**Mindfulness**
- Dunning et al. (2019), *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry* 60(3):244–258. 33 RCTs, N = 3,666 children and
  adolescents. Small benefits vs all controls (d = 0.16–0.30) on attention, executive function, depression,
  anxiety/stress and behaviour. Against active controls (17 RCTs, n = 1,762) only mindfulness (d = 0.42), depression
  (d = 0.47) and anxiety/stress (d = 0.18) remained. — [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:ccdb9b38-8f3c-4b93-8f7c-44d9cf609d8d)
- Galante et al. (2018), *Lancet Public Health* 3(2):e72–e81. Pragmatic RCT, 616 Cambridge students: 8-week mindfulness
  course plus usual support vs usual support. Lower distress after the course and during the exam term (β −0.44, 95% CI
  −0.60 to −0.29); about 6 students offered the course per student kept out of clinical-level distress. No adverse
  self-harm events. Control group had no attention-matched activity. — [PubMed 29422189](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29422189/)

**Expressive writing**
- Ramirez & Beilock (2011), *Science*: 10 minutes writing about test worries just before an exam improved scores vs
  sitting quietly, in lab and classroom studies. — [DOI 10.1126/science.1199427](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199427); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110113141605.htm)
- Frattaroli (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(6):823–865. Meta-analysis of 146 randomised disclosure studies (mostly
  health and wellbeing outcomes, not exams): average r = .075, small but significant. — [PubMed 17073523](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073523/)
- A 2021 classroom study (Myers, Davis & Chan, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*) tested expressive
  writing against an instructional intervention in a college course. — [PMC8192598](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8192598)
- Commentary reports replication failures and methodological concerns for pre-exam expressive writing, but I could not
  confirm a specific published direct replication (e.g. DiMenichi et al. 2018 is named but was not opened). — [Learning and the Brain blog](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/6662422)

**Anxiety reappraisal**
- Jamieson, Mendes, Blackstock & Schmader (2010), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 46(1):208–212. Students told
  that arousal can help scored higher on a practice GRE maths test and, 1–3 months later, on their real GRE maths
  section. — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/4214916)
- A 2022 classroom study with 93 community-college maths students found reappraisal improved the second exam (reported
  in press coverage). — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/2022/01/reconceptualizing-stress-as-a-coping-tool-improves-students-performance-on-upcoming-math-exam-62361)
- A 2018 maths-competition study found no condition effect on maths performance (benefit only for men in exploratory
  analyses). — [Fairfield University record](https://fairfield.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/stress-reappraisal-during-a-mathematics-competition-testing-effec/)

**Self-affirmation**
- Hanselman, Rozek, Grigg & Borman (2017), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 109(3):405–424. Well-powered replication
  in a setting where an earlier large field trial had worked, same procedures: no effect, precise enough to rule out
  benefits larger than 0.10 SD. — [PMC5403146](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403146)
- Serra-Garcia, Hansen & Gneezy (2020), *Psychological Science*: a reanalysis (not a meta-analysis) of Miyake et al. 2010
  (gender gap in physics). The original result depended on covariate-adjusted interaction effects; without covariates,
  affirmation had no significant effect on women's average performance. The paper lists several other failed
  replications (Borman 2012; Dee 2015; Hanselman 2017; and others). — [DOI 10.1177/0956797620923587](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620923587)
- Wu, Spreckelsen & Cohen (2021, *Journal of Social Issues*) meta-analysis of 58 studies reportedly found g = .15 for
  identity-threatened students and g = .01 for others. This came from a search summary; I did not open the paper. — reported alongside [Escobar-Soler et al. 2023 meta-analysis, PMC10779329](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10779329/)

### Inferences
- **Structured programmes: research 4, effect ++ on anxiety / + weak on grades, band B.** Real benefit for wellbeing;
  do not promise better marks.
- **Mindfulness: research 4, effect + on stress and distress, band B for wellbeing.** Good trial during real exam terms;
  effects vs active controls are small; no exam-score evidence found.
- **Expressive writing: research 3, effect ±, band C.** Famous original, small general effect (r = .075 for health), and
  unclear replication for exam use.
- **Reappraisal: research 2–3, effect +, band C.** Some real-test results (GRE, classroom), small samples, one null.
- **Self-affirmation: research 4, effect 0 / ±, band E for general use.** Large, careful replications found nothing; a
  possible small effect (g ≈ .15) only for students under identity threat, which would place a targeted use in C.
- **ANX overall: band B**, driven by structured programmes and mindfulness for wellbeing.

### Gaps
- No separate meta-analysis of CBT apps or breathing exercises for exams was searched; relaxation is covered only inside
  Huntley 2019.
- The 2024 *Scientific Reports* reappraisal meta-analysis mentioned by a secondary source was not verified.
- Huntley 2019 pooled performance effect size not confirmed from the paper.
- Crisis or helpline evidence was out of scope.

## GEN: AI-made study material vs making your own

### Takeaway
Making material yourself (generating answers, cards, summaries) helps memory by about 0.4 SD in lab studies, and a 2023
set of six experiments found self-made flashcards beat premade ones. Direct trials of AI-generated study material are
almost absent; the most-cited positive ChatGPT meta-analysis was retracted in April 2026.

### Cited Findings
- Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott & McDaniel (2007), *Memory & Cognition* 35(2):201–210. Meta-analysis of 86 studies, 445 effect
  sizes: generating beats reading, d = 0.40, with large variation by moderators (reported larger for longer retention
  intervals and recognition/cued recall, per secondary summaries). Mostly lab word lists. — [PubMed 17645161](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)
- McCurdy et al. (2020), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*: 126 articles; how constrained the generation task is moderates
  the effect. — reported in the same search; [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/theories-of-the-generation-effect-and-the-impact-of-generation-co/)
- Pan, Zung, Imundo, Zhang & Qiu (2023), *Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition*, "User-generated digital
  flashcards yield better learning than premade flashcards." Six experiments with undergraduates, total time equal;
  self-made cards generally won (25% better in one experiment), but Study 1 found no difference. A UCLA survey of 901
  undergraduates found over half downloaded premade sets. An earlier study (Lin & McDaniel 2018) is cited as finding the
  opposite. — [Michelle Miller summary](https://michellemillerphd.substack.com/p/r3-21-january-8-2024-do-students); [Tech & Learning](https://www.techlearning.com/news/tech-tools-provide-flashcards-to-students-thats-not-a-good-thing-says-new-research); [Learning and the Brain](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=7456)
- Wang & Fan (2025), *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications*, a meta-analysis of 51 ChatGPT studies reporting
  g = 0.867 on learning performance, was retracted on 22 April 2026 over discrepancies in the meta-analysis. — [GovTech report](https://www.govtech.com/education/nature-retracts-oft-cited-paper-on-positive-impact-of-chatgpt)

### Inferences
- **Generation effect: research 4, effect +, band B.** Strong lab basis, moderate size, conditions matter.
- **Own vs premade flashcards: research 2, effect +, band C.**
- **AI makes your material (GEN as defined): research 2, effect n/a, band D.** No good trials comparing AI-made cards,
  quizzes or summaries with self-made ones on exam outcomes were found. By inference from the generation effect and Pan
  2023, handing the making to AI likely removes a known benefit, unless the student still answers, edits or tests from
  the material. AI-made practice questions that the student must answer may behave like premade retrieval practice,
  which is a different (well-supported) idea.
- Vendor claims about AI study tools should weigh little; the one big positive meta-analysis is retracted.

### Gaps
- No randomised trials of AI flashcard makers or notes-to-quiz tools on real exam scores were found.
- Learner-generated vs provided summaries and questions beyond flashcards were not searched.

## OFF: Answer lookup and offloading

### Takeaway
When a tool supplies answers, practice looks better but later unaided performance gets worse. The best evidence is a
~1,000-student field experiment: open GPT-4 access lowered later exam scores by about 17%, while a hint-only tutor
version avoided the harm.

### Cited Findings
- Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman (2025), *PNAS* 122(26) e2422633122, "Generative AI without guardrails
  can harm learning." Field experiment with nearly 1,000 Turkish high-school maths students. Unrestricted GPT-4 ("GPT Base")
  raised practice scores but students scored about 17% lower than controls on later exams without it; a tutor version
  prompted to give hints, not answers ("GPT Tutor"), largely avoided the harm. One school, one subject. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [SSRN 4895486](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486); [Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education)
- Kosmyna et al. (2025), "Your Brain on ChatGPT," arXiv:2506.08872 (preprint, not peer reviewed as of my search). 54
  participants writing SAT-style essays: LLM users showed the weakest EEG connectivity, search users intermediate,
  brain-only the strongest. Only 18 completed the crossover session; "cognitive debt" is the authors' interpretation. — [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/)
- Risko & Gilbert (2016), *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20:676–688, "Cognitive offloading." People offload when they
  believe it is more efficient, and those beliefs are often inaccurate; memory for offloaded information typically
  decreases. — [DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002)
- Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011), *Science* 333:776–778 ("Google effects on memory") has a mixed replication record; the
  "Google Stroop" effect did not replicate in a 2020 PeerJ study and in a large replication project. — [PeerJ 10325](https://peerj.com/articles/10325)

### Inferences
- **OFF: research 3, effect −, band E.** One strong field RCT plus a consistent theory base; the neuro-preprint is weak
  and should not carry the case. The direction is clear for answer-supplying use; guardrailed, hint-first designs are the
  exception that the Bastani trial supports.
- Integrity guardrails (authorship logs, etc.) were not studied in what I found.

### Gaps
- No trials of photo-solver apps (e.g. 作业帮, 콴다) on exam outcomes were found.
- No evidence searched on integrity guardrails' effect on learning.

## QFX: Quick fixes and popular beliefs

### Takeaway
Most quick fixes have been tested and failed: learning styles, brain training, speed reading, the Mozart effect and sleep
learning of new facts. Smart drugs give tiny lab gains in healthy people and no exam evidence. Binaural beats, background
music and gum have small, mixed lab results.

### Cited Findings
- **Learning styles.** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 9(3):105–119:
  very few studies used a design able to test style-matching, and those that did found virtually no evidence of the
  needed interaction; no adequate basis for using style assessments in teaching. — [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)
- **Brain training.** Simons et al. (2016), *PSPI* 17(3): reviewed 132 papers cited by brain-training proponents; strong
  gains on trained tasks, less on close tasks, little on distant tasks or everyday cognition; most studies had a major
  design flaw. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/brain-training.html)
- **Far transfer.** Sala & Gobet second-order meta-analysis (2019, *Collabra: Psychology*): near transfer exists, far
  transfer small or null; with placebo and publication bias controlled, effect and true variance were zero. Older-adult
  working-memory training meta-analysis (2019, *Intelligence*): far transfer g = 0.121, and g = −0.008 with active
  controls. Child WM training (2017, *Developmental Psychology*): far transfer g = 0.12. All from the same research group. — [Second-order meta-analysis record](https://pure.fujita-hu.ac.jp/en/publications/near-and-far-transfer-in-cognitive-training-a-second-order-meta-a/); [Older adults, Liverpool](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3061251/); [Does far transfer exist?](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3030311/)
- **Speed reading.** Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter & Treiman (2016), *PSPI* 17(1):4–34: readers are unlikely to double
  or triple speed while keeping comprehension; eye movements are ≤10% of reading time; good "speed readers" are skimmers
  who already know the topic. — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/speed-reading-promises-are-too-good-to-be-true-scientists-find-2); [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/so-much-to-read-so-little-time-how-do-we-read-and-can-speed-readi/)
- **Mozart effect.** Pietschnig, Voracek & Formann (2010), *Intelligence* 38(3):314–323: ~39 studies, >3,000 people;
  little evidence for a specific Mozart effect. — [DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2010.03.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2010.03.001); [University of Vienna release](https://medienportal.univie.ac.at/media/aktuelle-pressemeldungen/detailansicht/artikel/mozarts-music-does-not-make-you-smarter)
- **Sleep learning.** A 2023 historical review of 51 papers: simple conditioning during sleep seems possible, but
  behavioural evidence for carrying new knowledge into waking is inconclusive. Andrillon and colleagues found sounds
  played in deep non-REM sleep were later remembered worse. — [Ataei et al. 2023, Ruhr-Universität Bochum PDF](https://www.neuro.psy.ruhr-uni-bochum.de/np/mam/ataei_et_al_2023.pdf); [Earth.com on Andrillon](https://www.earth.com/news/learn-new-information-sleep/)
- **Modafinil / methylphenidate.** Roberts et al. (2020), *European Neuropsychopharmacology* 38:40–62: meta-analyses of
  47 studies in healthy, non-sleep-deprived adults (modafinil 14, methylphenidate 24, d-amphetamine 10). Modafinil overall
  SMD = 0.12; d-amphetamine no overall effect; authors say efficacy not established. — [DOI 10.1016/j.euroneuro.2020.07.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroneuro.2020.07.002); [LJMU](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/13268/)
  Battleday & Brem (2015), same journal: systematic review 1990–2014; simple tests mostly showed better executive function,
  about half showed gains in attention and memory, a few showed worse creative thinking; complex tasks showed more
  consistent gains; few side effects reported in these short studies. — [DOI 10.1016/j.euroneuro.2015.07.028](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroneuro.2015.07.028)
- **Binaural beats.** Garcia-Argibay, Santed & Reales (2019), *Psychological Research* 83(2):357–372: 22 studies, 35
  effect sizes, overall g = 0.45 across memory, attention, anxiety and pain; depends on frequency, exposure time and timing
  (before the task better than during). — [PubMed 30073406](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30073406/)
- **Background music.** Kämpfe, Sedlmeier & Renkewitz (2011), *Psychology of Music*: overall null effect, likely from
  positive and negative effects cancelling out; music disturbs reading and has small harmful effects on memory. — [DOI 10.1177/0305735610376261](https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735610376261)
  de la Mora Velasco, Chen, Hirumi & Bai (2023), *Psychology of Music* 51(6):1598–1626: meta-analysis on learners;
  secondary sources report music without lyrics and quiet music did best. Pooled effect not verified. — [DOI 10.1177/03057356231153070](https://doi.org/10.1177/03057356231153070)
- **Chewing gum.** Tucha et al. (2004) found better sustained attention but no memory benefit and some worse alertness;
  Onyper et al. (2011) found 5 minutes of chewing before testing helped, fading within ~20 minutes; Kozlov et al. (2012)
  found gum harmed short-term memory. — [Review, PMC4449948](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4449948); [Kozlov coverage, FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2012/05/10/Chewing-gum-impairs-short-term-memory-says-study/)

### Inferences
- **Learning styles: research 4, effect 0, band E.**
- **Brain training: research 5, effect 0 (far transfer), band E.** Training improves the trained game, not exam skills.
- **Speed reading: research 3, effect −, band E.** Gains in speed cost comprehension.
- **Mozart effect: research 4, effect 0, band E.**
- **Sleep learning of new material: research 3, effect 0, band E** (deep-sleep playback may even hurt).
- **Smart drugs in healthy students: research 4, effect + tiny (SMD ≈ 0.12, lab tests only), band C at the low end.**
  No exam-outcome trials found; prescription-only, with legal and safety issues not covered here. For a neutral ranking,
  treat as "not recommended" despite the C band.
- **Binaural beats: research 3, effect +, band C.** A medium pooled effect from small studies; publication bias not checked.
- **Background music: research 4, effect ±, band C** (off-grid; placed in C). Lyrics and reading tasks are the worst case.
- **Chewing gum: research 2, effect ±, band C.**
- **QFX overall: band E.**

### Gaps
- Cuevas 2015 (learning styles) not reached.
- Non-prescription nootropic supplements not searched.
- Harms, misuse rates and regulatory action on stimulants (e.g. non-medical use by students) not searched: budget ran out.
- Arzi 2012 (sleep conditioning) not directly reached.

## BAS: Default habits (rereading, highlighting, summarising, cramming, multitasking)

### Takeaway
The habits students use most are among the least effective. Rereading and cramming are clearly beaten by retrieval
practice and spacing; multitasking hurts the student and those nearby. Highlighting gives a small memory benefit but not
understanding; summarising helps only students who already do it well.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *PSPI* 14(1):4–58. Rated rereading, highlighting/underlining and
  summarisation (with keyword mnemonic and imagery) as low utility: they do not consistently boost performance across
  learners, materials and tests; practice testing and distributed practice rated high. — [DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266); [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- Highlighting meta-analysis, *Educational Psychology Review* (2021/22), DOI 10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1: 36 articles
  (1938–2019), 85 effect sizes. Learner-made highlighting improved memory (0.36) but not comprehension (0.20, not
  significant); instructor-provided highlighting improved both (0.44). Authors believed to be Ponce, Mayer & Méndez, not
  confirmed in my search. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1)
- Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3):354–380. 839 assessments, 317 experiments,
  184 articles. Spaced study beat massed study; the best gap grows with the retention interval. Benefits in over 80% of
  verbal-learning studies but only about a third of intellectual-skill (e.g. maths computation) studies. — [PubMed 16719566](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/); [Full text PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)
- May & Elder (2018), *International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education* 15:13. Review of 38 articles
  (2003–2017): media multitasking linked to less effective learning, lower exam scores and reduced study efficiency. — [summary, Lumen Learning](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/learning-hacks/?p=40)
- Sana, Weston & Cepeda (2013), *Computers & Education* 62:24–31. Simulated lecture: laptop multitasking hurt
  comprehension for the user and for nearby peers who could see the screen. — [EdITLib](https://mail.editlib.org/p/132235)

### Inferences
- **Rereading: research 4, effect − (vs retrieval practice), band E.** It is not zero, but it is worse than the
  alternative it usually replaces.
- **Cramming/massed study: research 5, effect −, band E.** Very large evidence base; massing can work for a test the next
  morning but loses to spacing for anything later.
- **Multitasking: research 4, effect −, band E.**
- **Highlighting: research 4, effect + small (memory only), band C at the low end.** Teacher-provided highlights work
  better than self-made ones.
- **Summarising: research 3, effect ±, band C.** Helps trained or capable students; Dunlosky rated it low utility overall.
- **BAS overall: band E.**

### Gaps
- Callender & McDaniel (2009) on rereading not reached; rereading evidence here rests on Dunlosky 2013.
- No numeric pooled effect for spacing vs massing taken from Cepeda (not in the abstract I saw).
- Text-to-speech "listening again" not searched.

## F1. Why I built it
I've spent [OWNER: how long, and how you know this world] around people preparing for UPSC.

The thing I kept seeing wasn't laziness. It was people reading for ten hours a day and losing most of it by spring. They'd re-read, highlight, re-read again. It felt like work. It didn't stay.

The research on this is old and unglamorous: pulling something out of memory is what makes it stick, and it sticks best when you do it just before you'd forget. Almost no prep tool is built that way, because streaks and leaderboards keep people on an app longer.

So we built Aspiran the other way. You answer first, then you look. A review that ends every day. You mark your own Mains answer before anyone else does. And a small pod, so you're not doing it alone.

It's new. We have no toppers to show, and I won't borrow anyone's. If you know someone preparing, ask them to do one topic and tell me what's wrong with it. It's free and needs no sign-up.

## F2. A number from this week
48 people opened Aspiran for the first time. 27 finished onboarding.

We had five screens before anyone could do anything: a garden, six ideas, a welcome in eleven languages, sign-in, consent, a name, a mascot.

I liked every one of them. 44% of people left anyway.

This week it's two screens. The first one asks you a question before it asks you for anything. If we explain memory research to you and then make you read five screens about it, we've missed our own point.

## F3. What we refuse to build
A short list of things that would make Aspiran grow faster and that we won't do:

Streaks. Leaderboards. "Your selection chance: 73%". "Only 4 seats left." Notifications that chase you. Selling phone numbers to counsellors.

Every one of these works. That's exactly why they're everywhere in ed-tech. They are very good at keeping an anxious 23-year-old on an app, and they do nothing for the exam.

Our test for a feature is simple: would we be comfortable if the aspirant's mother read exactly how it works?

## F4. The AI part, plainly
Aspiran uses AI in two places, and both are labelled.

Chotu is a study buddy: it explains a card, quizzes you, plans a day of at most three things. It does not predict ranks, and it doesn't play counsellor. If someone sounds like they're in trouble, it steps back and points to real people.

The second is a second opinion on Mains answers. You mark your own answer first, on five lines. Then you see the AI's marks next to yours.

I want to be exact about what we know. The AI's marking has not yet been compared with a human evaluator's on a proper sample. We're collecting 50 human-marked answers to do that, in English and Hindi, and I'll publish the result, good or bad.

Until then it is what the screen says it is: a second opinion.

The Constituent Assembly was set up under the Plan of 1946. Its members from the British Indian provinces were elected , by the provincial legislative assemblies, not by the people. The Preamble, word by word

Read your main book first. Then close it and fill every gap in pencil. Check against the key at the back, then go over in ink.

The Preamble opens with 'WE, THE OF INDIA'. This tells us the source of the Constitution's authority. It resolves to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SECULAR DEMOCRATIC . 'Republic' means the head of state is , not hereditary. It promises JUSTICE: social, economic and . It promises LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and . It promises EQUALITY of status and of . It promotes FRATERNITY, assuring the of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation. It ends with the date of adoption, 1949. The Preamble has been amended only once, by the Amendment Act of 1976. That Act added the words 'socialist', 'secular' and ''. The Preamble's ideas trace back to the Resolution of 1946-47.

2.2 · Fill-in notes

Features that shape everything else

Read your main book first. Then close it and fill every gap in pencil. Check against the key at the back, then go over in ink.

India has a system: the real executive is the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. Under Article , the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers. Under Article 75(3), the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the . A person who is not an MP can be a minister, but must enter Parliament within months. India has citizenship: a person is a citizen of India, not of a state. Voting rests on universal adult franchise under Article ; the voting age was lowered from 21 to 18 by the Amendment of 1988. The Constitution is partly rigid and partly flexible. Some changes, like forming a new state under Article 3, need only a majority. Most amendments under Article 368 need a majority in each House. Changes to federal features, such as the election of the President, also need ratification by of the state legislatures.

The usual mistakeThe mistake aspirants usually make here: writing that the Preamble 'cannot be amended' or that it 'is not part of the Constitution'. The second was the Berubari view of 1960, which Kesavananda Bharati (1973) reversed. Today the Preamble is part of the Constitution, can be amended, and has been amended once, in 1976. A second trap: 'integrity' is one of the 1976 words, though it sits in the fraternity clause, not in the list describing the State.

Articles 1 to 4: the Union and its territory

Read your main book first. Then close it and fill every gap in pencil. Check against the key at the back, then go over in ink.

Article 1 says: 'India, that is , shall be a Union of States.' The states and their territories are listed in the Schedule. The territory of India has three parts: the territories of the states, the , and any territory that may be acquired. Article 2 lets Parliament admit or establish new states that are part of the Union. Article lets Parliament form a new state, change a state's area or boundaries, or change its name. Such a Bill can be introduced only on the recommendation of the . Before that, the President refers it to the affected state's legislature for its views. Those views are binding on Parliament. Under Article , such laws are not treated as amendments under Article 368, so a majority is enough. In the Berubari reference (1960), the Supreme Court held that giving Indian territory to another country needs a constitutional amendment under Article . The Amendment Act of 2015 gave effect to the land boundary agreement with Bangladesh, including the exchange of enclaves.

Articles 5 to 11 and the Citizenship Act, 1955

Read your main book first. Then close it and fill every gap in pencil. Check against the key at the back, then go over in ink.

Part of the Constitution (Articles 5 to 11) says who was a citizen when the Constitution began. It does not lay down a full code. Article gives Parliament the power to regulate citizenship by law. Parliament did this through the Citizenship Act, . Under Article , a person who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of a foreign State is not a citizen of India; India does not allow dual citizenship. The Act lists five ways to become a citizen: birth, descent, registration, , and incorporation of territory. A person born in India on or after 2004 is a citizen by birth only if both parents are citizens, or one is a citizen and the other is not an . Citizenship can be lost in three ways: renunciation, termination and . The 2019 amendment says certain persons from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and , belonging to six named communities and who entered India on or before 31 December , shall not be treated as illegal migrants. Supporters see it as refuge for persecuted minorities; critics argue it classifies by religion and conflicts with Article 14. An Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) cardholder gets a lifelong visa but cannot or hold posts such as President or a judge of the Supreme Court.

The usual mistakeThe mistake aspirants usually make here: thinking that Parliament needs the state's consent to change its boundaries, or that giving away territory can be done under Article 3. The state is only consulted, and its views do not bind Parliament. Ceding territory to another country needs an amendment under Article 368. A second trap: OCI is not dual citizenship, and an OCI cardholder cannot vote.

The frame of Part III

Read your main book first. Then close it and fill every gap in pencil. Check against the key at the back, then go over in ink.

Fundamental Rights sit in Part of the Constitution, from Article 12 to Article . Article 12 defines 'the State': the Union and state governments, Parliament, state legislatures, local authorities and 'other authorities'. Article 13 says a law that takes away or abridges these rights is to that extent. The right to equality runs from Article 14 to Article 18; Article abolishes untouchability and Article 18 abolishes titles. Article 19 protects six freedoms, such as speech and expression, each open to restrictions on grounds the Article lists. The right to property was removed from Part III by the Amendment, 1978; it is now a constitutional right under Article . Article 21A, free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to , was added by the Amendment, 2002. Article 32 lets a person go straight to the to enforce these rights. During a National Emergency, the President may suspend the enforcement of rights under Article , but never the rights in Articles 20 and .

The usual mistakeThe mistake aspirants usually make here: mixing up Articles 358 and 359. Article 358 suspends Article 19 by itself, and only in an emergency declared for war or external aggression. Article 359 needs a Presidential order, can cover other rights, and can never touch Articles 20 and 21.

The frame of Part IV and Part IVA

The Directive Principles of State Policy sit in Part of the Constitution, from Article 36 to Article . Article 36 gives 'the State' the same meaning as in Part . Part III versus Part IV: how the clash was settled

Read your main book first. Then close it and fill every gap in pencil. Check against the key at the back, then go over in ink.

In 1951, in State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan, the Supreme Court held that Directive Principles must give way to Rights when the two clash. Parliament answered with the Amendment, 1951. In 1971 the 25th Amendment added Article : a law giving effect to Article 39(b) and (c) could not be struck down for violating Articles 14 and . In Kesavananda Bharati (1973) the Court upheld this protection but struck down the clause that shut out review. The Amendment, 1976, stretched the protection to all Directive Principles. In (1980) the Court struck that stretch down and said the Constitution rests on the harmony and between Part III and Part IV.

The usual mistakeThe mistake aspirants usually make here: writing that Directive Principles have 'no legal value'. They cannot be enforced in court, but Article 37 makes applying them a duty of the State, courts use them to judge whether a restriction on a right is reasonable, and the Minerva Mills balance protects both Parts.

12.1 · Fill-in notes

Article 368 and the three ways to change the Constitution

Read your main book first. Then close it and fill every gap in pencil. Check against the key at the back, then go over in ink.

Article 368 is in Part of the Constitution. Its clause (1), added by the Amendment (1971), says Parliament amends the Constitution 'in exercise of its power', by way of addition, variation or repeal. An amendment Bill can be introduced in House of Parliament. Each House must pass it by a majority of its membership and by two-thirds of the members present and voting. If the two Houses disagree, there is no sitting for an amendment Bill. If the Bill changes federal provisions, such as the Lists in the Seventh Schedule or the representation of States in Parliament, the legislatures of at least of the States must also ratify it. The President give assent to a Bill passed this way. Some changes are made by a majority and are expressly not amendments for Article 368: forming new States under Articles 2 to 4, for example, or creating or abolishing a Legislative Council under Article . Article 368(3) says Article does not apply to an amendment. Clauses (4) and (5), added by the Amendment, were struck down in Minerva Mills (1980).

From Shankari Prasad to the NJAC case

Read your main book first. Then close it and fill every gap in pencil. Check against the key at the back, then go over in ink.

In (1951), the Supreme Court held that Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights, because an amendment is not 'law' under Article 13. Sajjan Singh (1965) agreed. In (1967), an eleven-judge bench reversed this and held that Parliament cannot take away or abridge Fundamental Rights. Parliament replied with the Amendment (1971). In v State of Kerala (1973), a bench of judges held, by 7 to 6, that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution but cannot alter its . In v Raj Narain (1975), the Court used the doctrine to strike down a clause of the 39th Amendment on the Prime Minister's election. In Minerva Mills (1980), it held that a amending power is itself part of the basic structure, and so is the balance between Fundamental Rights and the . In Waman Rao (1981), it held that the doctrine applies to amendments made after 1973, the date of the Kesavananda judgment. In I.R. Coelho (2007), it held that laws placed in the Schedule after that date can be tested against the basic structure. In 2015, in the Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association case, the Court struck down the Amendment, which had set up the National Judicial Appointments Commission.

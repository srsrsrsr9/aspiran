Chapter 1

The Preamble

One sentence of 85 words that tells you where the Constitution's power comes from, what kind of country it makes, and what that country is for.

After this chapter you can
  • Break the Preamble into its four parts from memory.
  • Explain each of the five words that describe India.
  • Name the one amendment to the Preamble and the three words it added.
  • Say whether the Preamble is part of the Constitution, and which case decided it.

About 20 minutes · GS2 · Prelims and Mains

Chapter 11
1.1 · The whole sentence, taken apart

Four parts, one sentence

Read the Preamble as answers to four questions: who gives the Constitution, what India is, why it exists, and when it was adopted.

* added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976. The Preamble itself says the Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949.
Memory hookWho · What · Why · When. And the five words in order: S-S-S-D-R, “three S's, then a Democratic Republic”.
Chapter 1 · The Preamble2
1.2 · What each word means

The five words that describe India

WordWhat it means in the Indian Constitution
SovereignIndia decides its own internal and external affairs. No outside power is above it. Joining the Commonwealth or the UN does not change this.
SocialistDemocratic socialism: a mixed economy where public and private sectors exist side by side, aiming to reduce inequality. Not state ownership of everything.
SecularThe State has no religion of its own and treats all religions as equal. Indian secularism is “positive”: the State can support and reform, not just stay away.
DemocraticPower rests with the people, who rule through elected representatives (parliamentary form). The Preamble aims at social and economic democracy too, not only voting.
RepublicThe head of State is elected (the President, indirectly), not hereditary. Every public office is open to every citizen.

Where the ideals were borrowed from

The Preamble is built on the Objectives Resolution, moved by Jawaharlal Nehru in the Constituent Assembly on 13 December 1946 and adopted on 22 January 1947. The Assembly passed the Preamble last, so that it would match the Constitution as finally written.

Trap“Socialist” and “Secular” were not in the original Preamble. Secularism was still part of the original Constitution through the rights to freedom of religion (Articles 25 to 28).
Chapter 1 · The Preamble3
1.3 · The Preamble in court

Is the Preamble part of the Constitution?

The Supreme Court changed its answer once. Learn it as a short story with a twist.

What the Preamble can and can't do

It can
  • Help judges read an unclear Article.
  • Show the basic features of the Constitution.
  • Be amended under Article 368, like any other part.
It can't
  • Be enforced in a court on its own.
  • Give Parliament any power.
  • Take any power away from Parliament.
How UPSC asksStatement questions mixing “part of the Constitution” (true, since 1973) with “enforceable in a court” (false). Read each half of a statement separately.

Fundamental Rights

Part III, Articles 12 to 35. Six rights that the State cannot take away by an ordinary law, and a direct road to the Supreme Court if it tries.

After this chapter you can
  • Place any Article from 12 to 35 in its group, without looking.
  • Say which rights belong only to citizens.
  • Say what happens to rights during an Emergency.
  • Tell the five writs apart, and against whom each works.
  • Trace six cases that shaped Part III.

About 40 minutes, best in two sittings · GS2 · Prelims and Mains

Chapter 27
2.1 · The map of Part III

Twenty-four Articles, six rights

Learn the map first. Once you know which block an Article sits in, most questions become easy to place.

Memory hookThe six rights in order: Every Friend Eats Rice, Cooks Curry. Equality, Freedom, against Exploitation, Religion, Culture and education, Constitutional remedies.
Keep in mindThe Constitution began with seven rights. The right to property was removed by the 44th Amendment (1978). It is now a legal right under Art 300A, outside Part III.
Chapter 2 · Fundamental Rights8
2.2 · One line per Article

What each Article protects

ArtIn one line
12Defines “the State”: governments, legislatures, local and other authorities.
13Any law that takes away a Fundamental Right is void. The base of judicial review.
14Equality before law and equal protection of laws.
15No discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.
16Equal opportunity in public employment.
17Untouchability abolished, and its practice is an offence.
18Titles abolished. Military and academic distinctions allowed.
19Six freedoms (next page).
20Protection on conviction: no retrospective criminal law, no double punishment, no forced self-incrimination.
21Life and personal liberty, taken away only by a procedure established by law.
22Safeguards on arrest and preventive detention.
23No trafficking in human beings or forced labour (begar).
24No child under 14 in factories, mines or hazardous work.
25–28Conscience and religion; managing religious affairs; no tax to promote a religion; limits on religious instruction in State-funded schools.
29–30Any group can protect its language, script or culture; minorities can set up and run their own educational institutions.
32Right to go straight to the Supreme Court to enforce these rights.
TrapArticle 21A is a Fundamental Right; the law that carries it out is the Right to Education Act, 2009. Options often swap the two.
Chapter 2 · Fundamental Rights9
2.3 · Article 19

Six freedoms, each with its own limits

Every freedom in Article 19 can be limited by a “reasonable restriction”, but only on the grounds the Constitution names for it.

Speech and expression 19(1)(a)Limits: sovereignty and integrity, security of the State, friendly relations with other countries, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence.
Assemble peacefully, without arms 19(1)(b)Limits: sovereignty and integrity, public order.
Form associations, unions, co-operatives 19(1)(c)Limits: sovereignty and integrity, public order, morality. “Co-operatives” added by the 97th Amendment, 2011.
Move freely in India 19(1)(d)Limits: general public interest, protecting Scheduled Tribes.
Live and settle anywhere in India 19(1)(e)Limits: general public interest, protecting Scheduled Tribes.
Any profession, trade or business 19(1)(g)Limits: public interest, required qualifications, State monopolies.

There is no 19(1)(f) any more: it was the freedom to acquire property, removed in 1978.

2.5 · The five writs

Five orders a court can issue

WritLiterallyWhat it doesAgainst
Habeas corpus“have the body”Produce a detained person; free them if the detention is illegal.Public authorities and private persons
Mandamus“we command”Orders a public official or body to do its legal duty.Public bodies; not private persons, President or Governors
Prohibition“to forbid”Stops a lower court or tribunal before it exceeds its powers.Judicial and quasi-judicial bodies only
Certiorari“to be informed”Quashes an order already passed without jurisdiction.Judicial, quasi-judicial, and administrative bodies
Quo warranto“by what authority?”Asks a person holding a public office what right they have to it.Holders of substantive public offices
Memory hookProhibition prevents, certiorari cures. Only habeas corpus reaches private persons.
Chapter 2 · Fundamental Rights11
2.6 · Six cases that shaped Part III

How the courts grew these rights

1950 · A.K. GopalanArticle 21 read narrowly: any procedure set by law was enough, fair or not.
1967 · GolaknathParliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights. (Undone by the 24th Amendment, 1971.)
1973 · Kesavananda BharatiParliament can amend any part, including rights, but not the basic structure.
1978 · Maneka GandhiThe procedure under Article 21 must be fair, just and reasonable. Gopalan's narrow reading ends.
1992 · Indra SawhneyReservations for backward classes upheld; usually capped at 50%; the “creamy layer” is kept out.
2017 · K.S. PuttaswamyPrivacy is a Fundamental Right, part of Article 21.

Supreme Court or High Court?

Keep in mindDr B.R. Ambedkar called Article 32 the “heart and soul” of the Constitution: a right is only as strong as the road to enforce it.

One-page revision sheet

The night before: Chapters 1 and 2

Preamble

  • Who · What · Why · When.
  • Sovereign, Socialist*, Secular*, Democratic, Republic.
  • Justice (S, E, P) · Liberty (5) · Equality (2) · Fraternity.
  • Adopted 26 Nov 1949.
  • *42nd Amdt, 1976: socialist, secular, integrity. Only amendment.
  • Built on the Objectives Resolution (Nehru, 13 Dec 1946).
  • Berubari 1960: not part → Kesavananda 1973: part → LIC 1995: part.
  • Amendable under Art 368, not its basic features.
  • Not enforceable; gives or removes no power.
  • Justice from Russia; L-E-F from France.

Emergency

  • Art 358: Art 19 off automatically, war or external aggression only.
  • Art 359: President suspends court access for named rights.
  • Never 20 and 21 (44th Amdt, 1978).

Fundamental Rights

  • Part III, Arts 12–35. Six rights: E F E R C C.
  • Equality 14–18 · Freedom 19–22 · Exploitation 23–24 · Religion 25–28 · Culture 29–30 · Remedies 32.
  • Property (31, 19(1)(f)) gone in 1978 → Art 300A.
  • 21A: education 6–14, 86th Amdt 2002.
  • Citizens only: 15, 16, 19, 29, 30.
  • Co-operatives in 19(1)(c): 97th Amdt 2011.

Writs

  • HC: body; also private persons.
  • M: “we command”; not President, Governors, private persons.
  • P prevents; C cures.
  • QW: “by what authority”; public offices.
  • Art 32 is a FR, rights only; Art 226 wider.

Cases

  • Gopalan 50 · Golaknath 67 · Kesavananda 73 · Maneka 78 · Indra Sawhney 92 · Puttaswamy 17.

Check yourself

  2. Socialist, Secular, Integrity.
  3. Social, economic, political. From the Russian Revolution.
  4. Thought, expression, belief, faith, worship.

## 1. Making a big plan feel small

The proposal has three loops, three passes, pods, a map and Chai. Put on one screen, that's overwhelming. The rule I'd
build to:

- **Today shows one thing.** A single large button: "Next: 15-minute review" or "Next: write one answer". When it's
  done, the next thing appears. The day's list is one tap away for people who like lists.
- **The week is a sentence, not a calendar.** For example: "This week: Federalism, two answers, Friday check with the pod."
- **The map is somewhere you visit, not where you start.** You open it to look around. It never opens with red gaps.
- **Every screen answers "why am I doing this?"** in one line, the first time only. For example: "We bring this back
  now because you're about to forget it."
- **New features arrive with the journey, not on day one.** Week 1 has review, reading and Chai. Answer writing opens
  in week 2. Pods open once your first five topics are done. Interview tools appear only after Mains.

### The "pause and think" lines

I agree this is powerful, but:
- **No made-up quotes for real people.** Putting fake words in Ambedkar's or Kalam's mouth is easy to catch and would
  cost us trust. Use real, checked quotes with their source, or lines that are clearly our own.
- Most lines should be **ours** and unsigned (or signed "Aspiran"). The website already uses four:
  - "Re-reading feels like learning. Recalling is learning."
  - "The exam doesn't ask what you read. It asks what you can still find."
  - "Nobody clears this alone. Nobody can write your answer for you either."
  - "You're not behind. You're only ever measured against yesterday."
- **Each line sits where its idea is used.** For example, the re-reading line goes on the review screen the first
  time someone taps "show me again" instead of trying. A line that appears with nothing to do next is decoration.
- There are about 40 lines in the content kit for you to edit, each tied to a moment. "Discuss" means you rewrite
  them and I push back where one says something the research doesn't support.

## 2. What "structured argument under a word limit" means in the app

Every Mains answer, essay paragraph, GS4 case and interview answer uses one shape:
**claim, then two or three reasons with one example each, then a way forward, all within the word limit.**
The skeleton drill (2 minutes, no writing in full) trains the shape. Full answers use it. The rubric marks it.
The interview mock asks for it out loud. One skill, practised everywhere.

| Tactic | Band | Where it goes in Aspiran |

## 4. "Your room": sleep, notes, journal, to-dos

I agree these are what keep people coming back for two years, and that they should be private. How each works:

- **Sleep.** One tap in the morning, "slept about 6 hours, woke up tired", or an import from the phone's health data
  if they allow it. After three weeks, a private view: "Your review accuracy on days after under 6 hours: 61%. After 7
  or more: 78%." That's their own data, not a lecture.
  The research backs this: sleep in the weeks before a test matters more than the night before.
  **Never** add a sleep streak, a sleep score or any comparison with others.
- **Notes.** Typed, dictated (speech to text, in Hindi too), or a photo of paper. A note can be pinned to a map topic,
  and then it shows up when that topic comes back for review. That's the hook: your own words come back at the right time.
- **Journal.** Free writing, private. Research on journalling for exam stress is mixed (band C), so we **offer** it and
  don't push it. Chotu never reads it unless the person asks for a summary. If someone writes something worrying,
  the crisis protocol still applies; the notice says so plainly.
- **To-dos.** Their own list, separate from our plan. If they write "revise federalism", we offer once to link it to
  the map. No overdue red, ever.
- **"We don't interfere"** means:
  - nothing in here is used for marketing;
  - nothing goes to the pod;
  - AI reads it only when the person taps a button;
  - **export everything** as one PDF/zip at any time. Their two years of notes are theirs to keep. That's also the
    strongest kind of loyalty: people stay because their work lives here, not because leaving is hard.

## 7. When people are inconsistent

Most people will miss days, so the system has to assume it. Rules:

- **Nothing piles up.** The review queue is capped each day. Missed items roll forward and the scheduler re-spaces
  them. Someone who missed 10 days sees a normal-sized day, not 400 items.
- **No guilt numbers.** We never say "you missed 9 days". We say "Welcome back. Here's a 15-minute restart."
- **After about 3 missed days:** a lighter "restart day" (review only, the most important items first).
- **After about 2 weeks:** the plan builder quietly re-sizes the plan to the time left and says once what moved.
- **After about a month:** an offer of a "return week": one diagnostic set to see what stuck, then a fresh plan.
  Nothing is lost; topics that faded go back to *Met*, not to zero.
- **Pods:** someone quiet for 7 days gets one private note from the pod helper (a human role, see below). The pod
  never sees "X has missed 7 days".
- **Loops still work with gaps:**
  - Loop A works on any day you open it.
  - Loop B works if you do just the Friday check.
  - Loop C runs once a month whatever you did that month.
  - Doing a third of the plan still moves people forward. That's the point of spacing.

## 9. Writing: stylus, three snaps, mark then rewrite

- **Stylus:** allow it on tablets, but keep **paper the default**. The exam is handwritten on paper, and speed and
  neatness with a pen are part of what's tested. The research doesn't show handwriting itself is magic: the famous
  "longhand beats laptop" study didn't replicate. So the argument is purely "practise how you'll be tested". People
  writing with a stylus get a monthly prompt to do one paper answer.
- **Three snaps for essays:** skeleton (the 2-minute plan), outline (paragraph headings and examples), full essay.
  Each is a photo. Feedback looks at all three: "Your skeleton had a strong second argument that your essay dropped."
  The number is flexible: 3 by default, a 4th "rewrite" snap allowed. We keep the snaps so the person can scroll their
  own essays over a year and see their thinking change. That makes a good private "you vs. you" view.
- **Mark, then rewrite:** use a strong model for marking (the AI cost note prices this). Steps:
  1. You mark your own answer.
  2. AI marks appear next to yours, row by row.
  3. You rewrite the weakest row only, in 5 minutes.
  4. AI says whether that row improved.
  The gap between your marks and the AI's shrinking over months is the real progress signal.

## 10. GS4 (ethics), plainly

The earlier paragraph was too compressed. Here is what GS4 case questions ask, and how we'd train it:

A GS4 case is a short story about an officer facing a hard choice. For example: a flood relief officer finds the
contractor is a minister's relative and the supplies are poor. The question asks what you'd do and why.

A good answer does four things. We call it the **case circle**:
1. **Who is affected?** ("Stakeholders" just means everyone with something to gain or lose: the flood victims, the
   contractor, the minister, your juniors, you.)
2. **What's in tension?** Name the clash in plain words: honesty against career safety; speed against fairness.
3. **What are the options?** Usually three, each with what it costs.
4. **What do you do, and why?** Pick one, with a principle behind it.

"Thinkers" and "terms" mean: a short reference to an idea from an ethics thinker (Gandhi's talisman, Kant's "treat
people as ends") or a term from the syllabus ("probity", "conflict of interest"). They're **seasoning**. One per
answer, only when it fits. Many aspirants memorise 50 quotes and paste them in, and examiners notice.

In the app: a case appears, you fill the four parts in short lines (a tap-through screen), then write it out by hand.
The pod compares two answers and says which handled the tension better. Chotu adds one thinker or term that would
fit, **after** you try.

## 11. Stage markers: honest view

You asked whether markers like "by month 3 you should have X" are old and generic. My view:

- **Markers against other people** ("toppers finished GS1 by month 4") are what coaching uses. They're mostly
  invented, and they make people feel behind. Don't.
- **Markers against the exam** are useful and honest, because the exam date is real. For example: "To have every
  topic reviewed twice before Prelims, you need to finish the first pass by March."
- **Markers against yourself** are the most motivating: "In October your answers scored 2 of 5 on structure; now 3.5."

So: keep markers, phrased as **"to be ready by your exam date"** and **"you, then and now"**, never as
"where you should be". The plan builder already knows the exam date. This is cheap to build.

## 13. Keep for Mains: should we nudge?

Not worrying too much. Without any nudge, most people won't save examples, and Mains needs them. But nudging every
card turns it into noise. The rule:
- Editors mark about 1 in 5 Chai cards as **high value for Mains**.
- On those cards only, if the person hasn't saved it, one line at the end: "This one is worth keeping for GS2."
- No nudge on anything else, no reminders later, no count of unsaved cards.
- At the monthly consolidation, the case file shows which topics have no examples yet. That's useful, not nagging.

## 14. Interview: generous, and varied

Only people who clear Mains reach it, so its cost per person is high but its value is very high. Plan:
- **DAF bank:** the person enters their DAF (the detailed form UPSC interviews from). Chotu drafts 60 questions from
  it: hometown, college subject, hobbies, job, optional, and current issues linked to each.
- **Chotu boards:** a voice mock with 5 "members", each with a different style: the friendly chair, the sceptical
  economist, the hobby-prober, the current-affairs hawk, the silent one who asks one hard follow-up. Each run picks a
  different mix, so no two boards feel alike. Clearly AI.
- **Pod boards:** 3 to 5 people from the pod or an "interview pod" (people interviewing the same year) sit as a board
  on a video call, using the DAF bank. Humans bring the unpredictability AI can't fake.
- **After each mock:** structure (claim, reasons, example), honesty ("I don't know" said well), and calm. Never a
  predicted score.
- **Generous means:** unlimited text mocks, and a large voice allowance in the interview phase (the cost note covers this).

## 17. Tracking and consent: pushback

You said: track everything, don't worry about consent. I agree with tracking a lot: learning data is how we prove
the method works. But "don't worry about consent" isn't safe or necessary:

- **The law.** India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and its Rules need a clear notice and consent before
  collecting personal data, with a way to withdraw. Health-like data (sleep) and anything about distress is more
  sensitive. Penalties reach crores.
- **Google Play.** The Data safety form must list everything we collect. A mismatch can get the app pulled.
- **The brand.** We sell "the app that respects you". The never-shown list on the site is a promise. Hidden tracking
  breaks it.

The good news: **consent can be one honest screen**, and it doesn't have to cost us data.
- At sign-up: "We keep what you do in the app (answers, reviews, time spent) to make your plan and to improve
  Aspiran. We don't sell it or show ads. Your room (notes, journal, sleep) is private and used only for you."
  One button: "Okay".
- Separate opt-ins only for:
  - sleep data imported from the phone's health data;
  - using anonymised learning data in research we publish;
  - the "how did your attempt go" survey after results;
  - marketing messages.
- Delete and export buttons in settings.

That gets us about 95% of the data with zero legal risk. The website already works this way: details are saved as
the visitor talks, and the phone number is kept only after they tick a box.

## 20. How we'll know it works

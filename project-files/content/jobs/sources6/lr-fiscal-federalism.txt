Fiscal federalism: how money is shared between the Union and the states.
The imbalance. The Union collects the more buoyant taxes, such as income tax and corporation tax, while the states carry much of the spending on health, education, police and agriculture. The Constitution builds in ways to transfer money from the Union to the states.
The Finance Commission (Article 280). The President constitutes a Finance Commission every five years, or earlier. It has a chairman and four other members. It recommends how the net proceeds of shareable Union taxes are divided between the Union and the states (vertical devolution), and how the states' share is divided among states (horizontal devolution). It also recommends grants-in-aid to states under Article 275. Its recommendations are advisory; the Union government lays them before Parliament with an explanatory memorandum.
The 15th Finance Commission, chaired by N.K. Singh, recommended that states get 41 percent of the divisible pool for 2021-22 to 2025-26. The 16th Finance Commission, chaired by Arvind Panagariya, covers the period 2026-27 to 2030-31.
The divisible pool does not include cesses and surcharges, which the Union keeps in full. States have argued that a rising share of cesses and surcharges reduces what they receive.
The 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) added Article 243-I and Article 243Y, under which State Finance Commissions review the finances of panchayats and municipalities every five years. Article 280(3) asks the Union Finance Commission to recommend measures to supplement the resources of panchayats and municipalities.
GST. The 101st Amendment Act, 2016 introduced the Goods and Services Tax, a tax on the supply of goods and services shared by the Union and the states. GST came into effect on 1 July 2017. Article 246A gives both Parliament and state legislatures the power to make laws on GST. Article 269A provides for GST on inter-state supplies (IGST), collected by the Union and shared with the states.
The GST Council (Article 279A) is chaired by the Union Finance Minister, and its members are the Union Minister of State for Finance or Revenue and the Finance Ministers (or other nominated ministers) of the states. A decision needs a majority of at least three-fourths of the weighted votes of members present and voting; the Union's vote has a weight of one-third, and all the states together have two-thirds. In Union of India v Mohit Minerals (2022), the Supreme Court said the GST Council's recommendations are not binding on Parliament and the state legislatures; they have persuasive value.
Compensation. The GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017 promised states compensation for revenue loss for five years. That period ended in June 2022.
Rate changes. In September 2025, the GST Council agreed to move to mainly two rates, 5 percent and 18 percent, with a higher rate of 40 percent for a small set of goods, effective from 22 September 2025.
The debate. One view says GST and the Finance Commission system have created a common market and a fair, rule-based sharing of resources. The other says states have given up much of their power to tax, depend more on transfers, and that criteria such as population can disadvantage states that controlled population growth. Both sides call for trust and regular consultation between the Union and the states.

Citizenship is dealt with in Part II of the Constitution, Articles 5 to 11.
Article 5: citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution for persons domiciled in India.
Article 6: rights of citizenship of certain persons who migrated to India from Pakistan.
Article 7: rights of citizenship of certain persons who migrated to Pakistan.
Article 8: rights of citizenship of certain persons of Indian origin residing outside India.
Article 9: a person who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of a foreign state is not a citizen of India.
Article 10: continuance of the rights of citizenship.
Article 11: Parliament may regulate the right of citizenship by law.
The Constitution deals only with who were citizens when it came into force on 26 January 1950. Everything after that is governed by the Citizenship Act, 1955, which Parliament made under Article 11.
The Citizenship Act, 1955 provides five ways to acquire citizenship: by birth, by descent, by registration, by naturalisation, and by incorporation of territory.
It provides three ways to lose citizenship: renunciation (a citizen gives it up), termination (when a citizen voluntarily acquires another country's citizenship), and deprivation (the Government of India takes it away, for example for citizenship obtained by fraud).
India has single citizenship. There is no separate state citizenship, unlike in the United States.
Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI) was introduced by an amendment to the Citizenship Act in 2005. OCI is not dual citizenship: OCI cardholders cannot vote or hold constitutional offices.
The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 provides a faster path to citizenship for Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014. For them, the residence requirement for naturalisation was reduced from 11 years to 5 years. Its rules were notified in March 2024. Supporters say it protects persecuted minorities from neighbouring countries; critics say leaving out some groups conflicts with equality under Article 14. Petitions against it are pending in the Supreme Court.

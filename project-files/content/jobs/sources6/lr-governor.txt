The Governor: the office, its powers, and the debates around it.
The office. Article 153 provides for a Governor for each state; one person can be Governor of two or more states (7th Amendment Act, 1956). The President appoints the Governor by warrant under his hand and seal (Article 155). The Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President, for a term of five years (Article 156). A Governor must be a citizen of India and at least 35 years old (Article 157). The Governor is the constitutional head of the state, and executive power of the state is vested in the Governor (Article 154).
Powers. The Governor appoints the Chief Minister, and other ministers on the Chief Minister's advice (Article 164). The Governor summons and prorogues the state legislature and can dissolve the Legislative Assembly (Article 174). The Governor can issue ordinances when the legislature is not in session (Article 213). The Governor can grant pardons for offences against state laws (Article 161). Under Article 356, the Governor's report can be the basis for President's Rule.
Advice and discretion. Article 163 says there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister at its head to aid and advise the Governor, except where the Governor is required by the Constitution to act "in his discretion". In Nabam Rebia v Deputy Speaker (2016), a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court held that the Governor's discretion is limited to areas the Constitution specifies, and that the Governor cannot on his own advance the session of the Assembly without the advice of the Council of Ministers.
Bills. Under Article 200, when a bill passed by the state legislature is presented, the Governor can give assent, withhold assent, return the bill (other than a Money Bill) for reconsideration, or reserve it for the consideration of the President. If the legislature passes the bill again, with or without changes, the Governor shall not withhold assent. Article 201 deals with bills reserved for the President.
The bills dispute. Several state governments said Governors held bills for long periods without acting. In State of Tamil Nadu v Governor of Tamil Nadu (April 2025), a two-judge bench of the Supreme Court set time limits for Governors to act on bills and treated ten bills as having received assent. The President then referred questions to the Supreme Court under Article 143. On 20 November 2025, a five-judge bench gave its opinion: courts cannot fix time limits for the Governor or the President to act on bills, and there is no "deemed assent"; but a Governor cannot sit on a bill indefinitely, and in a case of prolonged and unexplained inaction a court can direct the Governor to decide, without directing what the decision should be. The bench also read "withhold assent" together with the duty to return the bill to the legislature.
Removal. In B.P. Singhal v Union of India (2010), the Supreme Court held that although Governors hold office at the pleasure of the President, they cannot be removed arbitrarily or merely because the Union government has changed; a removal can be challenged if it is arbitrary or mala fide.
Reform proposals. The Sarkaria Commission (set up in 1983) recommended that the Governor be an eminent person from outside the state who has not been active in politics in the recent past, and that the Chief Minister be consulted on the appointment. The Punchhi Commission (2007 to 2010) recommended a fixed five-year tenure, and that a Governor be removed only by a resolution of the state legislature.
The debate. One view holds that the Governor is a necessary link between the Union and the states and a guardian of the Constitution in a state, with real discretion in situations such as a hung Assembly. The other view holds that Governors are often seen as agents of the Union government, and that delays on bills and controversial decisions on government formation undermine an elected state government. Both views agree the office should work within the Constitution's limits; they differ on how much discretion it should have.

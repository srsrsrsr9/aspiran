=== Section for node pol-features ===
The Preamble of the Constitution of India.
It opens with "We, the people of India", which shows that the Constitution draws its authority from the people.
It describes India as a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic.
It sets out four objectives: Justice (social, economic and political); Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; Equality of status and of opportunity; and Fraternity, assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation.
The Preamble is based on the Objectives Resolution, which Jawaharlal Nehru moved in the Constituent Assembly on 13 December 1946. The Assembly adopted the resolution on 22 January 1947.
The Constitution, with its Preamble, was adopted on 26 November 1949.
The Preamble has been amended only once, by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976. It added the words "Socialist", "Secular" and "Integrity".
In the Berubari Union case (1960), the Supreme Court said the Preamble is not a part of the Constitution. In the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), the Supreme Court held that the Preamble is a part of the Constitution.
The Preamble is not enforceable in a court of law. Courts use it to interpret the Constitution where the text is unclear.
In November 2024, the Supreme Court dismissed petitions that challenged the addition of "Socialist" and "Secular" by the 42nd Amendment.

=== Section for node pol-citizenship ===
Citizenship is dealt with in Part II of the Constitution, Articles 5 to 11.
Article 5: citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution for persons domiciled in India.
Article 6: rights of citizenship of certain persons who migrated to India from Pakistan.
Article 7: rights of citizenship of certain persons who migrated to Pakistan.
Article 8: rights of citizenship of certain persons of Indian origin residing outside India.
Article 9: a person who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of a foreign state is not a citizen of India.
Article 10: continuance of the rights of citizenship.
Article 11: Parliament may regulate the right of citizenship by law.
The Constitution deals only with who were citizens when it came into force on 26 January 1950. Everything after that is governed by the Citizenship Act, 1955, which Parliament made under Article 11.
The Citizenship Act, 1955 provides five ways to acquire citizenship: by birth, by descent, by registration, by naturalisation, and by incorporation of territory.
It provides three ways to lose citizenship: renunciation (a citizen gives it up), termination (when a citizen voluntarily acquires another country's citizenship), and deprivation (the Government of India takes it away, for example for citizenship obtained by fraud).
India has single citizenship. There is no separate state citizenship, unlike in the United States.
Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI) was introduced by an amendment to the Citizenship Act in 2005. OCI is not dual citizenship: OCI cardholders cannot vote or hold constitutional offices.
The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 provides a faster path to citizenship for Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014. For them, the residence requirement for naturalisation was reduced from 11 years to 5 years. Its rules were notified in March 2024. Supporters say it protects persecuted minorities from neighbouring countries; critics say leaving out some groups conflicts with equality under Article 14. Petitions against it are pending in the Supreme Court.

=== Section for node pol-state-exec ===
The President of India and the Governor of a state, compared.
Office: The President is provided for in Article 52. Every state has a Governor under Article 153. The 7th Amendment Act, 1956 allows one person to be Governor of two or more states.
How they get the post: The President is elected indirectly by an electoral college of the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the state Legislative Assemblies, including the Legislative Assemblies of Delhi and Puducherry (added by the 70th Amendment Act, 1992). The Governor is not elected. The President appoints the Governor by warrant under his hand and seal (Article 155).
Minimum age: 35 years for both.
Term: The President holds office for five years from the date of entering office (Article 56). The Governor's term is also five years, but the Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President (Article 156), so can be removed earlier.
Removal: The President can be removed only by impeachment by Parliament for violation of the Constitution (Article 61). There is no impeachment for a Governor; the Governor holds office at the President's pleasure.
Pardoning power: The President's power under Article 72 covers sentences by court martial and the death sentence. The Governor's power under Article 161 covers offences against state laws. A Governor cannot pardon a death sentence, though the Governor can suspend, remit or commute it, and has no power over court-martial sentences.
Ordinances: The President can issue ordinances under Article 123 when either House of Parliament is not in session. The Governor can issue ordinances under Article 213 when the state legislature is not in session. An ordinance must be laid before the legislature and ceases to operate six weeks after it reassembles, unless approved earlier.
Bills: Under Article 111 the President can give assent, withhold assent, or return a bill (other than a Money Bill) for reconsideration. Under Article 200 the Governor can give assent, withhold assent, return a bill (other than a Money Bill), or reserve it for the consideration of the President.
Discretion: The President acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74). The Governor also acts on ministerial advice, but Article 163 refers to functions the Governor exercises "in his discretion".

=== Section for node pol-const-bodies ===
The Election Commission of India.
Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of elections to Parliament, the state legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice-President in the Election Commission.
Elections to panchayats and municipalities are not conducted by the Election Commission of India. They are conducted by State Election Commissions under Articles 243K and 243ZA.
The Commission was a single-member body (only the Chief Election Commissioner) for most of its early history. It has been a three-member body since 1993: the Chief Election Commissioner and two Election Commissioners.
The Chief Election Commissioner can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court. An Election Commissioner can be removed only on the recommendation of the Chief Election Commissioner.
In Anoop Baranwal v Union of India (March 2023), the Supreme Court said that until Parliament made a law, appointments would be made on the advice of a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, and the Chief Justice of India.
Parliament then passed the Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023. Under it, the selection committee is the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or the leader of the largest opposition party), and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister. The Chief Justice is not on the committee. Supporters say appointments are an executive function; critics say the government now has a majority on the committee. Petitions against the Act are pending in the Supreme Court.
Commissioners hold office for six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier.
The Model Code of Conduct is not a law passed by Parliament. It is a set of guidelines that evolved with the consent of political parties, and the Commission enforces it from the announcement of an election.

=== Section for node pol-const-bodies ===
Four constitutional posts that are often confused.
Attorney General for India (Article 76): appointed by the President; must be qualified to be appointed a judge of the Supreme Court; holds office during the pleasure of the President, and the Constitution fixes no term. The Attorney General is the highest law officer of the country. Under Article 88, the Attorney General has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of both Houses of Parliament and their committees, but has no right to vote. The equivalent post in a state is the Advocate General (Article 165).
Comptroller and Auditor General of India, CAG (Article 148): appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal; can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court; holds office for six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier, under the CAG (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971. Not eligible for any further office under the Government of India or any state after leaving the post. Under Article 151, the CAG's reports on the accounts of the Union go to the President, who has them laid before Parliament. The CAG's reports are examined by Parliament's Public Accounts Committee.
Chief Election Commissioner (Article 324): heads the Election Commission; can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court; holds office for six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier.
Speaker of the Lok Sabha (Article 93): elected by the members of the Lok Sabha from among themselves. The Speaker certifies whether a bill is a Money Bill, and that decision is final (Article 110(3)). The Speaker presides over a joint sitting of the two Houses (Article 118(4)). In the House, the Speaker votes only to break a tie, a casting vote (Article 100). The Speaker decides questions of disqualification on the ground of defection under the Tenth Schedule.

=== Section for node pol-elections ===
The anti-defection law: the Tenth Schedule.
The Tenth Schedule was added to the Constitution by the 52nd Amendment Act, 1985. Its aim was to curb defections, which had brought down many governments, often in return for office.
A member of a House belonging to a political party is disqualified if the member voluntarily gives up membership of that party, or votes or abstains from voting in the House contrary to the party's direction (the whip) without prior permission, unless the party condones it within 15 days.
An independent member is disqualified if the member joins a political party after the election. A nominated member is disqualified if the member joins a political party after six months from taking the seat.
Exception for merger: disqualification does not apply if the original political party merges with another party and at least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party agree to the merger.
The original Schedule also protected a "split" by one-third of a legislature party. The 91st Amendment Act, 2003 removed this exception.
The 91st Amendment also capped the size of the Council of Ministers at 15 percent of the total strength of the Lok Sabha (Article 75(1A)) or the state Legislative Assembly (Article 164(1A)). In a state, the number of ministers, including the Chief Minister, cannot be less than 12. It also said a member disqualified for defection cannot be appointed a minister until re-elected (Articles 75(1B) and 164(1B)).
Questions of disqualification are decided by the Speaker of the House or the Chairman (in the Rajya Sabha or a Legislative Council).
In Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the Tenth Schedule, but held that the Speaker's or Chairman's decision is subject to judicial review.
In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020), the Supreme Court said that disqualification petitions should ordinarily be decided within three months, and suggested Parliament consider giving the power to an independent body instead of the Speaker.
Supporters say the law brought stability. Critics say it weakens individual legislators, since they must vote as the party says even on matters of conscience, and that Speakers, who usually belong to a party, sometimes delay decisions.

=== Section for node pol-judiciary ===
The Supreme Court's jurisdiction, article by article.
Original jurisdiction (Article 131): disputes between the Government of India and one or more states, or between states. Such a dispute can begin only in the Supreme Court.
Writ jurisdiction (Article 32): any person can move the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Court can issue writs such as habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.
Appellate jurisdiction: appeals from High Courts in constitutional cases (Article 132), civil cases (Article 133) and criminal cases (Article 134).
Special leave to appeal (Article 136): the Supreme Court may, in its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from any judgment or order of any court or tribunal in India, except a court or tribunal of the armed forces.
Advisory jurisdiction (Article 143): the President may refer a question of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its opinion. The opinion is not binding on the President.
Review of its own judgments (Article 137).
Court of record (Article 129): its judgments are records of lasting value, and it can punish for contempt of itself.
Law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts in India (Article 141).
Complete justice (Article 142): the Supreme Court may pass any decree or order necessary for doing complete justice in any case before it.
Transfer of cases (Article 139A): it can transfer cases between High Courts, or to itself.
Judges of the Supreme Court retire at 65 (Article 124(2)). Since the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019, the sanctioned strength is 34, including the Chief Justice of India.

=== Section for node pol-scheduled-areas ===
The Fifth and Sixth Schedules: two ways the Constitution protects tribal areas.
The Fifth Schedule (Article 244(1)) deals with the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in any state other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. The President declares an area to be a Scheduled Area. Each state with Scheduled Areas has a Tribes Advisory Council of not more than 20 members, of whom about three-fourths are Scheduled Tribe members of the state Legislative Assembly. The Governor can direct that an Act of Parliament or the state legislature shall not apply to a Scheduled Area, or shall apply with changes, and can make regulations for peace and good government there.
Ten states have Fifth Schedule areas: Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha and Rajasthan.
The Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA) extends Part IX on panchayats to Fifth Schedule areas, with powers for the gram sabha.
The Sixth Schedule (Article 244(2)) applies to tribal areas in four states: Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. These areas are run by Autonomous District Councils, which can make laws on subjects such as land, forests, inheritance and marriage, and can run courts for disputes between tribal people. A district council has up to 30 members, of whom up to four are nominated by the Governor and the rest are elected. There are ten Autonomous District Councils in the four states.
In short: the Fifth Schedule relies on the Governor and a Tribes Advisory Council; the Sixth Schedule gives elected councils their own law-making powers.

=== Section for node pol-parliament ===
Joint sitting of the two Houses of Parliament (Article 108).
The President may summon a joint sitting of the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha when a bill passed by one House is rejected by the other; or the Houses disagree on amendments to the bill; or more than six months pass from the date the other House receives the bill without it being passed.
A joint sitting is not available for a Money Bill. On a Money Bill the Lok Sabha prevails: the Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations, within 14 days (Article 109).
A joint sitting is also not available for a Constitution Amendment Bill. Under Article 368, each House must pass such a bill separately by a special majority.
The Speaker of the Lok Sabha presides over a joint sitting (Article 118(4)). In the Speaker's absence, the Deputy Speaker presides, and in the Deputy Speaker's absence, the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
At a joint sitting, the bill is passed by a majority of the total number of members of both Houses present and voting. Because the Lok Sabha has more members, it usually prevails.
Only three bills have gone to a joint sitting: the Dowry Prohibition Bill (1961), the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill (1978), and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill (2002).

=== Section for node pol-statutory ===
Constitutional, statutory and executive bodies.
A constitutional body is created by the Constitution itself. A statutory body is created by an Act of Parliament. An executive body is created by a government resolution or order, with no Act behind it.
Constitutional bodies: the Election Commission of India (Article 324); the Union Public Service Commission (Article 315); the Finance Commission (Article 280); the Comptroller and Auditor General (Article 148); the National Commission for Scheduled Castes (Article 338); the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (Article 338A, added by the 89th Amendment Act, 2003); the National Commission for Backward Classes (Article 338B, added by the 102nd Amendment Act, 2018); the GST Council (Article 279A, added by the 101st Amendment Act, 2016).
Statutory bodies: the National Human Rights Commission (Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993); the Central Information Commission (Right to Information Act, 2005); the Central Vigilance Commission (Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003; it was first set up in 1964 by a government resolution); the Lokpal (Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013); the National Green Tribunal (National Green Tribunal Act, 2010); the National Disaster Management Authority (Disaster Management Act, 2005); the National Commission for Women (National Commission for Women Act, 1990).
Executive bodies: NITI Aayog, set up by a Union Cabinet resolution on 1 January 2015, replacing the Planning Commission, which was also set up by a resolution, in 1950.
The National Commission for Backward Classes was first a statutory body under a 1993 Act; the 102nd Amendment made it a constitutional body.

=== Section for node pol-history ===
Acts that shaped the Constitution, before 1947.
Regulating Act, 1773: the first step by the British Parliament to control the East India Company in India. The Governor of Bengal became the Governor-General of Bengal; Warren Hastings was the first. It provided for a Supreme Court at Calcutta, set up in 1774.
Charter Act, 1833: the Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India; Lord William Bentinck was the first. It ended the Company's commercial activities.
Government of India Act, 1858: after the revolt of 1857, the Crown took over the government of India from the Company. It created the post of Secretary of State for India, and the Governor-General also became the Viceroy.
Indian Councils Act, 1909 (the Morley-Minto Reforms): introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
Government of India Act, 1919 (the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms): introduced dyarchy in the provinces, and a bicameral legislature at the Centre.
Government of India Act, 1935: provided for an All-India Federation (which never came into being), provincial autonomy in place of dyarchy in the provinces, dyarchy at the Centre, and a Federal Court, set up in 1937. Many provisions of the Constitution draw on this Act.

=== Section for node pol-comparison ===
Features of the Indian Constitution and where they were drawn from, as commonly taught.
Britain: parliamentary government, rule of law, the legislative procedure, single citizenship, the cabinet system, prerogative writs and bicameralism.
United States: Fundamental Rights, independence of the judiciary, judicial review, impeachment of the President, removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges, and the post of Vice-President.
Ireland: the Directive Principles of State Policy, nomination of members to the Rajya Sabha, and the method of electing the President.
Canada: a federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with the Centre, appointment of state Governors by the Centre, and the advisory jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.
Australia: the Concurrent List, freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse, and the joint sitting of the two Houses of Parliament.
Weimar Constitution of Germany: suspension of Fundamental Rights during an Emergency.
Soviet Union (USSR): Fundamental Duties, and the ideal of justice (social, economic and political) in the Preamble.
France: the republic, and the ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity in the Preamble.
South Africa: the procedure for amending the Constitution, and the election of members of the Rajya Sabha.
Japan: "procedure established by law".
India did not copy these features as they were. The framers adapted them; the largest single source was the Government of India Act, 1935.

=== Section for node pol-pm-com ===
The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers.
Article 74: there is a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President. The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 made this advice binding on the President. The 44th Amendment Act, 1978 allowed the President to send the advice back once for reconsideration; the President must act on the advice given after reconsideration.
Article 75(1): the Prime Minister is appointed by the President, and other ministers are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister.
Article 75(1A), added by the 91st Amendment Act, 2003: the total number of ministers, including the Prime Minister, cannot exceed 15 percent of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha.
Article 75(3): the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. It is not collectively responsible to the Rajya Sabha.
Article 75(5): a minister who is not a member of either House of Parliament for six consecutive months ceases to be a minister at the end of that period.
Article 78: the Prime Minister must communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers about the administration of the Union and proposals for legislation, and give information the President asks for.
In the states, Article 164(1A) caps the Council of Ministers at 15 percent of the total members of the Legislative Assembly, with a minimum of 12 ministers including the Chief Minister.

=== Section for node pol-emergency ===
S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994).
Background: Article 356 lets the President impose President's Rule in a state if satisfied, on a report from the Governor or otherwise, that the government of the state cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. In 1989 the Karnataka government headed by S.R. Bommai was dismissed under Article 356 without being allowed to prove its majority on the floor of the Assembly.
The bench: nine judges of the Supreme Court.
What the Court held:
- A proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review. The court can examine whether there was relevant material for the President's satisfaction.
- The majority of a government should be tested on the floor of the House, not in the Governor's residence.
- The state Legislative Assembly should not be dissolved until both Houses of Parliament approve the proclamation. Until then it may only be kept in suspended animation.
- If the court finds a proclamation invalid, it can restore the dismissed government and the Assembly.
- Secularism is a part of the basic structure of the Constitution.
- Federalism is a basic feature of the Constitution.
Context: Article 356 had been used many times before 1994. The Sarkaria Commission (set up in 1983) recommended that it be used sparingly, as a last resort. After Bommai its use fell. In Rameshwar Prasad v Union of India (2006), the Supreme Court held the 2005 dissolution of the Bihar Assembly unconstitutional.
Why it matters for the exam: it is the leading case on Article 356, on the Governor's role in testing a majority, and on federalism and secularism as basic features.

=== Section for node pol-fr ===
Article 32 lets a person move the Supreme Court directly to enforce Fundamental Rights. Dr B.R. Ambedkar called it the heart and soul of the Constitution.
The Supreme Court can issue five kinds of writs:
- Habeas corpus ("to have the body"): orders a detained person to be produced before the court, to test whether the detention is lawful. It can be issued against public authorities and private persons.
- Mandamus ("we command"): orders a public official or body to perform a public duty. It cannot be issued against a private individual, or against the President or a state Governor.
- Prohibition: issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to stop it from acting beyond its jurisdiction, while the case is still going on.
- Certiorari: issued by a higher court to quash an order a lower court or tribunal has already passed without jurisdiction.
- Quo warranto ("by what authority"): asks a person holding a public office to show by what right they hold it.
High Courts can issue writs under Article 226. Their power is wider, because they can issue writs for Fundamental Rights and "for any other purpose".

=== Section for node pol-dpsp ===
Fundamental Rights are in Part III of the Constitution and are justiciable: a person can go to court to enforce them. Examples: equality before the law (Article 14); protection of six freedoms including speech and expression (Article 19); abolition of untouchability (Article 17); the right to constitutional remedies (Article 32).
Directive Principles of State Policy are in Part IV and are not justiciable: courts cannot enforce them, but they are fundamental in the governance of the country. Examples: equal pay for equal work for men and women (Article 39(d)); a uniform civil code for citizens (Article 44); organising village panchayats (Article 40); separating the judiciary from the executive (Article 50).

=== Section for node pol-fd ===
Fundamental Duties are in Article 51A, in Part IVA of the Constitution.
They were added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976, on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. The original Constitution had no Fundamental Duties.
The 42nd Amendment added ten duties. The 86th Amendment Act, 2002 added an eleventh: a parent or guardian is to provide opportunities for education to their child between six and fourteen years of age.
Fundamental Duties apply to citizens only, not to foreigners.
They are not directly enforceable in a court of law; the Constitution provides no direct legal sanction for their violation. Parliament can enforce them through suitable laws.
Among the duties: to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem; to protect and improve the natural environment; to develop the scientific temper; to safeguard public property and abjure violence.
The idea of Fundamental Duties was inspired by the Constitution of the former USSR.

=== Section for node pol-parliament ===
How a Money Bill passes Parliament (Articles 109, 110 and 117):
1. A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and only on the recommendation of the President.
2. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha certifies that it is a Money Bill; the Speaker's decision is final.
3. After the Lok Sabha passes it, it goes to the Rajya Sabha, which cannot reject or amend it. The Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations, and must return the Bill within 14 days.
4. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any of the recommendations. If the Rajya Sabha does not return it within 14 days, the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses.
5. The Bill goes to the President, who may give or withhold assent but cannot return it for reconsideration.
There is no joint sitting for a Money Bill.

=== Section for node pol-parliament ===
Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha compared:
Members: Lok Sabha members are directly elected by the people. Rajya Sabha members are elected by the elected members of state legislative assemblies, by proportional representation with the single transferable vote; 12 are nominated by the President.
Term: The Lok Sabha has a normal term of five years and can be dissolved earlier. The Rajya Sabha is a permanent body; one-third of its members retire every second year.
Presiding officer: The Lok Sabha elects its Speaker. The Vice-President of India is the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
Money Bills: A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha. The Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations, within 14 days.
Council of Ministers: The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, not the Rajya Sabha. Only the Lok Sabha can pass a no-confidence motion.

=== Section for node pol-emergency ===
National Emergency under Article 352 has been proclaimed three times.
1962: on the ground of external aggression, during the war with China.
1971: on the ground of external aggression, during the war with Pakistan.
1975: on the ground of "internal disturbance", declared in June 1975 while the 1971 proclamation was still in force.
1978: the 44th Amendment replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion" as a ground for a National Emergency.
The three proclamations are a common Prelims fact; aspirants often mix up which one was on internal grounds.

=== Section for node pol-local ===
Question for practice: "Discuss the significance of the 73rd Constitutional Amendment for local self-government in India." (150 words)
Facts: The 73rd Amendment Act, 1992 gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj institutions. It added Part IX to the Constitution and the Eleventh Schedule, which lists 29 subjects that may be devolved to panchayats. It came into force on 24 April 1993, now marked as National Panchayati Raj Day.
It provides for a three-tier system (village, intermediate and district) in states with a population above 20 lakh, regular elections every five years by a State Election Commission, a State Finance Commission every five years, and reservation of at least one-third of seats for women.
Editor's note on intros: a strong introduction for "discuss" defines the subject with a specific fact and signals the dimensions the answer will cover. A weak introduction is generic ("Local government is very important in a democracy") or starts listing provisions without framing.

=== Section for node pol-federal ===
The Seventh Schedule divides subjects between the Union and the states in three lists.
Union List (Parliament makes laws): defence, foreign affairs, banking, railways, atomic energy.
State List (state legislatures make laws): police, public order, public health and sanitation, agriculture.
Concurrent List (both can make laws; a Union law prevails in case of conflict): criminal law, marriage and divorce, education, forests.
Education and forests were moved from the State List to the Concurrent List by the 42nd Amendment in 1976.

=== Section for node pol-president ===
The President of India is elected by an electoral college made up of the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the legislative assemblies of the states and of the Union Territories of Delhi and Puducherry. Nominated members of Parliament and of the assemblies do not vote in the presidential election.
The Vice-President is elected by an electoral college of all members of both Houses of Parliament, elected and nominated. State legislators do not take part.
The President holds office for five years and is eligible for re-election.
Both elections are held by proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, and voting is by secret ballot.

=== Section for node pol-amendment ===
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) was heard by a bench of 13 judges of the Supreme Court, the largest ever. It was decided by a majority of 7 to 6.
The question: does Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 have any limit?
The court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but it cannot alter or destroy its basic structure.
The basic structure is not defined in one list. Later cases have counted features such as the supremacy of the Constitution, judicial review, federalism, secularism and free and fair elections as part of it.
The case is central to questions on the amending power, judicial review and the separation of powers.

=== Section for node pol-amendment ===
Article 368 of the Constitution sets out how Parliament amends the Constitution.
An amendment bill can be introduced in either House of Parliament, by a minister or by a private member. It cannot be introduced in a state legislature.
Each House must pass the bill separately by a special majority: a majority of the total membership of the House and a two-thirds majority of the members present and voting.
There is no joint sitting for an amendment bill. If the two Houses disagree, the bill fails.
If the amendment changes federal provisions, such as the representation of states in Parliament or the distribution of powers, it must also be ratified by the legislatures of half of the states, by a simple majority.
The bill then goes to the President, who must give assent. The 24th Amendment of 1971 made this assent compulsory.

=== Section for node pol-fiscal ===
Fiscal federalism: how money is shared between the Union and the states.
The imbalance. The Union collects the more buoyant taxes, such as income tax and corporation tax, while the states carry much of the spending on health, education, police and agriculture. The Constitution builds in ways to transfer money from the Union to the states.
The Finance Commission (Article 280). The President constitutes a Finance Commission every five years, or earlier. It has a chairman and four other members. It recommends how the net proceeds of shareable Union taxes are divided between the Union and the states (vertical devolution), and how the states' share is divided among states (horizontal devolution). It also recommends grants-in-aid to states under Article 275. Its recommendations are advisory; the Union government lays them before Parliament with an explanatory memorandum.
The 15th Finance Commission, chaired by N.K. Singh, recommended that states get 41 percent of the divisible pool for 2021-22 to 2025-26. The 16th Finance Commission, chaired by Arvind Panagariya, covers the period 2026-27 to 2030-31.
The divisible pool does not include cesses and surcharges, which the Union keeps in full. States have argued that a rising share of cesses and surcharges reduces what they receive.
The 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) added Article 243-I and Article 243Y, under which State Finance Commissions review the finances of panchayats and municipalities every five years. Article 280(3) asks the Union Finance Commission to recommend measures to supplement the resources of panchayats and municipalities.
GST. The 101st Amendment Act, 2016 introduced the Goods and Services Tax, a tax on the supply of goods and services shared by the Union and the states. GST came into effect on 1 July 2017. Article 246A gives both Parliament and state legislatures the power to make laws on GST. Article 269A provides for GST on inter-state supplies (IGST), collected by the Union and shared with the states.
The GST Council (Article 279A) is chaired by the Union Finance Minister, and its members are the Union Minister of State for Finance or Revenue and the Finance Ministers (or other nominated ministers) of the states. A decision needs a majority of at least three-fourths of the weighted votes of members present and voting; the Union's vote has a weight of one-third, and all the states together have two-thirds. In Union of India v Mohit Minerals (2022), the Supreme Court said the GST Council's recommendations are not binding on Parliament and the state legislatures; they have persuasive value.
Compensation. The GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017 promised states compensation for revenue loss for five years. That period ended in June 2022.
Rate changes. In September 2025, the GST Council agreed to move to mainly two rates, 5 percent and 18 percent, with a higher rate of 40 percent for a small set of goods, effective from 22 September 2025.
The debate. One view says GST and the Finance Commission system have created a common market and a fair, rule-based sharing of resources. The other says states have given up much of their power to tax, depend more on transfers, and that criteria such as population can disadvantage states that controlled population growth. Both sides call for trust and regular consultation between the Union and the states.

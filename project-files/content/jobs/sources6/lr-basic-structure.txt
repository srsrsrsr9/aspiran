The basic structure doctrine: how the Supreme Court limited Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.
The text. Article 368 gives Parliament the power to amend the Constitution, by a special majority of each House, and for some provisions with ratification by half the states. Article 13(2) says the State shall not make any "law" that takes away or abridges the Fundamental Rights. The question was whether a constitutional amendment is a "law" under Article 13.
Early cases. In Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965), the Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including the Fundamental Rights, because an amendment is not a "law" under Article 13.
Golak Nath (1967). An eleven-judge bench reversed this. It held that Parliament cannot amend the Fundamental Rights to take away or abridge them.
Parliament's reply. The 24th Amendment Act, 1971 said that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including the Fundamental Rights, that Article 13 does not apply to amendments under Article 368, and that the President must give assent to a Constitution Amendment Bill.
Kesavananda Bharati (1973). A thirteen-judge bench, by a 7 to 6 majority, decided the case on 24 April 1973. It upheld the 24th Amendment and overruled Golak Nath: Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution. But it held that Parliament cannot alter the "basic structure" or framework of the Constitution. The judgment did not give a complete list of what the basic structure is.
After Kesavananda. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975), the Court struck down a clause of the 39th Amendment that had placed the election of the Prime Minister beyond judicial scrutiny, and held free and fair elections to be part of the basic structure. The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 added clauses (4) and (5) to Article 368, saying amendments cannot be questioned in any court and that there is no limit on Parliament's amending power. In Minerva Mills (1980), the Court struck down those clauses, and held that limited amending power and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are themselves part of the basic structure.
Later cases. In Waman Rao (1981), the Court said the doctrine applies to amendments made after 24 April 1973. In S.R. Bommai (1994), secularism and federalism were treated as basic features. In L. Chandra Kumar (1997), the power of judicial review of the High Courts and the Supreme Court under Articles 226 and 32 was held to be part of the basic structure. In I.R. Coelho (2007), a nine-judge bench held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be tested against the basic structure. In 2015, in the Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association case, the Court struck down the 99th Amendment, which had set up the National Judicial Appointments Commission, holding that it affected the independence of the judiciary. In Janhit Abhiyan (2022), a five-judge bench upheld, by 3 to 2, the 103rd Amendment, which provides reservation for economically weaker sections, holding that it did not violate the basic structure.
Elements the courts have named as basic features include: the supremacy of the Constitution, the republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, judicial review, free and fair elections, the rule of law, and limited amending power.
The debate. Supporters say the doctrine protects the identity of the Constitution from being changed by a temporary majority, and has kept the core of the Constitution stable through many amendments. Critics say it lets judges, who are not elected, overrule Parliament, and that because there is no fixed list, it is uncertain which amendments will survive.

The anti-defection law: the Tenth Schedule.
The Tenth Schedule was added to the Constitution by the 52nd Amendment Act, 1985. Its aim was to curb defections, which had brought down many governments, often in return for office.
A member of a House belonging to a political party is disqualified if the member voluntarily gives up membership of that party, or votes or abstains from voting in the House contrary to the party's direction (the whip) without prior permission, unless the party condones it within 15 days.
An independent member is disqualified if the member joins a political party after the election. A nominated member is disqualified if the member joins a political party after six months from taking the seat.
Exception for merger: disqualification does not apply if the original political party merges with another party and at least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party agree to the merger.
The original Schedule also protected a "split" by one-third of a legislature party. The 91st Amendment Act, 2003 removed this exception.
The 91st Amendment also capped the size of the Council of Ministers at 15 percent of the total strength of the Lok Sabha (Article 75(1A)) or the state Legislative Assembly (Article 164(1A)). In a state, the number of ministers, including the Chief Minister, cannot be less than 12. It also said a member disqualified for defection cannot be appointed a minister until re-elected (Articles 75(1B) and 164(1B)).
Questions of disqualification are decided by the Speaker of the House or the Chairman (in the Rajya Sabha or a Legislative Council).
In Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the Tenth Schedule, but held that the Speaker's or Chairman's decision is subject to judicial review.
In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020), the Supreme Court said that disqualification petitions should ordinarily be decided within three months, and suggested Parliament consider giving the power to an independent body instead of the Speaker.
Supporters say the law brought stability. Critics say it weakens individual legislators, since they must vote as the party says even on matters of conscience, and that Speakers, who usually belong to a party, sometimes delay decisions.

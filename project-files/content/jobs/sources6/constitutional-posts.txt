Four constitutional posts that are often confused.
Attorney General for India (Article 76): appointed by the President; must be qualified to be appointed a judge of the Supreme Court; holds office during the pleasure of the President, and the Constitution fixes no term. The Attorney General is the highest law officer of the country. Under Article 88, the Attorney General has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of both Houses of Parliament and their committees, but has no right to vote. The equivalent post in a state is the Advocate General (Article 165).
Comptroller and Auditor General of India, CAG (Article 148): appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal; can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court; holds office for six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier, under the CAG (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971. Not eligible for any further office under the Government of India or any state after leaving the post. Under Article 151, the CAG's reports on the accounts of the Union go to the President, who has them laid before Parliament. The CAG's reports are examined by Parliament's Public Accounts Committee.
Chief Election Commissioner (Article 324): heads the Election Commission; can be removed only in the same manner and on the same grounds as a judge of the Supreme Court; holds office for six years or until the age of 65, whichever is earlier.
Speaker of the Lok Sabha (Article 93): elected by the members of the Lok Sabha from among themselves. The Speaker certifies whether a bill is a Money Bill, and that decision is final (Article 110(3)). The Speaker presides over a joint sitting of the two Houses (Article 118(4)). In the House, the Speaker votes only to break a tie, a casting vote (Article 100). The Speaker decides questions of disqualification on the ground of defection under the Tenth Schedule.

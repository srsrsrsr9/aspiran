The anti-defection law: what the Tenth Schedule does, and the debate around it.
Why it came. In the 1960s and 1970s, many state governments fell because legislators changed parties, often in return for office. The phrase "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" came from a Haryana legislator who changed parties several times in 1967.
The law. The 52nd Amendment Act, 1985 added the Tenth Schedule to the Constitution. It also amended Articles 101, 102, 190 and 191 on vacation of seats and disqualification.
Grounds for disqualification. A member belonging to a political party is disqualified if the member voluntarily gives up membership of that party; or votes or abstains in the House contrary to the party's direction without prior permission, unless the party condones it within 15 days. An independent member who joins a political party after the election is disqualified. A nominated member who joins a political party after six months from taking the seat is disqualified.
The merger exception. A member is not disqualified if the member's original political party merges with another party and at least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party agree to the merger.
The 91st Amendment, 2003. The original Schedule protected a "split" by one-third of a legislature party. The 91st Amendment Act, 2003 removed this exception. It also capped the size of the Council of Ministers at 15 percent of the Lok Sabha or the state Assembly (with a minimum of 12 in a state), and barred a member disqualified for defection from being a minister until re-elected.
Who decides. The Speaker or the Chairman of the House decides questions of disqualification. Paragraph 7 of the Schedule barred courts from examining these decisions.
Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu (1992). A five-judge bench upheld the Tenth Schedule as valid. It held that the Speaker or Chairman acts as a tribunal while deciding disqualification, and that the decision is subject to judicial review. Paragraph 7 was held invalid because it had not been ratified by the states as Article 368(2) requires for such a change.
Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020). The Supreme Court said disqualification petitions should ordinarily be decided within three months, and suggested that Parliament consider giving this power to an independent body, such as a permanent tribunal, instead of the Speaker.
The debate. Supporters say the law has reduced individual defections and given governments stability, and that voters often vote for a party, so a legislator who changes sides should seek a fresh mandate. Critics say it has shifted defection from individuals to groups who use the merger route; that it makes legislators vote as the party says even on matters of conscience, weakening debate in the House; and that Speakers, who belong to a party, have sometimes delayed decisions for long periods. Reform ideas include limiting the whip to confidence motions and money bills, giving the decision to the Election Commission or an independent tribunal, and fixing time limits.

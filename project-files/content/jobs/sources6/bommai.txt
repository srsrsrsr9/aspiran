S.R. Bommai v Union of India (1994).
Background: Article 356 lets the President impose President's Rule in a state if satisfied, on a report from the Governor or otherwise, that the government of the state cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. In 1989 the Karnataka government headed by S.R. Bommai was dismissed under Article 356 without being allowed to prove its majority on the floor of the Assembly.
The bench: nine judges of the Supreme Court.
What the Court held:
- A proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review. The court can examine whether there was relevant material for the President's satisfaction.
- The majority of a government should be tested on the floor of the House, not in the Governor's residence.
- The state Legislative Assembly should not be dissolved until both Houses of Parliament approve the proclamation. Until then it may only be kept in suspended animation.
- If the court finds a proclamation invalid, it can restore the dismissed government and the Assembly.
- Secularism is a part of the basic structure of the Constitution.
- Federalism is a basic feature of the Constitution.
Context: Article 356 had been used many times before 1994. The Sarkaria Commission (set up in 1983) recommended that it be used sparingly, as a last resort. After Bommai its use fell. In Rameshwar Prasad v Union of India (2006), the Supreme Court held the 2005 dissolution of the Bihar Assembly unconstitutional.
Why it matters for the exam: it is the leading case on Article 356, on the Governor's role in testing a majority, and on federalism and secularism as basic features.

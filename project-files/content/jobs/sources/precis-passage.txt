Passage (English, written by the editor for Paper B practice):

Libraries were once thought to be places of silence, where people came to read and left without speaking to anyone. In many Indian towns, that picture has changed. District libraries now open their reading rooms early in the morning, and by eight o'clock the tables are full of young people preparing for competitive examinations. Many of them cannot afford coaching, and some do not have a quiet corner at home. For them, the library offers three things that are hard to find elsewhere: space, books and the company of others working towards the same goal. Librarians report that students form informal study groups, share notes and test each other before mock examinations. Some libraries have added newspapers in several languages, free internet access and evening talks by retired officers. Yet many still struggle with too few seats, old books and irregular funding. Where local governments and citizens have invested modestly, in longer opening hours, more seats and fresh editions of standard books, the reading rooms have filled almost at once. A public library, it seems, is no longer only a store of books. It has become a shared workplace for the ambitions of those who would otherwise prepare alone. Its value lies not just in what is on its shelves, but in the people it brings together, and the quiet discipline it lends to each of them.

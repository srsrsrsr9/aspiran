N: Here is a question to carry on your walk. If Parliament can change any part of the Constitution, what stops it from changing the Constitution into something else altogether?

F: Is that a real worry? Parliament is elected. Surely it would not do that.

N: That is exactly the question India's courts argued over for more than twenty years. And the answer they reached is one of the most tested ideas in Indian polity. It is called the basic structure doctrine. Let us walk through it as a story, one case at a time.

N: Start with the text. Article three hundred and sixty-eight is titled "Power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure therefor". It lets Parliament amend any provision by way of addition, variation or repeal.

F: Just like an ordinary law?

N: No. The procedure is harder. An amendment Bill can start in either House. It must pass each House by a majority of the total membership of that House, and by two-thirds of the members present and voting. That is the special majority.

F: And that is all?

N: For some provisions, there is one more step. If the amendment touches things like the election of the President, the Union and State executive powers, the Supreme Court and High Courts, the lists in the Seventh Schedule, or the representation of States in Parliament, then at least half of the State legislatures must also ratify it. Then the Bill goes to the President, who must give assent.

F: So the procedure is the hurdle. Once you clear it, you can change anything?

N: That was the first view. Let us see how it held up.

N: Scene one. The year is nineteen fifty-one. The new Republic is barely a year old. Parliament passes the First Amendment, which adds Articles thirty-one A and thirty-one B, to protect land reform laws from challenge. Landholders go to the Supreme Court. Their argument is clever.

F: What was it?

N: Article thirteen says the State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges fundamental rights. They said an amendment is also a law. So an amendment that cuts fundamental rights must be void.

F: That sounds reasonable, actually.

N: It sounds reasonable. But on the fifth of October, nineteen fifty-one, in Shankari Prasad Singh Deo versus Union of India, a bench of five judges said no. The Court held that "law" in Article thirteen means ordinary law. An amendment is made in exercise of constituent power, so Article thirteen does not touch it. The First Amendment stood. In nineteen sixty-four, in Sajjan Singh's case, the Court took the same line for the Seventeenth Amendment.

F: So Parliament wins round one, and round two.

N: Yes. Scene two. Nineteen sixty-seven. The Golaknath family of Punjab challenges a Punjab land tenure law. This time eleven judges sit. On the twenty-seventh of February, nineteen sixty-seven, by six to five, the Court reverses its earlier view.

F: Reverses it how?

N: It holds that an amendment is "law" under Article thirteen. So fundamental rights in Part Three cannot be taken away or abridged through Article three hundred and sixty-eight. To avoid unsettling years of land reforms, the Court applied this only to the future. That idea is called prospective overruling.

F: So now the Court wins. Fundamental rights are frozen.

N: That is how many people read it. And Parliament replied. The Twenty-fourth Amendment of nineteen seventy-one rewrote Article three hundred and sixty-eight. It said in plain words that Parliament amends in exercise of its constituent power, that Article thirteen does not apply to amendments, and that the President must give assent to an amendment Bill.

F: So the question goes back to the Court again.

N: Scene three, the big one. A religious head from Kerala, Kesavananda Bharati, had challenged Kerala's land reform laws in nineteen seventy. Along the way, his case became a challenge to the Twenty-fourth, Twenty-fifth and Twenty-ninth Amendments. Thirteen judges heard it. Judgment came on the twenty-fourth of April, nineteen seventy-three.

F: And who won?

N: Here is the beautiful part. Both sides won something, and both lost something. The majority view of seven judges held, first, that Golaknath was wrong. Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including fundamental rights. Second, the power to amend is not the power to destroy. An amendment cannot damage or destroy the basic structure of the Constitution.

F: So Parliament can change any article, but not the foundation?

N: Exactly. Think of a house. You can repaint it, add a room, change the windows. You cannot remove the foundation and still call it the same house.

F: So what is in the foundation? Is there a list?

N: Here comes the trap. Different judges gave examples. Chief Justice Sikri named things like the supremacy of the Constitution, a republican and democratic form of government, the secular character of the Constitution, separation of powers, and the federal character. Justices Shelat and Grover gave a similar list, and wrote, quote, "These cannot be catalogued but can only be illustrated." Unquote.

F: So there is no fixed list.

N: None. The Court identifies basic features case by case. That is why the list has grown over the years. Hold on to that.

N: Scene four. Nineteen seventy-five. On the twelfth of June, the Allahabad High Court sets aside the election of the Prime Minister, Indira Gandhi, finding a corrupt practice. While her appeal is pending in the Supreme Court, Parliament passes the Thirty-ninth Amendment. It inserts Article three hundred and twenty-nine A, whose clause four declared that election valid and put it beyond the earlier election law.

F: Wait. Parliament decided a single election case by amending the Constitution?

N: That was the objection. On the seventh of November, nineteen seventy-five, in Indira Nehru Gandhi versus Raj Narain, the Court struck down clause four. Justice Khanna said it violated the principle of free and fair elections, which is an essential postulate of democracy and part of the basic structure. This was the first time the new doctrine was used to strike down an amendment.

F: That is a big moment. What happened after?

N: Parliament passed the Forty-second Amendment in nineteen seventy-six. Among many changes, section fifty-five added two clauses to Article three hundred and sixty-eight. Clause four said no amendment could be questioned in any court on any ground. Clause five said there shall be no limitation whatever on Parliament's constituent power.

F: So that is Parliament saying, the basic structure does not apply to us.

N: Yes. Scene five. Minerva Mills, a textile company, had been taken over by the government. Its owners challenged the takeover, and with it the Forty-second Amendment. On the thirty-first of July, nineteen eighty, the Court struck down clauses four and five of Article three hundred and sixty-eight.

F: On what reasoning?

N: The Court said the Constitution gave Parliament a limited amending power. A body with limited power cannot use it to make that power unlimited. In its words, a limited amending power is itself one of the basic features. It also struck down section four of the same amendment, which had extended Article thirty-one C to cover all Directive Principles. The Court said the Constitution rests on a balance between fundamental rights and Directive Principles.

F: Here is a small thing that confuses me. If clauses four and five were struck down, why do I still see them when I read Article three hundred and sixty-eight?

N: Good catch. Many printed copies of the Constitution still carry those words, because the Court declared them void rather than Parliament removing them. Read Article three hundred and sixty-eight together with Minerva Mills.

N: Now, is the doctrine still alive? Very much. On the sixteenth of October, twenty fifteen, in the Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association case, the Court declared the Ninety-ninth Amendment, which had created the National Judicial Appointments Commission, the NJAC, unconstitutional and void.

F: And something more recent?

N: Yes. On the twenty-fifth of November, twenty twenty-four, in Balram Singh versus Union of India, a bench of Chief Justice Sanjiv Khanna and Justice Sanjay Kumar dismissed petitions against the words "socialist" and "secular", which the Forty-second Amendment put into the Preamble in nineteen seventy-six. The Court said the power to amend rests with Parliament and extends to the Preamble. It also said amendments can be challenged on various grounds, including violation of the basic structure. And it noted that earlier Constitution Benches had called secularism a basic feature.

F: So the doctrine works both ways. It limits Parliament, but it also lets the Court uphold an amendment.

F: Is everyone agreed that the doctrine is right?

N: No, and you should know both sides. Critics say the words basic structure appear nowhere in the Constitution. They argue that unelected judges decide what the foundation is, and the list can keep growing, so the doctrine is open-ended. Supporters reply that a written Constitution with a limited amending power needs some guard. Without it, a temporary majority could, in theory, remove elections or the courts themselves. The debate has returned in public life after the NJAC judgment too. For the exam, present it as a debate, with both views.

F: Does this come up in the Mains?

N: It does. UPSC Mains twenty twenty-five, General Studies Paper Two, question twelve, asked this, quote: "Indian Constitution has conferred the amending power on the ordinary legislative institutions with a few procedural hurdles. In view of this statement, examine the procedural and substantive limitations on the amending power of the Parliament to change the Constitution." Unquote.

F: Procedural and substantive. So the procedure is the special majority and State ratification. And the substance is the basic structure.

N: You have it. Procedure from Article three hundred and sixty-eight. Substance from Kesavananda, Raj Narain and Minerva Mills.

N: Now the usual trap, in two parts. First, people mix up the cases. Golaknath said fundamental rights cannot be amended. Kesavananda said anything can be amended, but not the basic structure. Second, people treat the list of basic features as complete. It is not. It was illustrated, never catalogued.

N: Three things to keep. First, Article three hundred and sixty-eight gives Parliament the power to amend any provision, through a special majority, and for some provisions, ratification by half the States. Second, Kesavananda Bharati, nineteen seventy-three, held that this power cannot damage the basic structure. It overruled Golaknath. Third, the basic features are not an exhaustive list. Courts identify them case by case, from free and fair elections in Raj Narain to a limited amending power in Minerva Mills.

N: Now three quick recall questions.

N: One. Which case first held that Parliament cannot amend the basic structure of the Constitution? Is it Kesavananda Bharati, or Golaknath, or Shankari Prasad?

[pause 4s]

N: Kesavananda Bharati, nineteen seventy-three. Golaknath had said fundamental rights could not be amended at all, and Shankari Prasad had upheld the First Amendment.

N: Two. What did the Court hold in Golaknath, nineteen sixty-seven? Was it that amendments are outside Article thirteen, or that Parliament can amend even the basic structure, or that fundamental rights cannot be abridged by amendment?

[pause 4s]

N: That fundamental rights cannot be abridged by amendment. Six of eleven judges treated an amendment as law under Article thirteen.

N: Three. In Minerva Mills, nineteen eighty, what did the Court strike down? Was it clause four of Article three hundred and twenty-nine A, or clauses four and five of Article three hundred and sixty-eight, or the rule on ratification by the States?

[pause 4s]

N: Clauses four and five of Article three hundred and sixty-eight. A limited amending power is itself a basic feature. Clause four of Article three hundred and twenty-nine A fell earlier, in Raj Narain.

F: So the house can be repaired, extended, repainted. Just not knocked down.

N: That is the whole doctrine in one line. If you want to go further, the syllabus note on amendments in the app has this in writing, and Mains answer writing is a good place to try the twenty twenty-five question in your own words. That is the basic structure story, from nineteen fifty-one to today.

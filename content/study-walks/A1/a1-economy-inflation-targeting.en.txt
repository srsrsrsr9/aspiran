N: Here is something to chew on while you walk. Onion prices in India more than doubled in a year. Who in this country is responsible for bringing that under control? And what happens if they fail?

F: The RBI, surely. Isn't fighting inflation its whole job?

N: It is a big part of its job. But the full answer has more people in it than most aspirants expect. Let's start with a real month.

N: On the twelfth of November twenty twenty-six, the National Statistics Office, the NSO, released retail inflation for October. The number was six point zero nine per cent. That is just above six per cent. Food inflation was higher still, close to nine per cent.

F: And onions were the culprit?

N: Onions led the list. Onion prices were up by more than a hundred and sixteen per cent over October of the year before. Garlic, ginger and lemon were also up sharply. But potato and cauliflower were actually cheaper than a year earlier. So it was not every vegetable. It was a few items rising very fast.

F: So did the RBI do anything?

N: It had already moved. A month earlier, from the fifth to the seventh of October twenty twenty-six, the Monetary Policy Committee met. It raised the policy repo rate by a quarter of a percentage point, to five point five per cent. The vote was unanimous, six to zero. It also changed its stance to what it called calibrated tightening. That means rate cuts are off the table for now. The next move can only be a hike or a pause.

F: Why raise rates if inflation was still below six per cent at that time?

N: Because the committee looks ahead, not back. In its resolution it pointed to a deficient south-west monsoon and strong El Niño conditions. It named price spikes in onion and sugar. It worried that price rises were spreading to more items. It projected inflation of about six per cent for the October to December quarter. So it acted before the number arrived.

N: As of November twenty twenty-six, the repo rate stands at five point five per cent. The committee meets again in early December.

F: Okay. But who set the six per cent? Did the RBI choose it?

N: This is the heart of the topic. Let's build it step by step.

N: In twenty sixteen, Parliament amended the Reserve Bank of India Act, nineteen thirty-four, through the Finance Act. Since then, the preamble of the Act says the primary objective of monetary policy is to maintain price stability, while keeping in mind the objective of growth.

F: Price stability first, growth second.

N: Yes, and that is why the system is called flexible inflation targeting. Flexible, because growth is still kept in mind. Targeting, because there is a number.

N: Now the key point. Under section forty-five Z A of the Act, the Central Government decides the inflation target, in consultation with the RBI. It does this once every five years. The RBI is consulted. It does not decide.

F: So the Government sets the goal, and the RBI has to hit it.

N: Exactly. The first target was four per cent, with a band of two per cent on either side. So the target is four, and anything from two to six is within the band. It was kept the same for April twenty twenty-one to March twenty twenty-six.

F: And then the five years ran out.

N: Right. That brings us to twenty twenty-six. Before the review, the RBI published a discussion paper in August twenty twenty-five. It asked four questions. Should the target be headline inflation or core inflation? Is four per cent still right? Should the band change? Should a range replace a single number?

F: That sounds like a big debate.

N: It was, and it is fair to say economists disagreed. Some argued for targeting core inflation, which leaves out food and fuel. Their reason: food prices jump because of weather, and interest rates cannot grow onions. Others argued for headline inflation. Their reason: food is a large part of what poor families buy, so headline is the closest measure of the cost of living. The RBI says it received seventy-five direct responses. In the end, the Government, in consultation with the RBI, kept the target at four per cent, with the same band of two to six per cent, through March twenty thirty-one. Headline inflation stays the measure.

F: So nothing changed.

N: The numbers did not change. But the framework was reviewed openly and kept. That itself is a useful point for Mains.

F: Now, who actually moves the repo rate? The Governor?

N: Not alone. The Monetary Policy Committee, the MPC, decides the repo rate. It has six members. Three come from the RBI, and the Governor chairs it. Three are external members appointed by the Government for four years. The committee must meet at least four times a year, and it publishes its minutes.

F: So the Government sets the target, and the committee chooses the rate to reach it.

N: That is the clean way to say it. Now, which inflation are we measuring? Here aspirants often trip.

F: There's CPI and WPI. They're basically the same, right?

N: That's the trap. They are different. The Consumer Price Index, the CPI, tracks the prices that households pay in shops. It includes services, like rent, transport, restaurants and schooling. The NSO, under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation, publishes it. Since February twenty twenty-six, its base year is twenty twenty-four.

N: The Wholesale Price Index, the WPI, tracks prices at the wholesale level, mostly goods and commodities. It has no services at all.

F: And the target uses which one?

N: The CPI. The RBI Act itself defines inflation as the year-on-year change in the monthly Consumer Price Index. So when you read four per cent target, read four per cent CPI inflation. Never WPI.

F: And headline versus core?

N: Headline inflation is the full CPI basket. Core inflation takes out the most volatile items, mainly food and fuel. In August twenty twenty-six, headline was four point eight per cent, while core was four point two. When headline runs above core, it usually means food or fuel is pushing prices up.

F: Got it. Now explain how raising the repo rate actually brings onion prices down. That part never made sense to me.

N: Fair question. First, what is the repo rate? It is the rate at which the RBI lends money to banks overnight, against government securities. After the October decision, the RBI also set the Standing Deposit Facility rate at five point two five per cent. That is the floor, where banks park spare money with the RBI. The Marginal Standing Facility rate is five point seven five per cent. That is the ceiling, for emergency borrowing.

N: Now the chain. When the repo rate rises, banks pay more to borrow. Over time they charge more on home loans, car loans and business loans. They also offer a little more on deposits. So people borrow and spend a bit less, and save a bit more. Demand in the whole economy cools. That slowly eases prices. This journey from the policy rate to your loan rate is called transmission.

F: But that does nothing for onions directly.

N: You are right, and that is honest economics. A hike does not create onions. Supply is fixed by the harvest, storage and trade. What a hike does is stop a food shock from spreading. If people start expecting high prices, workers ask for higher wages, and shops raise prices of everything. The RBI wants to keep those expectations anchored. In October it said exactly this, pointing to elevated inflation expectations.

F: And transmission is slow?

N: It can take months. That is why the RBI has pushed banks to link many loans to external benchmarks, such as the repo rate itself, so that changes pass through faster.

F: Last big one. What if inflation goes above six per cent? October was six point zero nine. Has the RBI failed?

N: No, and this is a common mistake. One month above six is not a failure. Under the law, the RBI is said to have failed only if average inflation stays above the upper band, or below the lower band, for three consecutive quarters.

F: And then?

N: Then, under section forty-five Z N, the RBI must send a report to the Central Government. The report gives the reasons for the failure, the remedial actions it proposes, and an estimate of how long it will take to bring inflation back to target.

F: Has that ever happened?

N: Once. In twenty twenty-two, after the war in Ukraine pushed up food and fuel prices, inflation stayed above six per cent for three quarters in a row, from January to September. The MPC held an additional meeting on the third of November twenty twenty-two, and the RBI sent its report to the Government.

F: So the system has teeth, but it is about accountability, not punishment.

N: Well put. And over the long run, the RBI's own figures show average inflation fell from about eight point one per cent in the decade before targeting to about four point six per cent in the decade with it.

N: Now the exam angle. UPSC Prelims twenty twenty-three, General Studies Paper One, question twenty-two, gave two statements. The first said that after the pandemic, many central banks raised interest rates. The second said central banks generally assume they can counter rising consumer prices through monetary policy. The correct answer, as per UPSC's key, was that both are true and the second explains the first. That is exactly the logic of the October hike.

N: For Mains, General Studies Paper Three asks about the Indian economy, growth and mobilisation of resources. A question on whether inflation targeting suits a country with a large food basket fits right here. Write both sides.

N: So, three things to keep.

N: First, the Central Government sets the inflation target, in consultation with the RBI, once every five years. As of November twenty twenty-six, it is four per cent CPI inflation, with a band of two to six per cent, through March twenty thirty-one.

N: Second, the six-member Monetary Policy Committee sets the repo rate. A rate change works through transmission, by cooling demand and anchoring expectations, not by fixing supply.

N: Third, failure means average inflation outside the band for three consecutive quarters. Then the RBI reports to the Government with reasons, remedies and a timeline. This happened once, in twenty twenty-two.

N: Now three quick questions.

N: Question one. Under the RBI Act, who decides the inflation target? Is it the RBI alone, or the Central Government in consultation with the RBI, or the Monetary Policy Committee?

[pause 4s]

N: The Central Government in consultation with the RBI. The MPC sets the repo rate to reach the target; it does not choose the target.

N: Question two. Which index does the inflation target use? Is it the Consumer Price Index, or the Wholesale Price Index, or the GDP deflator?

[pause 4s]

N: The Consumer Price Index. The Act defines inflation as the yearly change in monthly CPI. The WPI has no services and is not the target.

N: Question three. When is the RBI deemed to have failed its target? Is it when inflation crosses six per cent in any single month, or when the yearly average is above four per cent, or when average inflation stays outside the two to six per cent band for three consecutive quarters?

[pause 4s]

N: Three consecutive quarters outside the band. A single month above six, like October twenty twenty-six, is not a failure.

F: So the onion story was really a lesson in who decides what.

N: That is the whole topic in one line. The Government sets the goal. The committee picks the rate. The CPI keeps the score. And Parliament wrote the rules for when things go wrong.

N: A question for your walk. If a constitution borrows ideas from many countries, is it a copy, or is it something new?

F: Honestly, I have heard people call ours a bag of borrowings. Is that unfair?

N: It is a charge the framers heard too, and they answered it on the floor of the Constituent Assembly. That answer is the best way into this syllabus line, comparison with other constitutions. Because comparing is not about listing countries. It is about seeing what India took, and what India changed.

N: The date is the fourth of November, nineteen forty-eight. Doctor B. R. Ambedkar moves the Draft Constitution in the Assembly. Near the end of a long speech, he takes up the critics. He says it is claimed that there is nothing new in the draft, that about half of it was copied from the Government of India Act of nineteen thirty-five, and that the rest was borrowed from other countries.

F: And how did he reply?

N: He asked whether anything can be new in a constitution framed at this hour in the history of the world. More than a hundred years had passed since the first written constitution. The basic ideas were settled everywhere. So, in his words, the only new things, if there can be any, in a constitution framed so late in the day are the variations made to remove the faults and to accommodate it to the needs of the country.

F: So the newness is in the changes, not in the ideas.

N: Exactly. He also said there is nothing to be ashamed of in borrowing, and nobody holds patent rights in the fundamental ideas of a constitution. His one regret about the nineteen thirty-five Act was that what came from it was mostly administrative detail.

F: Fine. So what did India change? Give me real comparisons.

N: Three comparisons from that same speech. Each one is a choice. First, the executive. Ambedkar said a democratic executive should be both stable and responsible, but no system gives both in equal degree. The American and Swiss systems give more stability but less responsibility. The British system gives more responsibility but less stability.

F: Why?

N: Because the American executive does not depend on a majority in the legislature. The British executive does. In Britain, he said, responsibility is assessed daily, by members, through questions, resolutions, no-confidence motions and debates, and periodically by voters at elections. The Draft chose the parliamentary system, preferring more responsibility to more stability.

F: So when the Lok Sabha asks questions in Question Hour, that is the daily assessment.

N: That is it. Second comparison, federation. Ambedkar compared India with the United States. Both are dual polities, a Union and States side by side. But in the United States, the dual polity comes with dual citizenship, a citizenship of the country and of the State. India chose a dual polity with a single citizenship. One citizenship for the whole of India, no State citizenship.

F: Any other difference with America?

N: Yes. In the United States, the States frame their own constitutions. Under the Indian plan, the Union and the States work within a single frame that neither can get out of. States in India do not write separate constitutions of their own.

N: Third comparison, Australia. Ambedkar said every federation suffers from two weaknesses, rigidity and legalism. Australia softened them with a long list of concurrent subjects. He noted that Australia had thirty-nine concurrent subjects, and the Draft had thirty-seven. India followed the Australian plan, he said, but on a far more extensive scale, and added devices of its own.

F: Like what?

N: For example, Parliament's power to legislate on a State subject when it becomes a matter of national concern, after a resolution of the upper House. You will meet that in the Federalism topic.

F: And who holds the leftover powers, the subjects in no list?

N: That is a sharp comparison point. Under Article two hundred and forty-eight, Parliament has exclusive power to make laws on any matter not in the Concurrent List or the State List, including taxes not mentioned in those lists. So residuary power rests with the Union.

F: Was that the same in nineteen thirty-five?

N: No, and UPSC tested it. UPSC Prelims twenty eighteen, General Studies Paper One, question thirty-eight, asked who held residuary powers in the federation set up by the Government of India Act of nineteen thirty-five. The options were the Federal Legislature, the Governor General, the Provincial Legislature and the Provincial Governors. UPSC's key gives the Governor General.

F: I would have said the Federal Legislature. It sounds like the obvious Union body.

N: Most people would, which is why it is a good question. Under the nineteen thirty-five Act, residuary power went to the Governor General. The Constitution moved it to Parliament.

N: Now a recent Indian example of comparison in action. In December twenty twenty-four, the Constitution One Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Amendment Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha. It would empower the Election Commission to hold elections to the Lok Sabha and all State Assemblies together, called simultaneous elections. It was then sent to a Joint Parliamentary Committee for detailed study.

F: How is that a comparison question?

N: Go back to Ambedkar. A parliamentary executive depends on a majority, so a House can be dissolved early, and election cycles drift apart. A presidential system with fixed terms does not drift that way. So the debate on simultaneous elections is partly a debate about how much of the stability side India wants, without losing the responsibility side. Supporters stress lower costs and less disruption from frequent elections. Critics worry about the effect on federalism and on accountability. For Mains, set out both.

N: Here is the trap on this line. Aspirants learn a chart, country on the left, feature on the right, and stop there. Then a Mains question asks them to compare, and they only list. UPSC wants the adaptation. Not just that India took federalism, but that India took it with single citizenship, a single constitutional frame, a long concurrent list and residuary powers with the Union. A second trap is assuming India copied the American federation. Ambedkar himself explained how India differed from it.

N: Three things to keep. First, Ambedkar's answer to the copying charge, on the fourth of November, nineteen forty-eight: the newness of a late constitution lies in variations that remove faults and suit the country's needs. Second, India chose the British-style parliamentary executive for more responsibility, over the American-style executive for more stability. Third, India's federation differs from the American one, with single citizenship and a single frame, and it follows and extends the Australian plan of concurrent powers, with residuary power under Article two hundred and forty-eight resting with Parliament.

N: Now three recall questions.

N: One. In Ambedkar's comparison, which system gives more stability but less responsibility? Is it the American system, or the British system, or neither?

[pause 4s]

N: The American system. Its executive does not depend on a majority in the legislature. The British system gives more responsibility.

N: Two. India's single citizenship was contrasted with dual citizenship in which federation? Australia, or the United States, or Canada?

[pause 4s]

N: The United States, where there is a citizenship of the country and of the State.

N: Three. Under Article two hundred and forty-eight, who holds residuary legislative power? The State Legislatures, or the President, or Parliament?

[pause 4s]

N: Parliament. Under the Government of India Act of nineteen thirty-five, it had been the Governor General.

F: So comparing is really asking, what did we take, and why did we change it.

N: That is the habit this line rewards. The syllabus note on the making of the Constitution in the app has these comparisons in writing, and the flashcards help with the numbers. That is comparison with other constitutions, seen through India's own choices.

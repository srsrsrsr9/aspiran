N: A question for your walk. If the Constitution never uses the words "basic structure", where did the idea come from, and who decides what is inside it?

F: I always assumed it was written somewhere. Some article I had not reached yet.

N: Most people assume that. It is not in any article. It is a doctrine, built by the Supreme Court, one judgment at a time.

N: Start in April nineteen seventy-three. The case is Kesavananda Bharati versus State of Kerala. It began as a writ petition filed in nineteen seventy, against Kerala's land reform laws. By the time it was heard, it had become a test of Parliament's power to amend the Constitution itself.

F: How big was the bench?

N: Thirteen judges. One of them, Justice Beg, noted in his opinion that this bench was larger than any previous bench of the Court. Judgment came on the twenty-fourth of April, nineteen seventy-three.

F: And what did they decide?

N: The core idea is simple to say. Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution. But the power to amend is not a power to destroy. An amendment cannot damage the basic structure, the essential features that make the Constitution what it is.

F: So the basic structure is a limit on amendments.

N: Yes. Keep that word, amendments. The doctrine was built to test constitutional amendments made under Article three hundred and sixty-eight. That is where it bites hardest.

F: Fine. But what is in the basic structure? Did the Court give a list?

N: This is the heart of the episode. Different judges gave examples. Justices Shelat and Grover, for instance, wrote a list. It included the supremacy of the Constitution, a republican and democratic form of government, the secular and federal character of the Constitution, the demarcation of power between the legislature, the executive and the judiciary, and the dignity of the individual. And right before the list, they wrote a short line in brackets in their judgment. These cannot be catalogued but can only be illustrated.

F: Illustrated, not catalogued. So the list is open.

N: Open, and it has grown. The Court names a feature only when a real case puts it in question.

F: Can you give me a case where it actually grew?

N: Two good ones. First, Minerva Mills, nineteen eighty. After the Emergency era, the Forty-second Amendment had added clauses four and five to Article three hundred and sixty-eight. They tried to say no amendment could be questioned in any court, and that Parliament's amending power had no limit at all.

F: That sounds like Parliament switching the doctrine off.

N: That is how the Court read it. Speaking for the majority, Chief Justice Chandrachud said that since the Constitution gave Parliament a limited amending power, Parliament cannot use that limited power to enlarge it into an absolute one. The two clauses were struck down. And a new feature was named. A limited amending power is itself part of the basic structure.

F: And the second case?

N: The National Judicial Appointments Commission case, decided on the sixteenth of October, twenty fifteen. The Constitution Ninety-ninth Amendment Act of twenty fourteen had replaced the collegium system with a commission for appointing judges. The Court asked one question. Does this impinge on the independence of the judiciary, and so violate the basic structure? The majority said it did. The amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act were both struck down.

F: So independence of the judiciary is a basic feature too.

N: Yes. The judgment says this is acknowledged in many decisions and is no longer in doubt.

F: Is the doctrine still alive today, or is it history?

N: Very much alive. Here is a recent example. On the twenty-fifth of November, twenty twenty-four, in Balram Singh versus Union of India, the Supreme Court heard petitions against the words "socialist" and "secular". These words, along with "integrity", were added to the Preamble by the Forty-second Amendment in nineteen seventy-six.

F: What was the argument?

N: Among other things, that the words were added long after the Constitution was adopted in nineteen forty-nine, and that "socialist" ties the hands of elected governments on economic policy. The Court dismissed the petitions. It said the power to amend rests with Parliament and extends to the Preamble. And it said amendments can be challenged on grounds that include violation of the basic structure.

F: So the doctrine was the measuring tape, even though the amendment survived.

N: Exactly. The doctrine does not mean amendments fall. It means they can be tested.

N: Now, a fair warning. The legitimacy of this doctrine is contested, and you should know both sides.

F: Who objects to it?

N: Critics make three points. First, the phrase is nowhere in the text, so they say judges have added a limit the framers did not write. Second, because the list is open, nobody can know in advance what an amendment may not touch. Third, it lets unelected judges overrule a special majority of elected Parliament.

F: And the other side?

N: Supporters reply that a written constitution with a limited amending power needs some guard, or a passing majority could rewrite its identity. They point to Minerva Mills, where the alternative was an amending power with no limit at all. Both views are held by serious people. For Mains, present both, and then give a balanced view of your own.

N: Now the past question. UPSC Prelims twenty nineteen, General Studies Paper One, question forty-five, asked you to judge two statements. One said the Supreme Court struck down the Ninety-ninth Amendment as violating the independence of the judiciary. That one is correct, as you just heard. The other statement was wrong. It said the Forty-fourth Amendment brought in an article placing the Prime Minister's election beyond judicial review.

F: Why was that wrong?

N: Because UPSC swapped the amendment. The amendment that tried to do that was the Thirty-ninth, not the Forty-fourth. Small number, big trap.

N: And here is the usual mistake on this topic. Aspirants treat the basic structure as a fixed list, often learnt from a single table, and pick an option that says the Constitution lists its basic features. It does not. The features are identified by courts, case by case. A second mistake is mixing it with Golaknath. Golaknath said fundamental rights could not be amended at all. Kesavananda said any part can be amended, but not in a way that destroys the basic structure.

N: Three things to keep. First, the basic structure doctrine comes from Kesavananda Bharati, nineteen seventy-three, decided by thirteen judges. Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, but cannot destroy its basic structure. Second, the basic features are illustrated, not catalogued. Courts name them case by case, like a limited amending power in Minerva Mills and independence of the judiciary in the National Judicial Appointments Commission case. Third, the doctrine is still applied, as in Balram Singh in twenty twenty-four, and its legitimacy is debated, so present both sides in Mains.

N: Now three recall questions.

N: One. In which case did the Supreme Court first hold that the amending power cannot destroy the basic structure? Was it Golaknath, or Minerva Mills, or Kesavananda Bharati?

[pause 4s]

N: Kesavananda Bharati, nineteen seventy-three. Golaknath came before it with a different rule, and Minerva Mills built on it later.

N: Two. In the National Judicial Appointments Commission case of twenty fifteen, which amendment was struck down? Was it the Ninety-ninth, or the Forty-second, or the Forty-fourth?

[pause 4s]

N: The Ninety-ninth Amendment of twenty fourteen. The Court held it impinged on the independence of the judiciary.

N: Three. What did Minerva Mills, nineteen eighty, add to the doctrine? Was it that fundamental rights can never be amended, or that a limited amending power is itself a basic feature, or that the Preamble cannot be amended?

[pause 4s]

N: That a limited amending power is itself a basic feature. That is why clauses four and five of Article three hundred and sixty-eight were struck down.

F: So the Constitution can change, but it has to stay recognisably itself.

N: That is the doctrine in a sentence. The syllabus note on the Constitution in the app has the cases in writing, and Mains answer writing is a good place to practise the two sides. That is the basic structure, from nineteen seventy-three to today.

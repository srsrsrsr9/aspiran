N: Here is a question for your walk. India became a republic in nineteen fifty. So why did so many laws from before nineteen fifty keep working the very next morning?

F: Because nobody had time to write new ones?

N: Partly. But the real answer is written into the Constitution itself. And to understand it, we need to walk back through the Acts the British Parliament passed for India. That is what "historical underpinnings" means. The ground the Constitution was built on.

F: Do we have to memorise every Act from seventeen-something?

N: No. Today we take a few moments UPSC actually asks about, and one idea that ties them together. Power in British India was slowly gathered at the centre, then slowly shared with elected Indians, but always on narrow terms. The Constitution kept some of the machinery and changed the foundation.

N: Start with a centre. In eighteen thirty-three, a Charter Act changed one title. The Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India.

F: Just a new name on the door?

N: More than a name. The head of one presidency now became the head of the whole of British India. That is the beginning of a single all-India government. UPSC asked exactly this in Prelims twenty twenty-three, General Studies Paper One, question fifty. Which Act made the Governor-General of Bengal the Governor-General of India? The options were the Regulating Act, Pitt's India Act, the Charter Act of seventeen ninety-three and the Charter Act of eighteen thirty-three. UPSC's official key gives the Charter Act of eighteen thirty-three.

F: And the trap is picking the Regulating Act, because it sounds like the first big one.

N: Exactly. Keep the two titles apart. The eighteen thirty-three Act turned the Bengal office into Governor-General of India.

N: Now jump to nineteen nineteen. The Government of India Act of that year set up a central legislature with two chambers. One was the Council of State, with sixty members. The other was the Legislative Assembly, with one hundred and forty-five members.

F: Two houses. So that is where our Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha come from?

N: Careful. That is the usual shortcut, and it is only half true. The shape looks similar. The spirit was very different. The Council of State was presided over by a President appointed by the Governor-General. And the Indian Statutory Commission, the Simon Commission, described its franchise as extremely restricted. Property qualifications were set high. Electors were mostly grouped in communal constituencies. Women could not vote for the Council of State or stand for it.

F: So a second chamber of wealthy men, chosen by a tiny electorate.

N: That is a fair summary of what the Commission itself observed. Now the big one. The Government of India Act of nineteen thirty-five.

F: The Act everyone says the Constitution copied.

N: Hold that thought. First, what it planned. It planned a federation, joining British India and the Indian States, the princely states. At the centre, a federal legislature with two houses. A Council of State of two hundred and sixty members, one hundred and fifty-six from British India and one hundred and four from the Indian States. A Federal Assembly of three hundred and seventy-five members, two hundred and fifty from British India and one hundred and twenty-five from the States. The Council of State was to be permanent, with about one-third retiring every third year.

F: That sounds a lot like the Rajya Sabha today. A permanent house, one-third retiring.

N: Good catch. That idea of a continuing house does echo. But here is the fact many aspirants forget. The federal scheme of nineteen thirty-five never came into operation.

F: So the federal part stayed on paper.

N: Yes. And one more nineteen thirty-five fact that UPSC has tested. Prelims twenty eighteen, General Studies Paper One, question thirty-eight asked who got residuary powers in the federation under that Act. The options were the Federal Legislature, the Governor-General, the Provincial Legislature and the Provincial Governors. UPSC's key gives the Governor-General.

F: Residuary powers means powers over subjects not in any list?

N: Right. Today, under Article two hundred and forty-eight, Parliament holds them. In nineteen thirty-five, they sat with the Governor-General. That one contrast tells you how much the foundation changed.

N: So now your earlier thought. Did the Constitution copy the nineteen thirty-five Act?

F: Most books seem to say yes.

N: Here is the fair version. When the Constituent Assembly designed Parliament, it had the working of the nineteen nineteen legislature in front of it, and the nineteen thirty-five plan, and models from other countries. The Union Constitution Committee, chaired by Jawaharlal Nehru, reported in July nineteen forty-seven, with proposals for a second chamber. The Rajya Sabha's own account of its history says the old machinery could not furnish a satisfactory basis for independent India's legislature.

F: So they borrowed the frame but rebuilt the house.

N: That is a good way to carry it. Some scholars stress how much administrative detail carried over. Others stress how the source of power changed. In a Mains answer, give both, and show you know the difference.

N: Now back to our opening question. Why did old laws keep working in nineteen fifty? Two Articles answer it. Article three hundred and ninety-five repeals the Indian Independence Act of nineteen forty-seven and the Government of India Act of nineteen thirty-five, with the Acts amending it.

F: So the two big constitutional Acts were switched off.

N: Yes. But Article three hundred and seventy-two keeps every other law in force on the day the Constitution began. Those laws continue until a competent legislature or authority alters, repeals or amends them. The President was also allowed, for the first three years, to adapt such laws to fit the Constitution.

N: So the courts did not wake up to an empty law book. And Parliament has kept changing that inherited law book ever since. A recent example. On the first of July twenty twenty-four, three new criminal laws came into force across India: the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, all of twenty twenty-three. They took the place of the older criminal codes. That is Article three hundred and seventy-two at work: old law runs until a legislature replaces it.

N: Let us gather what to keep.

N: First. Eighteen thirty-three made the Governor-General of Bengal the Governor-General of India. Nineteen nineteen gave a two-chamber central legislature with a very narrow franchise.

N: Second. The nineteen thirty-five Act planned a federation with a two-house federal legislature, but the federal scheme never came into operation. Residuary powers were with the Governor-General.

N: Third. Article three hundred and ninety-five repealed the nineteen thirty-five Act and the Indian Independence Act. Article three hundred and seventy-two kept all other existing laws alive until changed.

N: Now three quick questions. Answer in your head.

N: Question one. Under the Government of India Act of nineteen nineteen, the central legislature had how many chambers? Was it two, or one, or three?

[pause 4s]

N: Two. The Council of State and the Legislative Assembly.

N: Question two. Which Article of the Constitution repeals the Government of India Act of nineteen thirty-five? Is it Article three hundred and seventy-two, or Article three hundred and ninety-five, or Article three hundred and sixty-eight?

[pause 4s]

N: Article three hundred and ninety-five. Article three hundred and seventy-two keeps other existing laws in force, and Article three hundred and sixty-eight is the amending power.

N: Question three. In the federation planned by the nineteen thirty-five Act, who held residuary powers? Was it the Federal Legislature, or the Provincial Legislature, or the Governor-General?

[pause 4s]

N: The Governor-General. Today, under our Constitution, it is Parliament.

N: That is the ground beneath the Constitution: a centre built over a century, a narrow franchise, a federation that stayed on paper, and two Articles that switched off the old Acts while keeping the old laws running.

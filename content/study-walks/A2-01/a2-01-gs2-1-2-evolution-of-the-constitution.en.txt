N: Here is a question for your walk. India's Constitution has two birthdays, two months apart. Which one is the real one?

F: Twenty-sixth January, surely. That is Republic Day.

N: Hold on to that answer. By the end of this walk you will see why both dates matter, and why UPSC likes to mix them up. Today's line is the evolution of the Constitution. How a group of people in one hall turned years of argument into a document that still runs the country.

F: So this is the Constituent Assembly story.

N: Yes. Let us begin with the hall itself. On the twenty-sixth of November, twenty twenty-four, President Droupadi Murmu stood in the Central Hall of the old Parliament building, now called Samvidhan Sadan. It was the seventy-fifth anniversary of the Constitution's adoption. And she reminded the gathering that on that very day, seventy-five years earlier, in that very hall, the Constituent Assembly finished framing the Constitution.

F: So the Constitution was finished in the same room where Parliament later sat.

N: Exactly. The President also said something worth carrying into an answer. In one sense, the Constitution came out of nearly three years of deliberation. In the true sense, she said, it came out of the long freedom struggle. Its ideals were already in the national movement. The Preamble sums them up: justice, liberty, equality and fraternity.

F: Nearly three years. That is a long time to write one document.

N: It is. And think of who was in the room. According to the Press Information Bureau, the Assembly began with three hundred and eighty-nine members. After Partition, it came down to two hundred and ninety-nine. The members came from the provinces, from the princely states and from the chief commissioners' provinces.

F: So it was not only people elected from British India.

N: Right. And among them were fifteen women. PIB notes that they argued for equal rights and for votes for all, at a time when women in government were rare even in rich democracies.

F: Fifteen out of nearly three hundred. Small, but there.

N: Small in number, and important in what they pushed for. Now, the key idea of this line. The Constitution was not written in one go and dropped on the country. It evolved. It was debated clause by clause, drafted, sent back, refined. PIB describes years of tireless debate, drafting and refining. That is why it is often called a living document. It was built through argument, and it was built to keep changing.

N: Now the two birthdays. On the twenty-sixth of November, nineteen forty-nine, the Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution. In the words of the Preamble, the people of India adopted, enacted and gave to themselves this Constitution.

F: Then why do we celebrate the twenty-sixth of January?

N: Because of Article three hundred and ninety-four. Read it slowly, because it answers the riddle. It says that this Article, and a short list of others, come into force at once. The rest of the Constitution comes into force on the twenty-sixth of January, nineteen fifty. And it calls that day the commencement of the Constitution.

F: So some Articles started working in November nineteen forty-nine itself?

N: Yes. The list includes Articles five to nine, on citizenship. Article three hundred and twenty-four, on the Election Commission. Articles three hundred and sixty-six and three hundred and sixty-seven, on definitions and interpretation. And a few provisions for the provisional Parliament and the provisional President, which were needed to run the new system until the first elections.

F: That makes sense. You need to know who the citizens are, and who will run elections, before the elections.

N: Exactly the logic. So the twenty-sixth of November is the day of adoption. The twenty-sixth of January is the day of commencement. Both are real birthdays, for different reasons.

F: And the twenty-sixth of November is Constitution Day?

N: Yes. Samvidhan Divas has been observed every year since twenty fifteen. PIB says it is a day for citizens to reflect on their rights and duties, with readings of the Preamble in schools and offices. And two months after the seventy-fifth Constitution Day, on the twenty-sixth of January, twenty twenty-five, the Republic itself turned seventy-five.

F: Now I see where an exam could trick me.

N: It did. UPSC Prelims twenty twenty-three, General Studies Paper One, question eighty-five. Statement one said Constitution Day is celebrated on the twenty-sixth of November every year to promote constitutional values. Statement two said that on the twenty-sixth of November, nineteen forty-nine, the Constituent Assembly set up a Drafting Committee under Dr B R Ambedkar to prepare a draft.

F: Both sound right to me.

N: That is the trap. UPSC's official key says statement one is correct, and statement two is incorrect. The twenty-sixth of November, nineteen forty-nine, is the day the Constitution was adopted. It is not the day the Drafting Committee was set up. A committee that prepares the draft has to exist long before the final adoption.

F: So the mistake is attaching the right person to the wrong event.

N: Yes. Here is the general trap for this whole line. Aspirants remember dates as a loose cloud. Then UPSC swaps one event into another event's date. The cure is to attach each date to one verb. Adopted, on the twenty-sixth of November nineteen forty-nine. Commenced, on the twenty-sixth of January nineteen fifty. Constitution Day, observed since twenty fifteen.

F: One date, one verb. I like that.

N: One more image to keep. The original handwritten copies of the Constitution are preserved in helium-filled cases in the Parliament Library. The document you study as text is also a physical object that people still guard with care.

F: And does the evolution stop in nineteen fifty?

N: No. The making ended in nineteen fifty, but the document kept evolving through amendments and through court rulings. Those are their own story. For this line, hold on to the making: who sat in the Assembly, how long it took, and how the Constitution came into force in two steps.

N: Let us gather what to keep.

N: First. The Constituent Assembly worked for nearly three years. It began with three hundred and eighty-nine members, came down to two hundred and ninety-nine after Partition, and included fifteen women.

N: Second. The Constitution was adopted on the twenty-sixth of November, nineteen forty-nine. Under Article three hundred and ninety-four, a few Articles, including citizenship and the Election Commission, came into force at once. The rest commenced on the twenty-sixth of January, nineteen fifty.

N: Third. Constitution Day, Samvidhan Divas, falls on the twenty-sixth of November and has been observed since twenty fifteen. The seventy-fifth anniversary of adoption was marked in the Central Hall of Samvidhan Sadan in twenty twenty-four.

N: Now three quick questions.

N: Question one. On which date did the Constituent Assembly adopt the Constitution? Was it the twenty-sixth of January nineteen fifty, or the twenty-sixth of November nineteen forty-nine, or the fifteenth of August nineteen forty-seven?

[pause 4s]

N: The twenty-sixth of November, nineteen forty-nine. The twenty-sixth of January nineteen fifty is the date of commencement.

N: Question two. Which Article lists the provisions that came into force at once, before the rest of the Constitution? Is it Article three hundred and ninety-five, or Article three hundred and sixty-eight, or Article three hundred and ninety-four?

[pause 4s]

N: Article three hundred and ninety-four, titled Commencement. It also names the twenty-sixth of January nineteen fifty as the commencement of the Constitution.

N: Question three. Since which year has the twenty-sixth of November been observed as Constitution Day? Is it twenty fifteen, or nineteen fifty, or two thousand and five?

[pause 4s]

N: Twenty fifteen. PIB and the President's office both date Samvidhan Divas from that year.

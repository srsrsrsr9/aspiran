--- part 1 ---
The monsoon from start to finish.

What a monsoon is.

Welcome. In this talk we will follow the Indian monsoon through one whole year, from its first stirrings in summer to its departure in autumn. Close your eyes if you like. It is a story that is easy to picture.

The word monsoon comes from the Arabic word mausim, which means season. At its core, a monsoon is a seasonal reversal of winds. In summer, winds over much of South Asia blow from the sea towards the land. In winter, they blow from the land towards the sea. With the summer winds comes most of India's rain. Across the country, roughly three quarters of the year's rainfall arrives between June and September.

That is why the monsoon matters so much. It fills reservoirs, recharges groundwater and waters a large share of the crops sown in the kharif season. It shapes farm incomes, food prices and even electricity from hydropower. When the monsoon is good, much of the economy breathes easier.

For a long time, the monsoon was explained in one simple way: land heats faster than sea. That idea is still the starting point, but we now know the story has more characters. Let us meet them one by one.

The build-up in summer.

We are now in the second part: the months of April and May, when the stage is being set.

As the sun moves north, the land of the subcontinent heats up quickly. The northwest of India and the plains next to it become very hot. Warm air rises, and a low pressure area forms over this region.

--- part 2 ---
The seas around India heat more slowly, so pressure over them stays relatively higher. Air tends to move from high pressure to low pressure, so the stage is set for winds to flow from the sea towards the land.

At the same time, a band of rising air and clouds near the equator, called the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, shifts north with the sun. Over India in summer, this zone settles over the northern plains as the monsoon trough. Its position matters a great deal. When the trough sits further south, rains are heavier over central India.

The trade winds from the southern hemisphere, which normally blow from the southeast, cross the equator to reach this low. As they cross, the rotation of the earth turns them, so they arrive from the southwest. That is why our summer monsoon is called the southwest monsoon. A strong, low-level current of these winds runs along the coast of East Africa, close to Somalia. It is known as the Somali jet, and it feeds moisture into the Arabian Sea branch.

High above, the winds change too. The Tibetan plateau acts like a raised hot plate. It heats the air above it, which helps set up a strong easterly wind high in the atmosphere over southern India, called the tropical easterly jet. At the same time, the subtropical westerly jet, which in winter flows south of the Himalayas, shifts north of them. Many scientists see this northward shift as a key signal that the monsoon is ready to begin.

Notice that no single factor does all the work.

--- part 3 ---
Land heating, the shifting convergence zone, the cross-equatorial winds and the jets in the upper air work together, like instruments in an orchestra. If one of them is weak in a given year, the whole performance changes.

Onset and advance.

Let us move to the third part: the arrival.

The monsoon usually reaches the coast of Kerala around the first of June. The India Meteorological Department declares onset only when certain checks are met, such as rainfall at a set of stations, the depth of westerly winds, and the amount of outgoing heat seen by satellites. The date varies from year to year by about a week either side.

From Kerala, the monsoon advances in two branches. The Arabian Sea branch moves up the west coast. When these moist winds meet the Western Ghats, they are forced to rise. Rising air cools, and cooler air holds less moisture, so heavy rain falls on the windward slopes. This is called orographic rain. Once the winds cross the Ghats and descend on the other side, they warm up and become drier. That is why places in the interior of Maharashtra and Karnataka, in the rain shadow, receive much less rain.

The Bay of Bengal branch moves towards the northeast. Part of it strikes the hills of the northeast, where it brings very heavy rain. Mawsynram and Cherrapunji, in the Khasi hills of Meghalaya, are among the wettest places on earth, partly because the hills there form a funnel that traps the moist winds.

--- part 4 ---
The Himalayas then turn this branch westward, along the Ganga plains. So rainfall generally decreases as we move from east to west across the northern plains.

The two branches meet over the northern plains, and the monsoon covers the whole country, usually by about the eighth of July. That normal date was revised a few years ago, from mid July, after new data were studied. Areas in the far northwest, such as western Rajasthan, receive the least rain.

Inside the season, rain does not fall every day. There are wet spells and dry spells. A long dry spell during the season is called a break in the monsoon. Breaks often happen when the monsoon trough shifts north towards the foothills of the Himalayas. Then the plains stay dry while the hills get heavy rain. Low pressure systems from the Bay of Bengal, moving west across central India, bring many of the good rainy spells.

Before we leave the summer season, one more thing is worth noticing. Even a normal monsoon can be uneven. A district may get its whole month's rain in a few days, with floods, and then go dry for weeks. For farmers and for planners, the spread of rain across time and space matters as much as the total. That is why the India Meteorological Department reports rainfall not only for the country, but also for four broad regions and for each meteorological subdivision and district.

Withdrawal and the northeast monsoon.

We are now in the fourth part: how the season ends, and how one more rain season follows.

--- part 5 ---
As the sun moves south, the land cools, the low pressure over the northwest weakens, and the winds begin to change. The monsoon starts to withdraw from western Rajasthan, usually around the middle of September. It then retreats step by step, and normally leaves the whole country by about the middle of October.

This period, October and November, is often called the season of the retreating monsoon. Skies clear, but days can be warm and humid. You may have heard the phrase October heat for this.

Now comes a twist. As the winds reverse, they blow from the northeast. When they cross the Bay of Bengal, they pick up moisture. They then bring rain to the southeastern coast, especially Tamil Nadu, coastal Andhra Pradesh and south interior Karnataka, between October and December. This is the northeast monsoon. For Tamil Nadu, it is the main rainy season of the year. This season is also the peak time for cyclones in the Bay of Bengal, which can bring heavy rain and strong winds to the eastern coast.

So India, in a sense, has two monsoons. The southwest monsoon of summer waters most of the country. The northeast monsoon of early winter waters the southeast.

What makes a monsoon strong or weak, and a recap.

We are in the last part. Why is one year's monsoon generous and another's disappointing?

Several large patterns play a role. The first is El Niño and La Niña in the Pacific Ocean. During El Niño, waters in the central and eastern Pacific become unusually warm.

--- part 6 ---
This shifts patterns of rising and sinking air across the tropics, and in many El Niño years, India's monsoon has been weaker. La Niña, the cooler phase, has often come with good rains. The link is strong, but it is not a rule; some El Niño years have still had normal rain.

The second is the Indian Ocean Dipole. When the western Indian Ocean is warmer than the eastern part, it is called a positive dipole, and it tends to support the Indian monsoon. A negative dipole tends to do the opposite. A positive dipole can sometimes soften the effect of an El Niño.

The third is the Madden Julian Oscillation, a band of clouds and rain that moves eastward around the tropics every month or two. When it passes over the Indian Ocean, it can bring a burst of active rain.

Forecasters combine all this. The India Meteorological Department issues a first long-range forecast in April and an update around the end of May. It describes the season's rain against the long period average, which is the average rainfall over many years. Rain between ninety-six and one hundred and four per cent of that average is called normal.

Now a short recap. A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of winds. In summer, a low forms over the heated northwest, the convergence zone moves north, and trade winds cross the equator to arrive from the southwest. The monsoon reaches Kerala around the first of June, advances in an Arabian Sea branch and a Bay of Bengal branch, and covers the country by about the eighth of July.

--- part 7 ---
It withdraws from mid September to mid October. Then the northeast monsoon brings rain to Tamil Nadu and nearby areas. And El Niño, the Indian Ocean Dipole and the Madden Julian Oscillation help decide how strong it will be.

The next time the first rain arrives, you will know the long journey behind it.

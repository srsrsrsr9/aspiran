--- part 1 ---
Ethics: emotional intelligence for administrators.

What emotional intelligence is.

Welcome. In this talk we will explore emotional intelligence, a topic named directly in the ethics syllabus. Let us keep it practical and close to the work of a public servant.

Start with a simple description. Emotional intelligence is the ability to notice, understand and manage emotions, in yourself and in others, and to use that understanding to think and act well.

Notice that it has two directions. One faces inward: knowing what you feel and managing it. The other faces outward: sensing what others feel and responding with care. An administrator needs both. A collector who is calm inside but blind to the fear of flood-hit families will struggle. So will one who feels deeply for others but loses their temper under pressure.

The idea took shape in psychology. In nineteen ninety, the researchers Peter Salovey and John Mayer described emotional intelligence as a set of abilities. In nineteen ninety-five, the writer and psychologist Daniel Goleman popularised the idea through a widely read book. Since then, it has been used in leadership training around the world, including in Indian administrative training.

It helps to compare emotional intelligence with the more familiar idea of intelligence, often measured as an intelligence quotient. That kind of intelligence is about reasoning, memory and problem solving. Emotional intelligence is about handling feelings and relationships.

--- part 2 ---
The two are different, and a person can be strong in one and weaker in the other. Many studies of leadership suggest that once people reach a certain level of technical skill, emotional and social abilities make much of the difference in how well they lead.

A quick note on why it matters for ethics. Ethical decisions are not made by logic alone. Fear, anger, pride and sympathy all push and pull. Emotional intelligence does not replace values; it helps us act on our values when emotions run high.

The models, in plain words.

We are now in the second part: the main models. There are two that most aspirants meet.

The first is the ability model of Mayer and Salovey. It describes four abilities, building from the simplest to the most complex. One, perceiving emotions: noticing feelings in faces, voices and in oneself. Two, using emotions: letting feelings help thinking, for example, a little worry can help us check our work. Three, understanding emotions: knowing how feelings arise and change, such as how frustration can grow into anger. Four, managing emotions: regulating one's own feelings and helping others with theirs.

The second is Goleman's model, which describes five components. Self-awareness is knowing your own emotions, strengths and limits. Self-regulation is managing impulses and staying calm and fair. Motivation is an inner drive to do good work, beyond reward or status. Empathy is understanding the feelings and needs of others.

--- part 3 ---
Social skills include communicating well, resolving conflict and building trust.

A useful way to remember Goleman's five is to group them. The first three are about managing yourself. The last two are about relating to others.

Let us see all five in one small scene. An officer chairs a review meeting where a project is badly delayed. Self-awareness lets them notice their own rising irritation. Self-regulation keeps their voice steady. Motivation keeps the focus on finishing the project for the people it serves, not on blaming. Empathy helps them sense that the junior engineer is anxious, not careless. And social skills help them close the meeting with a clear plan that everyone accepts.

You do not need to choose one model over the other in an answer. It is fine to say that the ability model treats emotional intelligence as a set of skills that can be measured, while Goleman's broader model links it to leadership and performance. What examiners usually value is how you apply the idea.

Emotional intelligence at work in administration.

Let us move to the third part, which is the heart of this talk: how emotional intelligence shows up in real administrative work. We will use a few imagined situations.

First, a crisis. A district faces sudden floods. Phones ring nonstop, information is incomplete, and senior officers want updates. Self-regulation helps the officer stay calm, so that decisions on evacuation and relief are clear and not driven by panic.

--- part 4 ---
Empathy helps them speak to frightened families in a way that reassures rather than dismisses. And social skills help them coordinate the police, health teams, volunteers and local leaders, many of whom are also under stress.

Second, a grievance. An elderly woman has been coming to an office for months about a pension that has not started. She is angry and raises her voice. A less aware officer may feel insulted and respond coldly. An emotionally intelligent officer recognises that her anger comes from long frustration and real need. They listen first, acknowledge the delay, and then act. The rule does not change, but the experience of the citizen changes completely.

Third, a team. A new officer joins a department where staff feel overworked and unheard. Motivation and empathy help the officer understand what lies behind low morale. Perhaps it is unclear duties or no recognition. Small steps, such as listening sessions, fair sharing of work and sincere appreciation, can rebuild trust.

Fourth, a conflict between groups. Two communities disagree over the use of a common water source. Emotions run high, and rumours spread. Emotional intelligence helps the administrator keep a neutral tone, hear each side fully, and choose words that lower tension rather than raise it.

Fifth, the officer's own wellbeing. Public service can be stressful, with long hours and hard choices. Self-awareness helps officers notice signs of burnout in themselves and seek rest or support.

--- part 5 ---
An exhausted officer is more likely to make poor decisions, so caring for oneself is also a public duty.

Sixth, public criticism. Today a decision can be criticised widely within hours, sometimes fairly and sometimes not. Emotional intelligence helps an officer separate the useful point inside a criticism from the noise around it. Instead of reacting defensively, the officer can check the facts, correct a genuine mistake quickly, and explain the decision calmly where it was sound. That steadiness protects both the officer and public trust in the institution.

Limits, misuse and how to build it.

We are now in the fourth part. Every useful idea has limits, and a balanced answer names them.

First, emotional intelligence is not the same as being nice. Sometimes the emotionally intelligent choice is to say no firmly, to deliver bad news, or to take disciplinary action. Kindness in tone does not mean softness in duty.

Second, it can be misused. A person who reads emotions well can also use that skill to manipulate others, to flatter, or to win loyalty for selfish ends. That is why emotional intelligence must be guided by values such as integrity and fairness. Skill without conscience is not ethical strength.

Third, empathy can be partial. We tend to feel more for people who are like us, or who are in front of us. An administrator has to extend empathy to those who are far away, unheard or different. Good policy depends on this wider circle of concern.

--- part 6 ---
Fourth, it should not replace rules and evidence. Feeling strongly about a case does not mean the case is right. Emotional intelligence works best together with sound procedure and good data.

Now, how can emotional intelligence be built? Research and experience suggest it can grow with practice. A few gentle habits help. Pause before reacting, even for one breath. Name the emotion you feel; naming it often reduces its grip. Listen fully before replying, and try to summarise the other person's view in your own words. Ask for honest feedback from colleagues you trust. Reflect at the end of the day on one interaction that went well and one that did not. Many training institutes also use role plays, case discussions and field visits for this purpose.

Indian traditions also speak of this inner balance. The Bhagavad Gita describes the sthitaprajna, a person of steady wisdom who is not shaken by pleasure or pain, praise or blame. You do not need to quote scripture in an answer, but the image is helpful. It reminds us that emotional balance is not the absence of feeling. It is the ability to feel fully and still act wisely.

A recap.

We have reached the last part. Let us bring everything together.

Emotional intelligence is the ability to perceive, understand and manage emotions, in oneself and in others, and to use that understanding to act well. The idea was described by Salovey and Mayer in nineteen ninety and popularised by Goleman in nineteen ninety-five.

--- part 7 ---
The ability model has four branches: perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions. Goleman's model has five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills.

In administration, emotional intelligence helps officers stay calm in crises, treat citizens with dignity, lead teams, defuse conflicts and look after their own wellbeing. It has limits: it is not the same as being nice, it can be misused without values, empathy can be partial, and it should work with rules and evidence, not replace them. And it can be developed through small, steady habits.

As you go through your day, try one small experiment. Before your next reply in a tense moment, pause for a breath and name the feeling. You will be practising exactly what this talk describes.

Three questions to check yourself. Pause after each one and choose an answer.

Question one. Which of these is one of the four branches of the ability model of Mayer and Salovey?

Option one: Motivation. Option two: Leadership. Option three: Understanding emotions. Option four: Social status.

Question two. A citizen shouts at an officer over a long-delayed pension. Which response best shows emotional intelligence?

Option one: Ask the citizen to leave until they calm down. Option two: Promise to break the rules to release the pension today. Option three: Listen first, acknowledge the delay, then explain and act within the rules. Option four: Reply in the same tone to show authority.

--- part 8 ---
Question three. Why should emotional intelligence be guided by values such as integrity?

Option one: Because it cannot be learned. Option two: Because the skill of reading emotions can also be used to manipulate others. Option three: Because it only matters in private life. Option four: Because rules make it unnecessary.

Now the answers. Question one: the answer is option three. The ability model has four branches: perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions; motivation belongs to Goleman's model.

Now the answers. Question two: the answer is option three. Recognising the frustration behind the anger, listening and then acting within the rules combines empathy, self-regulation and duty.

Now the answers. Question three: the answer is option two. Reading and influencing emotions is a powerful skill; without values, it can be used for flattery or manipulation instead of public good.

That is the end of this talk. Thank you for listening.

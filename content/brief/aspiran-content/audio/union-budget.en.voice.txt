--- part 1 ---
Reading the Union Budget.

Why the Budget matters.

Welcome. In the next twelve minutes or so, we will learn to read the Union Budget calmly, the way an administrator would. No formulas to memorise, just a clear map.

Let us start with a simple idea. A Budget is a plan for one year. It says how much money the Government of India expects to receive, how much it plans to spend, and how it will fill the gap between the two. That year is the financial year, which runs from the first of April to the thirty-first of March.

Interestingly, the word Budget does not appear in the Constitution. Article one hundred and twelve calls it the Annual Financial Statement. It asks the President to cause this statement to be laid before both Houses of Parliament every year. In practice, the Finance Minister presents it in the Lok Sabha.

Since twenty seventeen, the Budget has been presented on the first of February. Before that it came at the end of February. The earlier date gives Parliament time to pass it before the new financial year starts on the first of April. Also from twenty seventeen, the separate Railway Budget was merged into the Union Budget.

Why should an aspirant care? Because the Budget connects many parts of the syllabus. It touches economy, polity, schemes, agriculture, defence and welfare. Once you can read it, a lot of news starts to make sense.

The documents in the Budget bag.

We are now in the second part: what is actually inside the Budget.

--- part 2 ---
On Budget day, the Finance Minister reads a speech. But the speech is only the front door. Behind it sits a set of documents.

The first is the Annual Financial Statement itself. It shows the estimated receipts and spending of the Government for the coming year, and it is arranged around three funds. The Consolidated Fund of India, under Article two hundred and sixty-six, holds almost all government money. No money can leave it without a law passed by Parliament. The Contingency Fund, under Article two hundred and sixty-seven, is a small fund for urgent, unforeseen spending, placed at the disposal of the President. And the Public Account holds money the Government keeps in trust, such as small savings and provident funds.

The second document is the Demands for Grants. Article one hundred and thirteen says that spending from the Consolidated Fund must be put to the Lok Sabha as demands. Usually there is one demand for each ministry or department.

The third is the Finance Bill. It carries the tax proposals for the year: new taxes, changed rates and exemptions. Alongside it comes a memorandum that explains each change in plain terms.

There are also helpful explainers. The Receipt Budget shows where money comes from. The Expenditure Budget shows where it goes. And under the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, the Government lays statements on the medium-term fiscal policy and the macroeconomic framework.

--- part 3 ---
These tell you the Government's own targets for deficits and debt.

One more document is worth knowing, though it is not part of the Budget. The Economic Survey is usually tabled a day or so before the Budget. It is prepared in the Ministry of Finance under the Chief Economic Adviser. It reviews how the economy did over the past year and discusses big policy ideas. Think of the Survey as the report card and the Budget as the plan. Reading the Survey's overview chapter first often makes the Budget easier to follow.

A gentle tip. You do not need to read every page. The Budget at a Glance document puts the key numbers on a few pages. Most aspirants find it the best place to start.

The numbers that matter.

Now we come to the third part, the numbers. Let us take them one at a time.

First, receipts. Receipts are of two kinds. Revenue receipts do not create a liability. Taxes are the biggest share, along with non-tax revenue such as dividends and fees. Capital receipts either create a liability, like borrowing, or reduce assets, like selling a stake in a public sector company, which we call disinvestment.

Second, spending. Revenue expenditure keeps the government running. Salaries, interest payments, pensions and subsidies are all revenue spending. They do not create assets. Capital expenditure builds assets or reduces liabilities. Roads, railway lines and loans given to states are good examples.

--- part 4 ---
Economists often watch capital expenditure closely, because it tends to support growth in the years that follow.

Third, the deficits. Here is the simplest way to hold them in your head.

Revenue deficit is revenue spending minus revenue receipts. It tells you whether the Government is borrowing just to meet its day-to-day costs.

Fiscal deficit is total spending minus total receipts, not counting borrowing. Put simply, it is how much the Government needs to borrow in the year. It is usually shown as a share of gross domestic product.

Primary deficit is the fiscal deficit minus interest payments. It removes the burden of old debt and shows whether this year's policies, by themselves, are adding to borrowing.

A small exercise. Imagine the fiscal deficit is large, but most of it is interest on past loans. Then the primary deficit will be small. That tells you the current year is careful, even though old debt is heavy. Reading the two together gives a fairer picture.

A related idea is debt. Each year's fiscal deficit adds to the stock of government debt. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management framework asks the Government to keep both the deficit and the debt on a declining path over time. So when you read a Budget, notice two things together: the deficit for the year, and what the Government says about debt as a share of the economy in the medium term. A falling deficit with a rising debt ratio, or the other way round, is worth a second look.

Fourth, the shares.

--- part 5 ---
Many newspapers show a rupee coming in and a rupee going out. This tells you, for every rupee, how much comes from each tax or borrowing, and how much goes to interest, defence, subsidies, transfers to states and so on. It is an easy, visual way to see priorities.

From speech to law.

Let us move to the fourth part: how Parliament turns the Budget into law. This is where polity meets economy.

After the speech, there is a general discussion in both Houses. Members talk about the Budget as a whole. No voting happens at this stage.

Then Parliament usually breaks for a few weeks. During this time, the departmentally related standing committees study the Demands for Grants of the ministries they watch. They report back to the Houses. This gives detailed scrutiny without using up the time of the full House.

Next comes voting on the Demands for Grants. Only the Lok Sabha votes on them. The Rajya Sabha can discuss them, but cannot vote. Members can move cut motions to reduce a demand. A policy cut asks to reduce the demand to one rupee, to show disapproval of a policy. An economy cut asks for a specific reduction. A token cut asks to reduce it by one hundred rupees, to raise a particular grievance.

There is a limited number of days for this. On the last day, the Speaker puts all remaining demands to vote together. This is called the guillotine.

Some spending is not voted at all. It is called charged expenditure.

--- part 6 ---
Article one hundred and twelve lists examples, such as the salary of the President and of judges of the Supreme Court. Parliament can discuss these items but does not vote on them. This protects certain offices from political pressure.

After the demands are passed, the Appropriation Bill is introduced under Article one hundred and fourteen. It allows the Government to take money out of the Consolidated Fund. Finally, the Finance Bill is passed, which makes the tax proposals law.

What if all this is not finished by the first of April? Article one hundred and sixteen allows a vote on account. Parliament grants money for a part of the year so that the Government can keep running. In an election year, a vote on account or an interim budget is common, and the full Budget follows after the new Lok Sabha meets.

A simple way to read any Budget, and a recap.

We are in the last part. Let us put everything together into a calm routine you can use for any year.

Step one. Start with the fiscal deficit target and compare it with the previous year. Is the Government planning to borrow more or less, as a share of the economy?

Step two. Look at the balance between revenue and capital spending. Is capital spending rising?

Step three. Look at the rupee going out. Which items take the biggest shares?

Step four. Read the Finance Bill memorandum for major tax changes.

Step five. Pick two or three ministries linked to your interests, perhaps agriculture or health, and read their demands.

--- part 7 ---
That is it. Five steps, perhaps an hour of reading.

Now a short recap. The Budget is the Annual Financial Statement under Article one hundred and twelve. It is presented on the first of February. Its core documents are the statement itself, the Demands for Grants and the Finance Bill. Revenue items do not create assets or liabilities; capital items do. The fiscal deficit is the year's borrowing need, and the primary deficit removes interest from it. And Parliament passes the Budget through general discussion, committee scrutiny, voting on demands, the Appropriation Bill and the Finance Bill.

Take your time with it. The Budget is easier than it looks once you know where to stand.

Three questions to check yourself. Pause after each one and choose an answer.

Question one. Which Article of the Constitution refers to the Budget as the Annual Financial Statement?

Option one: Article one hundred and ten. Option two: Article one hundred and twelve. Option three: Article one hundred and fourteen. Option four: Article two hundred and sixty-six.

Question two. If interest payments are subtracted from the fiscal deficit, what do we get?

Option one: Revenue deficit. Option two: Effective revenue deficit. Option three: Primary deficit. Option four: Budget deficit.

Question three. Which statement about voting on the Demands for Grants is correct?

Option one: Both Houses vote on every demand. Option two: The Rajya Sabha can reject a demand passed by the Lok Sabha.

--- part 8 ---
Option three: Only the Lok Sabha votes on the demands, and charged expenditure is not voted. Option four: Charged expenditure is voted on the last day under the guillotine.

Now the answers. Question one: the answer is option two. Article one hundred and twelve asks for an annual statement of estimated receipts and spending to be laid before both Houses.

Now the answers. Question two: the answer is option three. Primary deficit is the fiscal deficit minus interest payments, so it shows the borrowing caused by the current year's policies.

Now the answers. Question three: the answer is option three. Under Article one hundred and thirteen the demands are voted by the Lok Sabha alone, and charged items are only discussed, not voted.

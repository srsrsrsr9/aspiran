--- part 1 ---
Ethics: integrity, probity and accountability.

Three words that are often mixed up.

Welcome. In this talk we will spend some quiet time with three words that appear again and again in the ethics paper: integrity, probity and accountability. They sound alike, and many people use them as if they mean the same thing. By the end, you will hear the difference clearly.

Here is a simple way to begin. Integrity is about the person. Probity is about how that person handles public duties and public money. Accountability is about the system that asks the person to explain and answer for what was done.

Imagine three circles. The innermost circle is integrity, your own values and character. The middle circle is probity, your conduct in office. The outer circle is accountability, the rules, institutions and citizens who hold you to account. A good public servant lives well in all three.

We will take each circle in turn, then see how they work together, and finally think about how to use them in an answer.

Integrity, the inner circle.

We are now in the second part: integrity.

The word integrity comes from the Latin integer, which means whole or undivided. A person of integrity is whole. Their values, their words and their actions match. They behave the same way whether or not anyone is watching.

Integrity is more than honesty, though honesty is part of it. Honesty is about telling the truth. Integrity is about living by your principles consistently, even when it is costly.

--- part 2 ---
Someone might never tell a lie, yet quietly bend rules for a friend. That person is truthful, but their integrity has a crack.

Integrity has a few parts worth naming. First, consistency: the same standards in small matters and large ones. Second, courage: the willingness to say no, or to speak up, when something is wrong. Third, humility: the readiness to admit a mistake. A person of integrity does not need to be perfect. They need to be honest with themselves.

Let us take an imagined example. A young officer is asked by a senior to backdate a file so that a favoured contractor qualifies for a tender. No one would ever find out. Integrity is what helps the officer refuse, politely but firmly, and record the instruction properly. The pressure is real, and refusing may be uncomfortable. Integrity does not remove the discomfort; it gives the officer a steady place to stand.

Integrity also has an intellectual side. Intellectual integrity means being honest about evidence. It means not twisting data to support a decision already made, and being willing to change your view when facts change. For administrators who write notes and reports, this matters greatly.

Indian thought has long valued this inner steadiness. Mahatma Gandhi offered a simple test for moments of doubt, often called his talisman. In his words, roughly, recall the face of the poorest and weakest person you have seen, and ask whether the step you are about to take will be of any use to them.

--- part 3 ---
Many officers find that this one question cuts through confusion. It turns integrity from an abstract word into a practical habit.

Probity, the middle circle.

Let us move to the third part: probity.

Probity means strict honesty and uprightness, especially in the conduct of public affairs. If integrity is the character, probity is the conduct that a public office demands. It is about using public power and public money only for the public purpose.

Probity has a strong procedural side. It means following fair processes: open tenders, clear criteria, written reasons, and records that others can check. A decision may happen to be right, but if it was made by skipping fair procedure, probity has still been harmed.

A central idea here is conflict of interest. This arises when a public servant's private interest could influence, or appear to influence, their public duty. Imagine an officer who must decide on a land use change, and a close relative owns land in that area. Even if the officer acts fairly, people may doubt the decision. Probity asks the officer to declare the interest and step aside from that decision. Notice the word appear. In public life, being fair is not enough; being seen to be fair also matters, because trust depends on it.

Probity also covers gifts and hospitality, the use of official vehicles and resources, the handling of confidential information, and care in spending.

--- part 4 ---
Many service conduct rules in India set out such duties, including declaring assets and seeking permission before accepting certain gifts.

In India, the Second Administrative Reforms Commission, in its fourth report titled Ethics in Governance, discussed these issues in depth. It suggested a code of ethics for civil servants, stronger systems for declaring assets and interests, and protection for people who report wrongdoing. Reading the summary of that report is a gentle and useful way to deepen your understanding.

Ancient Indian writing on statecraft knew this problem well. The Arthashastra, attributed to Kautilya, compares officials handling public money to someone with honey on the tongue, who finds it hard not to taste it. Its answer was not only to trust good character, but also to watch, check and rotate officials. That old insight leads us neatly to our next circle.

Accountability, the outer circle.

We are now in the fourth part: accountability.

Accountability means being answerable for one's decisions and actions, and accepting consequences. Scholars often break it into two parts. The first is answerability: the duty to explain and justify what was done. The second is enforceability: the existence of consequences, such as correction, penalty or reward.

In a democracy, accountability flows in many directions. There is political accountability, where ministers answer to Parliament and, through elections, to the people.

--- part 5 ---
There is administrative accountability, where officers answer to their seniors and to the rules. There is legal accountability, through courts and tribunals. There is financial accountability, through the Comptroller and Auditor General and parliamentary committees such as the Public Accounts Committee. And there is social accountability, where citizens themselves check the work of government, for example through social audits of rural employment works.

India has built several tools for this. The Right to Information Act of two thousand and five lets citizens ask public authorities for information, with time limits for replies. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act of two thousand and thirteen set up bodies to inquire into allegations of corruption against public servants. The Central Vigilance Commission oversees vigilance work in the central government. Citizens' charters tell people what service standards to expect from an office. Grievance portals give a channel for complaints.

People who report wrongdoing from inside an organisation are often called whistle-blowers. They take real personal risks. The Whistle Blowers Protection Act of two thousand and fourteen was passed to set up a way for such complaints to be made and for complainants to be protected. A system that punishes honest reporting quietly teaches everyone to stay silent, so this protection is an important part of accountability.

Transparency is the close companion of accountability.

--- part 6 ---
When information is open, it becomes easier to ask questions and harder to hide mistakes. But openness has limits too. Privacy, national security and the confidentiality of some deliberations are also valid concerns. Balancing them is part of good judgement.

A thinker often quoted here is the British Committee on Standards in Public Life, which in nineteen ninety-five set out seven principles of public life, often called the Nolan principles. They are selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty and leadership. They are not Indian law, but they offer a tidy checklist that many people find helpful.

Putting it together, and a recap.

We are in the last part. How do the three circles work together?

Think of them as supporting one another. Integrity without accountability depends entirely on the good nature of individuals, and people vary. Accountability without integrity creates a culture of box-ticking, where people follow rules in letter but not in spirit. Probity is the bridge. It turns inner values into outward conduct, and it gives accountability something solid to check.

When you meet a case study, you might use a gentle three-step lens. First, ask: what does integrity require of me personally here? Second, ask: what fair procedure does my office require, and is there any conflict of interest? Third, ask: who am I answerable to, and how will I record and explain my decision?

--- part 7 ---
This lens does not give a ready-made answer, but it helps you organise your thinking calmly.

Let us try it quickly on the earlier example of the backdated file. Integrity: I will not falsify a record. Probity: the tender must follow the published criteria, and I should note any pressure in writing. Accountability: I will keep a clear file note, inform the appropriate authority if needed, and be ready to explain my action. The answer feels steadier because each part has a place.

Now a short recap. Integrity is wholeness of character, where values, words and actions match. Probity is uprightness in public conduct, with fair procedure and care about conflicts of interest. Accountability is answerability plus enforceability, supported in India by tools like the Right to Information Act, the Lokpal, audit and social audit. The three work best together.

Take these words with you on your next walk. Notice where you see them in daily life, at a ration shop, in a hospital queue, in a news story. That noticing is itself good preparation.

Three questions to check yourself. Pause after each one and choose an answer.

Question one. Which statement best describes the difference between honesty and integrity?

Option one: They mean exactly the same thing. Option two: Honesty is about public money, integrity about private life. Option three: Honesty is about telling the truth, while integrity is about living consistently by one's principles.

--- part 8 ---
Option four: Integrity applies only to officers, honesty to everyone.

Question two. An officer has to decide a matter in which a close relative has a financial interest. What does probity require first?

Option one: Decide quickly before anyone objects. Option two: Declare the interest and step aside from the decision. Option three: Ask the relative for their view. Option four: Decide fairly, without telling anyone.

Question three. Accountability is often described as having two parts. Which pair is it?

Option one: Integrity and honesty. Option two: Openness and leadership. Option three: Answerability and enforceability. Option four: Efficiency and economy.

Now the answers. Question one: the answer is option three. Integrity includes honesty but also means that values, words and actions match consistently, even when it is costly.

Now the answers. Question two: the answer is option two. Probity asks the officer to avoid both real and apparent conflict of interest, so declaring it and stepping aside comes first.

Now the answers. Question three: the answer is option three. Accountability means the duty to explain one's actions, which is answerability, and the existence of consequences, which is enforceability.

That is the end of this talk. Thank you for listening.

--- part 1 ---
Internal security basics.

What internal security means.

Welcome. Internal security can sound like a heavy subject, full of agencies and acronyms. In this talk we will keep it simple and organised, like a map with a few clear roads.

Let us begin with the meaning. Internal security is about protecting the country from threats within its borders: threats to public order, to the unity and integrity of the nation, and to the safety of its people and institutions. It is different from external security, which is about threats from outside, such as a war. In practice, the two often meet. Some internal threats receive support from outside, and border management sits right between them.

Why does it matter for governance? Because development and security depend on each other. Where people do not feel safe, schools, roads and businesses struggle. And where development and justice are missing, grievances can be exploited by those who want to spread violence. Many official documents describe this as the link between security and development.

A useful way to organise the topic is to ask four questions. What are the challenges? Who is responsible? What laws and institutions exist? And how do we balance security with rights? We will take them one by one.

The main challenges.

We are now in the second part: the main challenges.

The Ministry of Home Affairs, in its annual reports, has usually grouped India's internal security challenges into a few broad theatres.

--- part 2 ---
Let us describe them in neutral, general terms.

The first is terrorism in the hinterland. This means attacks on civilians in cities and towns, aimed at spreading fear and dividing communities. Some such groups have received support from across the border.

The second is the situation in Jammu and Kashmir, where security forces have faced militancy and cross-border infiltration for decades. After the constitutional changes of two thousand and nineteen, Jammu and Kashmir became a Union Territory, and Ladakh a separate Union Territory.

The third is insurgency in parts of the North East. Several armed groups arose over issues of identity, autonomy and resources. Over the years, many peace agreements have been signed, and violence has reduced substantially in many areas, though some challenges remain.

The fourth is Left Wing Extremism, often called the Maoist or Naxalite movement. It has affected forested and tribal areas in parts of central and eastern India. The number of affected districts and the level of violence have fallen considerably over the past decade, according to government data.

Beyond these theatres, there are cross-cutting threats. Cyber security covers attacks on computers, networks and critical systems such as power grids and banks. Organised crime includes trafficking of drugs, arms and people. Money laundering and terror financing provide the fuel for many of these threats. Communal violence can damage social harmony.

--- part 3 ---
And misinformation on social media can inflame tension quickly.

Border management deserves a special mention, because it links internal and external security. India's land borders run through deserts, mountains, rivers, forests and densely populated plains, and each needs a different approach. Fencing, floodlights and technology help in some areas. In others, the key is the people who live near the border. Programmes such as the Vibrant Villages Programme aim to improve roads, livelihoods and services in border villages, so that these areas are not left behind.

Who is responsible, and the main institutions.

Let us move to the third part: who does what.

Here is a key constitutional point. Public order and police are subjects in the State List of the Seventh Schedule. So the primary responsibility for maintaining law and order lies with the State governments and their police forces. At the same time, Article three hundred and fifty-five places a duty on the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance, and to ensure that every State is governed according to the Constitution. So the Centre supports the States.

The Ministry of Home Affairs is the nodal ministry for internal security at the Union level. Under it are the Central Armed Police Forces. The Central Reserve Police Force helps States with law and order and counter-insurgency. The Border Security Force guards the borders with Pakistan and Bangladesh.

--- part 4 ---
The Indo-Tibetan Border Police guards the border with China. The Sashastra Seema Bal guards the borders with Nepal and Bhutan. The Assam Rifles guards the border with Myanmar and works in the North East. The Central Industrial Security Force protects airports, ports and key industrial sites. The National Security Guard is a specialised force for counter-terrorism and hostage situations.

After the Mumbai attacks of November two thousand and eight, India strengthened its institutions. The National Investigation Agency was created by a law passed in December two thousand and eight, to investigate terror and other serious offences across States. The Multi Agency Centre was strengthened to share intelligence among agencies. And work began on NATGRID, a system to link databases for faster, authorised access to information.

At the highest level, the National Security Council, supported by the National Security Council Secretariat and headed by the National Security Adviser, coordinates national security policy.

Intelligence is the quiet foundation of all this. The Intelligence Bureau, under the Ministry of Home Affairs, is the main agency for internal intelligence, and States have their own intelligence wings. Good intelligence allows threats to be prevented rather than only responded to. That is why sharing information quickly between agencies, and between the Centre and the States, has been such a strong theme in reforms.

--- part 5 ---
For cyber security, the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team, known as CERT-In, responds to cyber incidents, and the National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre protects key systems. Both draw powers from the Information Technology Act of two thousand.

Laws, approaches and the balance with rights.

We are now in the fourth part: the laws, the strategies, and the balance with rights.

Several laws shape this field. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act of nineteen sixty-seven, amended many times, deals with terrorism and unlawful associations. The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act of nineteen fifty-eight gives special powers to the armed forces in areas declared disturbed. The National Investigation Agency Act sets up the NIA. The Prevention of Money Laundering Act deals with laundering of criminal money. And in two thousand and twenty-three, Parliament passed three new criminal laws, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, replacing the Indian Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure and the Evidence Act. They came into force on the first of July, two thousand and twenty-four.

Approaches have also evolved. For Left Wing Extremism, the government follows a combined approach that pairs security operations with development, such as roads, mobile towers, schools and banking access, and with rehabilitation policies for those who surrender.

--- part 6 ---
In the North East, peace agreements and dialogue have played a major role. Community policing, where the police work with local people, builds trust and early warning. And police modernisation, including better training, forensic science and technology, is a continuing need.

Police reform is a related theme. In the Prakash Singh case of two thousand and six, the Supreme Court gave directions to States, such as setting up State Security Commissions, giving key police officers a minimum tenure, separating investigation from law and order duties, and creating Police Complaints Authorities. The idea was to make the police more professional, more insulated from improper pressure, and more accountable to citizens. Implementation has varied from State to State, and it remains a subject of discussion.

Now the balance with rights. Strong security powers can sometimes affect civil liberties, such as the right to personal liberty, free speech and privacy. Courts have repeatedly stressed that security laws must be applied with care and procedural safeguards. Human rights commissions, judicial review and a free press are part of the system of checks. Many experts argue that respecting rights is not a weakness in security; it is a strength, because it builds the public trust that security work depends on.

--- part 7 ---
When writing answers, a balanced structure helps: name the threat, explain its causes, describe the existing response, note the gaps, and suggest a way forward that combines security, development and rights.

A recap.

We have reached the last part. Let us bring the map together.

Internal security protects the country from threats within its borders, and it is closely tied to development and justice. The main theatres usually described are terrorism in the hinterland, Jammu and Kashmir, insurgency in parts of the North East, and Left Wing Extremism. Cross-cutting threats include cyber attacks, organised crime, money laundering, communal violence and misinformation.

Police and public order are State subjects, so States hold primary responsibility, while Article three hundred and fifty-five places a duty on the Union to protect States. The Ministry of Home Affairs leads at the Centre, with the Central Armed Police Forces, the National Investigation Agency, the Multi Agency Centre, and cyber bodies such as CERT-In. The National Security Council coordinates policy at the top.

Key laws include the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, the Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, the NIA Act, the money laundering law, and the three new criminal laws in force since July two thousand and twenty-four. Strategies combine security, development and dialogue. And the balance with rights is protected by courts, commissions and a free press.

This is a subject where calm thinking matters.

Three questions to check yourself. Pause after each one and choose an answer.

Question one. Under the Constitution, which level of government has primary responsibility for police and public order?

Option one: The Union, because they are in the Union List. Option two: The States, because they are in the State List. Option three: Both equally, because they are in the Concurrent List. Option four: The National Security Council.

Question two. Which agency was created by law after the Mumbai attacks of two thousand and eight to investigate terror offences across States?

Option one: Central Industrial Security Force. Option two: CERT-In. Option three: National Investigation Agency. Option four: Sashastra Seema Bal.

Question three. Which force guards India's border with Nepal and Bhutan?

Option one: Border Security Force. Option two: Indo-Tibetan Border Police. Option three: Sashastra Seema Bal. Option four: Assam Rifles.

Now the answers. Question one: the answer is option two. Police and public order are entries in the State List, so States hold primary responsibility, with the Union duty to protect States under Article three hundred and fifty-five.

Now the answers. Question two: the answer is option three.

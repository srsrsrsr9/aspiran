--- part 1 ---
Climate negotiations from Rio to now.

Why countries negotiate, and the key ideas.

Welcome. In this talk we will travel through about three decades of climate negotiations. It can feel like a long list of cities and acronyms, so let us build a simple story you can remember.

First, why negotiate at all? Greenhouse gases mix across the whole atmosphere. A tonne of carbon dioxide released anywhere warms the planet everywhere. So no country can solve the problem alone, and each country is tempted to let others do the hard work. Negotiations try to overcome that temptation by agreeing on shared rules.

Three ideas run through the whole story, so let us name them now.

The first is common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities. All countries share responsibility for protecting the climate, but those that industrialised earlier, and emitted more over time, and those with more resources, are expected to do more. India has consistently stressed this principle.

The second is the difference between mitigation and adaptation. Mitigation means reducing emissions, or absorbing them, so warming slows. Adaptation means preparing for the changes that are already coming, such as stronger heatwaves or rising seas.

The third is finance and technology. Developing countries argue that they need money and technology to grow cleanly and to adapt. Much of the tension in every negotiation is about who pays, how much and through which channels.

--- part 2 ---
Alongside the negotiators sits the science. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, set up in nineteen eighty-eight by the World Meteorological Organization and the United Nations Environment Programme, assesses published research and reports to governments. It does not do its own research or make policy. But its assessment reports have shaped almost every major turn in the talks, including the move to a one point five degree goal. When you read about a new report, ask how it might change the next round of negotiations.

Rio and the Kyoto years.

We are now in the second part: the beginning.

In June nineteen ninety-two, the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development met in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. It is often called the Earth Summit. Three important agreements are linked to Rio: the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, the Convention on Biological Diversity, and, a little later, the Convention to Combat Desertification.

The Framework Convention entered into force in nineteen ninety-four. Its objective is to stabilise greenhouse gas concentrations at a level that would prevent dangerous human interference with the climate system. It divided countries into groups. Annex One lists mainly industrialised countries and economies in transition. Countries not in Annex One are mostly developing countries, including India.

The countries that have joined the Convention meet every year at a Conference of the Parties, or COP.

--- part 3 ---
The first was held in Berlin in nineteen ninety-five.

At the third COP, in Kyoto, Japan, in nineteen ninety-seven, countries adopted the Kyoto Protocol. It set binding emission reduction targets, but only for developed countries listed in its Annex B. Developing countries had no binding targets. The Protocol entered into force in two thousand and five, and its first commitment period ran from two thousand and eight to two thousand and twelve.

Kyoto also created three market-based mechanisms. Emissions trading allowed countries with targets to trade emission units. Joint implementation allowed projects between developed countries. And the clean development mechanism allowed developed countries to earn credits by funding emission-reducing projects in developing countries. India hosted a large number of such projects.

But Kyoto had weaknesses. The United States, then the largest emitter, did not ratify it. And as emissions from large developing economies grew, many argued that a system with targets for only some countries could not solve the problem.

The road to Paris.

Let us move to the third part: the search for a new deal.

In two thousand and seven, at Bali, countries agreed on the Bali Action Plan, which set out a path to a new agreement by two thousand and nine. The talks at Copenhagen in two thousand and nine were expected to deliver it. They did not, at least not in a formal way.

--- part 4 ---
Instead, a short political document, the Copenhagen Accord, was noted by the conference. It did include an important promise: developed countries would aim to mobilise one hundred billion dollars a year by two thousand and twenty for climate action in developing countries.

The next year, at Cancun, many Copenhagen ideas were formally adopted, and the Green Climate Fund was established. In two thousand and eleven, at Durban, countries launched talks for a new agreement applicable to all parties, to be adopted by two thousand and fifteen. In two thousand and twelve, at Doha, the Kyoto Protocol was extended with a second commitment period up to two thousand and twenty. In two thousand and thirteen, at Warsaw, countries set up the Warsaw International Mechanism for loss and damage, and invited all countries to prepare their intended nationally determined contributions.

Notice how the approach was changing. Instead of targets set from the top for a few countries, the new idea was that every country would decide its own contribution, and then the world would add them up and push for more. This is often called a bottom-up approach.

Paris and after.

We are now in the fourth part: the Paris Agreement and the years that followed.

In December two thousand and fifteen, at the twenty-first COP, countries adopted the Paris Agreement. It entered into force in November two thousand and sixteen.

--- part 5 ---
Its central goal is to hold the increase in global average temperature to well below two degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, and to pursue efforts to limit it to one point five degrees. It also aims to strengthen adaptation and to make finance flows consistent with low-emission development.

The engine of Paris is the nationally determined contribution. Each country sets its own climate plan and updates it every five years, with each new plan expected to be more ambitious than the last. An enhanced transparency framework asks countries to report on their progress. And every five years, a global stocktake reviews collective progress. The first global stocktake concluded at the twenty-eighth COP, in Dubai, in two thousand and twenty-three.

The years after Paris added detail. At Glasgow in two thousand and twenty-one, the twenty-sixth COP finished the rulebook, including rules for carbon markets under Article six. Its final text called for phasing down unabated coal power, wording that was changed late in the talks. At Sharm el-Sheikh in two thousand and twenty-two, countries agreed to set up a fund for responding to loss and damage, a long-standing demand of vulnerable countries. At Dubai in two thousand and twenty-three, that fund was made operational, and the stocktake decision called on countries to contribute to transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, in a just, orderly and equitable manner.

--- part 6 ---
At Baku in two thousand and twenty-four, countries agreed a new collective finance goal, with developed countries taking the lead to provide at least three hundred billion dollars a year for developing countries by two thousand and thirty-five, within a wider call to scale up finance from all sources. Many developing countries, including India, said this was far too little.

The thirtieth COP was held in Belém, in the Brazilian Amazon, in November two thousand and twenty-five, the year when countries were due to submit their new climate plans. The talks continue each year, so it is worth checking the outcome of the most recent conference before your exam.

India's position, and a recap.

We are in the last part. Where does India stand in all this?

India's historical share of global emissions is small compared to its population, and its emissions per person are well below the world average. At the same time, India is now one of the larger total emitters, because of its size and growth. India's position has therefore combined two themes: insisting on equity and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, and taking on its own ambitious goals.

--- part 7 ---
India's updated nationally determined contribution, submitted in two thousand and twenty-two, has three main targets for two thousand and thirty: to reduce the emissions intensity of its gross domestic product by forty-five per cent from the two thousand and five level; to have about fifty per cent of its installed electricity capacity from non-fossil sources; and to create an additional carbon sink of two and a half to three billion tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent through more forest and tree cover. At Glasgow, India also announced a goal of net zero emissions by two thousand and seventy. India has also promoted ideas such as Mission LiFE, which stands for Lifestyle for Environment, and it co-founded the International Solar Alliance with France in two thousand and fifteen.

Now a recap. The Rio Earth Summit of nineteen ninety-two gave us the Framework Convention, with its principle of common but differentiated responsibilities. The Kyoto Protocol of nineteen ninety-seven set binding targets only for developed countries. Copenhagen in two thousand and nine fell short, but promised one hundred billion dollars a year. Paris in two thousand and fifteen created a system where every country sets its own contribution, aiming at well below two degrees and pursuing one point five. Later conferences added the rulebook, a loss and damage fund, the first global stocktake and a new finance goal.

--- part 8 ---
And India stresses equity while pursuing its own targets for two thousand and thirty and net zero by two thousand and seventy.

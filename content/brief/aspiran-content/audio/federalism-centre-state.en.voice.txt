--- part 1 ---
Federalism and Centre–State relations.

What kind of federation India is.

Welcome. In this talk we will look at one of the most discussed themes in Indian polity: how power is shared between the Union and the States. Let us take it in three layers, legislative, administrative and financial, and then look at the forums that hold it together.

Start with the first article of the Constitution. Article one says that India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States. Dr B. R. Ambedkar explained in the Constituent Assembly why the word Union was chosen. The Indian federation was not the result of an agreement among states, as in the United States, and no state has the right to leave it.

India has the main features of a federation: two levels of government, a written constitution, division of powers, an independent judiciary to settle disputes, and a second chamber, the Rajya Sabha, that represents the States. But it also has features that give the Centre extra strength: a single constitution, single citizenship, integrated courts, All India Services, emergency powers, and the power of Parliament to reorganise states under Article three.

Scholars have described India in different ways: quasi-federal, a federation with a centralising tendency, or a holding-together federation. The Supreme Court, in the S. R. Bommai case of nineteen ninety-four, said that federalism is part of the basic structure of the Constitution. That means it cannot be removed even by an amendment.

--- part 2 ---
Sharing law-making: the three lists.

We are now in the second part: legislative relations.

The Seventh Schedule contains three lists. The Union List covers subjects of national importance, such as defence, foreign affairs, atomic energy, railways, banking and currency. The State List covers subjects best handled locally, such as police, public order, public health and sanitation, agriculture, and local government. The Concurrent List covers subjects on which both can make laws, such as criminal law and procedure, marriage and divorce, education, forests, and electricity.

Under Article two hundred and forty-six, Parliament has exclusive power over the Union List, State legislatures over the State List, and both over the Concurrent List. Residuary powers, for subjects not in any list, belong to Parliament under Article two hundred and forty-eight. This is different from the United States, where residuary powers lie with the states.

If a State law on a Concurrent subject conflicts with a central law, Article two hundred and fifty-four says the central law generally prevails. But if the State law received the President's assent, it can prevail in that State, unless Parliament later makes a law on the same matter.

In some situations, Parliament can make laws even on State subjects. Under Article two hundred and forty-nine, the Rajya Sabha can pass a resolution, supported by two thirds of members present and voting, saying it is in the national interest.

--- part 3 ---
Under Article two hundred and fifty, Parliament can legislate on State subjects during a national emergency. Under Article two hundred and fifty-two, two or more States can request Parliament to legislate for them. And under Article two hundred and fifty-three, Parliament can make laws on any subject to implement international treaties.

The lists have also changed over time. The Forty-second Amendment of nineteen seventy-six moved five subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List: education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, and administration of justice in certain courts. This is often cited as an example of centralisation.

Sharing administration.

Let us move to the third part: administrative relations.

Articles two hundred and fifty-six and two hundred and fifty-seven ask States to exercise their executive power in a way that ensures compliance with laws made by Parliament, and does not impede the executive power of the Union. The Union can give directions to States in certain matters.

The All India Services, the Indian Administrative Service, the Indian Police Service and the Indian Forest Service, are a special link. Under Article three hundred and twelve, they are created by Parliament. Officers are recruited by the Centre but serve in the States, and can be deputed to the Centre. Sardar Patel argued for these services as a steel frame that would hold the country together.

--- part 4 ---
Today, deputation rules are sometimes a point of friction between the Centre and some States.

The Governor is another link. The Governor is appointed by the President and holds office during the President's pleasure. The Governor is the constitutional head of the State and generally acts on the advice of the State Council of Ministers. But the Governor also has some discretion, for example in reserving certain bills for the President's consideration under Article two hundred, or in reporting to the President on the situation in the State. The role of Governors, including delays in acting on bills, has been the subject of several Supreme Court judgments in recent years.

Then there is Article three hundred and fifty-six. If the President is satisfied, on a report of the Governor or otherwise, that the government of a State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution, President's rule can be imposed. The State government is dismissed, and the State legislature may be suspended or dissolved. In earlier decades this power was used many times, sometimes for political reasons. The Bommai judgment set important limits: the satisfaction of the President can be reviewed by courts, the floor of the Assembly is the place to test majority, and the Assembly should not be dissolved before Parliament approves the proclamation. Since then, its use has become much less frequent.

Sharing money.

--- part 5 ---
We are now in the fourth part: financial relations, where much of the real tension lies.

States carry heavy responsibilities, such as health, police and agriculture, but the most buoyant taxes are collected by the Centre. This gap between duties and revenues is called vertical imbalance. And some States are richer than others, which is called horizontal imbalance.

The Finance Commission, under Article two hundred and eighty, addresses both. It is set up by the President every five years. It recommends what share of the divisible pool of central taxes should go to the States, and how that share should be divided among them, using criteria such as population, area, income distance and forest cover. The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended forty-one per cent for the States. The Sixteenth Finance Commission covers the five years starting in April two thousand and twenty-six. The Centre also gives grants to States under Article two hundred and seventy-five, and for specific purposes under Article two hundred and eighty-two, including centrally sponsored schemes.

Another major change came with the Goods and Services Tax. The One Hundred and First Amendment of two thousand and sixteen gave both Parliament and State legislatures power to tax supplies of goods and services, and created the GST Council under Article two hundred and seventy-nine A. The Council includes the Union Finance Minister and the finance ministers of the States.

--- part 6 ---
The Centre has one third of the weight of votes, and the States together have two thirds. A decision needs three quarters of the weighted votes. So neither side can decide alone. Many see the Council as an example of cooperative federalism, where both levels pool sovereignty. At the same time, some States feel they have lost much of their own taxing power.

Borrowing is another area. Under Article two hundred and ninety-three, a State that owes money to the Centre needs the Centre's consent to borrow further. Since most States do owe the Centre, this gives the Centre a say over State borrowing.

Forums, reforms, and a recap.

We are in the last part: the forums that help the federation work, and the reform ideas.

The Inter-State Council, under Article two hundred and sixty-three, was set up in nineteen ninety, following a recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission. It is chaired by the Prime Minister and includes Chief Ministers. Zonal Councils, created under the States Reorganisation Act of nineteen fifty-six, bring neighbouring States together. NITI Aayog, set up in two thousand and fifteen in place of the Planning Commission, includes all Chief Ministers in its Governing Council. River water disputes between States are handled under Article two hundred and sixty-two, through tribunals set up by Parliament.

Two commissions are especially important for answers.

--- part 7 ---
The Sarkaria Commission, which reported in nineteen eighty-eight, recommended that Article three hundred and fifty-six be used only as a last resort, that Governors be chosen carefully and consulted with the Chief Minister, and that the Inter-State Council be set up. The Punchhi Commission, which reported in two thousand and ten, made further suggestions, including fixed tenure for Governors and clearer limits on their discretion.

You will often hear two phrases. Cooperative federalism means the Centre and States working together, as in the GST Council. Competitive federalism means States competing to attract investment and improve governance, as encouraged by various rankings and indices. A healthy federation needs both, along with trust.

Now a recap. India is a Union of States with strong central features, and federalism is part of the basic structure. Law-making is shared through the Union, State and Concurrent Lists, with residuary powers with the Centre and several ways for Parliament to legislate on State subjects. Administrative links include the All India Services, the Governor and Article three hundred and fifty-six, which the Bommai judgment limited. Financial relations run through the Finance Commission, grants, the GST Council and borrowing rules. And forums like the Inter-State Council and NITI Aayog, along with the Sarkaria and Punchhi recommendations, guide how the relationship evolves.

When you next read about a disagreement between the Centre and a State, try asking: is it legislative, administrative or financial? That one question will help you place it quickly.

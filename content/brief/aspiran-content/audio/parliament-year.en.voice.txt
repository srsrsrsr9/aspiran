--- part 1 ---
Parliament's year.

The rhythm of a year.

Welcome. In this talk we will walk through one year in the life of Parliament. We will look at the sessions, at how a day is organised, and at how a bill travels from one House to the other.

Let us begin with the basic rule. Article eighty-five of the Constitution says that the President shall summon each House from time to time. It also says that six months shall not pass between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next. That is the only fixed rule. Everything else is convention.

By convention, Parliament meets in three sessions a year. The Budget Session usually begins in late January or early February and runs into April. It has a break in the middle, when committees study the Demands for Grants. The Monsoon Session usually falls in July and August. The Winter Session usually falls in November and December.

A few words you will meet often. Summoning means calling the Houses to meet. Adjournment ends a sitting for a short time, perhaps hours or days, and is decided by the presiding officer. Adjournment sine die means adjourning without naming a day to meet again. Prorogation ends a session, and it is done by the President on the advice of the government. Dissolution ends the life of the Lok Sabha itself, which then needs fresh elections. The Rajya Sabha is a permanent House and is never dissolved. One third of its members retire every second year.

A useful point for exams.

--- part 2 ---
When the Lok Sabha is dissolved, bills pending in the Lok Sabha lapse. Bills pending in the Rajya Sabha that have not been passed by the Lok Sabha do not lapse. Prorogation, on the other hand, does not cause any bill to lapse.

What happens when Parliament is not in session and the government needs a law urgently? Article one hundred and twenty-three lets the President issue an ordinance, on the advice of the Council of Ministers. An ordinance has the same force as an Act, but it is temporary. It must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble, and it ceases to operate six weeks after Parliament meets again, unless it is replaced by a law earlier. So the session calendar and the ordinance power are closely linked. The gaps between sessions are exactly when ordinances appear.

A day in the House.

We are now in the second part. Let us sit in the gallery and watch an ordinary day.

The first hour of a sitting is usually Question Hour. This is when members ask ministers questions and ministers answer. It is one of the oldest and strongest tools of accountability.

There are three kinds of questions. A starred question is answered orally on the floor, and members can ask supplementary questions after the answer. An unstarred question gets a written answer, laid on the table, so there are no supplementaries. A short notice question deals with an urgent matter and can be asked with less than the usual notice. It is answered orally, if the minister agrees.

--- part 3 ---
After Question Hour, many days have Zero Hour. Zero Hour is not mentioned in the rules of procedure. It grew as a practice in the nineteen sixties, when members began raising urgent matters right after Question Hour, around twelve noon. Today members give notice in the morning and the presiding officer decides who may speak.

Then comes the main business of the day. That may be a bill, a debate, or a motion. Motions are formal proposals put to the House. A calling attention motion asks a minister to make a statement on a matter of public importance. An adjournment motion, allowed only in the Lok Sabha, proposes to set aside normal business to discuss a definite and urgent matter. A no confidence motion, again only in the Lok Sabha, says the House has lost confidence in the Council of Ministers. It needs the support of at least fifty members to be taken up, and if passed, the government is expected to resign. This follows from Article seventy-five, which makes the Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.

So in one day you may see questions, urgent issues raised, and law being made. That variety is what makes the House a living institution.

How a bill moves.

Let us now come to the heart of this talk: how a bill becomes law. We are in the third part.

A bill is a draft law. A bill brought by a minister is a government bill. A bill brought by any other member is a private member's bill. Most ordinary bills can start in either House.

--- part 4 ---
Private members' bills usually get time on Friday afternoons in the Lok Sabha. Very few have become law, but they let members bring new ideas to the floor, and sometimes the government later takes an idea up in its own bill.

An ordinary bill passes through three readings in each House.

The first reading is the introduction. The member asks for leave to introduce the bill, and once introduced, it is published in the Gazette. Usually there is no discussion at this stage.

The second reading is where the real work happens. First there is a general discussion on the principles of the bill. At this point the House may simply take up the bill, or send it to a select committee of that House, or to a joint committee of both Houses, or circulate it to seek public opinion. Many bills are also examined by the departmentally related standing committees. A committee studies the bill clause by clause, hears experts and suggests changes. Then the House considers the bill clause by clause and votes on amendments.

The third reading is short. The House debates whether to accept or reject the bill as a whole, and votes.

Then the bill goes to the other House and passes through the same three stages. If the second House agrees, the bill goes to the President for assent. If the second House rejects it, makes changes the first House does not accept, or does not pass it for six months, there is a deadlock.

--- part 5 ---
For ordinary bills, Article one hundred and eight offers a way out: a joint sitting of both Houses. The President summons it, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha presides, and the bill is decided by a majority of members present and voting. Joint sittings have been rare. A joint sitting cannot be held for a Money Bill or for a Constitution amendment bill.

Special kinds of bills and the President's assent.

We are in the fourth part. Some bills follow different paths, and it helps to know them.

A Money Bill is defined in Article one hundred and ten. It deals only with matters like taxes, borrowing, and money going into or out of the Consolidated Fund. It can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and only on the recommendation of the President. The Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations, and it has fourteen days to return the bill. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject those recommendations. If the Speaker certifies a bill as a Money Bill, that decision is final for the purposes of Parliament.

A Constitution amendment bill follows Article three hundred and sixty-eight. Most amendments need a special majority in each House: a majority of the total membership, and two thirds of the members present and voting. Some amendments, those affecting the federal structure, also need ratification by at least half of the state legislatures. There is no joint sitting for these bills.

Once a bill has passed both Houses, it goes to the President under Article one hundred and eleven.

--- part 6 ---
The President may give assent, withhold assent, or, for a bill that is not a Money Bill, return it to Parliament for reconsideration. If Parliament passes it again, with or without changes, the President shall not withhold assent. Once assented, the bill becomes an Act.

What about Parliament's committees? They are often called mini-Parliaments. There are financial committees such as the Public Accounts Committee, the Estimates Committee and the Committee on Public Undertakings. There are departmentally related standing committees that watch each group of ministries. And there are ad hoc committees made for a particular task. Committees work through the year, even when the House is not sitting. That is why much of Parliament's careful work happens in quieter rooms.

Recap.

We have reached the last part. Let us gather what we heard.

Parliament has no fixed calendar in the Constitution, only the six-month rule in Article eighty-five. By convention there are three sessions: Budget, Monsoon and Winter.

A day often begins with Question Hour, with starred, unstarred and short notice questions. Zero Hour follows by practice, not by rule.

An ordinary bill passes three readings in each House. Deadlocks on ordinary bills can be settled in a joint sitting under Article one hundred and eight, chaired by the Speaker. Money Bills start only in the Lok Sabha, and the Rajya Sabha has fourteen days. Constitution amendment bills need a special majority and never go to a joint sitting.

--- part 7 ---
Finally, the President's assent under Article one hundred and eleven turns a bill into an Act.

When you next read that a session has begun, try to picture this whole rhythm. It will make every headline easier to place.

Three questions to check yourself. Pause after each one and choose an answer.

Question one. Which kind of question in Parliament receives an oral answer and allows supplementary questions?

Option one: Unstarred question. Option two: Starred question. Option three: Zero Hour mention. Option four: Calling attention notice.

Question two. For which of these can a joint sitting of both Houses be held?

Option one: A Money Bill. Option two: A Constitution amendment bill. Option three: An ordinary bill on which the Houses disagree. Option four: A bill certified by the Speaker as a Financial Bill of any kind.

Question three. Which statement about the end of a session or a House is correct?

Option one: Prorogation causes all pending bills to lapse. Option two: The Rajya Sabha is dissolved every six years. Option three: A bill pending in the Rajya Sabha and not passed by the Lok Sabha does not lapse when the Lok Sabha is dissolved. Option four: Adjournment sine die is done by the President.

Now the answers. Question one: the answer is option two. A starred question is answered orally on the floor, and members may then ask supplementaries; an unstarred question gets a written answer.

Now the answers. Question two: the answer is option three.

--- part 8 ---
Article one hundred and eight allows a joint sitting only for ordinary bills; Money Bills and amendment bills are excluded.

Now the answers. Question three: the answer is option three. The Rajya Sabha is a permanent House, so such a bill survives dissolution of the Lok Sabha; prorogation causes no bill to lapse.

That is the end of this talk. Thank you for listening.

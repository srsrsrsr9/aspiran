--- part 1 ---
How the RBI sets rates.

What the repo rate is.

Welcome. In this talk we will follow one decision, a change in the policy repo rate, from the meeting room to your neighbourhood bank. Take it slowly; each idea builds on the last.

Let us start with the word repo. It is short for repurchase agreement. A bank that needs money overnight can sell government securities to the Reserve Bank of India and agree to buy them back the next day at a slightly higher price. That difference works like interest. The rate charged is the repo rate.

Because banks borrow from the Reserve Bank at this rate, it sets the floor of the whole interest-rate ladder. If the repo rate goes up, borrowing becomes costlier for banks, and over time they charge more on loans. If it goes down, the opposite happens. So the repo rate is the main signal of monetary policy. It is often called the policy rate.

Why does a central bank want to move rates at all? The main reason is to keep prices stable. When prices rise too fast, people's savings and wages buy less. Poorer households, with little cushion, feel this most. Higher rates cool spending and borrowing, which eases pressure on prices. Lower rates do the reverse and support growth. The art lies in choosing the right balance at the right time.

The Monetary Policy Committee and its target.

We are now in the second part: who makes the decision.

Until twenty sixteen, the Governor of the Reserve Bank decided the policy rate, with advice from a committee.

--- part 2 ---
In twenty sixteen, Parliament amended the Reserve Bank of India Act. The amendment created a Monetary Policy Committee and gave India a formal inflation target. This is called flexible inflation targeting.

The Committee has six members. Three come from the Reserve Bank: the Governor, who chairs it; the Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy; and one more officer nominated by the Bank's central board. The other three are outside experts appointed by the Central Government for a term of four years. Each member has one vote. If there is a tie, the Governor has a second, casting vote.

The law asks the Committee to meet at least four times a year. In practice it meets six times, roughly every two months. After each meeting, the Bank publishes a resolution with the decision and the vote. Two weeks later it publishes the minutes, where each member explains their vote. This openness helps people understand the thinking, and it lets markets prepare.

Now the target. The Central Government, in consultation with the Reserve Bank, sets the inflation target for a period of five years. Since the framework began, the target has been four per cent inflation, measured by the Consumer Price Index, with a tolerance band from two to six per cent. The band allows for shocks, such as a bad monsoon pushing up vegetable prices.

What if inflation stays outside the band?

--- part 3 ---
The rules say that if average inflation is above the upper limit, or below the lower limit, for three consecutive quarters, the target is treated as missed. The Reserve Bank then writes to the government. It explains why the target was missed, what it will do, and how long it expects to take to bring inflation back. That letter is a quiet but important form of accountability.

What the Committee looks at.

Let us move to the third part: what goes through the members' minds before they vote.

The first input is inflation itself, now and in the coming months. They look at headline inflation, and at core inflation, which removes the more volatile food and fuel items. Core inflation tells them whether price rises are spreading across the economy, or are limited to a few items.

The second input is growth. They look at gross domestic product, industrial output, services activity, credit growth and more. If growth is weak and inflation is calm, there is room to cut rates. If growth is strong and inflation is rising, there may be a case to raise them.

The third input is expectations. The Reserve Bank runs regular surveys of households, firms and professional forecasters. If people expect high inflation, they may ask for higher wages and set higher prices, which can make inflation stick. Keeping expectations anchored is a big part of the job.

The fourth input is the world outside. Oil prices, the value of the rupee, interest rates in large economies and global trade all matter.

--- part 4 ---
A sharp rise in crude oil prices, for example, can lift inflation in India even if domestic demand is calm.

Members also think about time. Monetary policy works with a lag. A change today may take several quarters to show its full effect on prices. So the Committee does not chase this month's number. It looks at where inflation is likely to be a year or so ahead. Along with the rate, the Committee announces a stance. A stance tells people which way policy is leaning, for example neutral, or accommodative, which means supportive of growth.

Let us try a small imagined case. Suppose vegetable prices jump after heavy rain, so headline inflation rises for two months, but core inflation stays steady and growth is slowing. A member might say the food shock will pass, and raising rates would hurt growth for little gain. Another member might worry that people will start expecting higher prices. Both views are reasonable. That is why the vote is recorded member by member, and why the minutes are worth reading.

The corridor and the other tools.

We are now in the fourth part: the tools that sit around the repo rate.

Think of a corridor with a floor and a ceiling. The repo rate sits in the middle. The aim is to keep the overnight interbank rate close to the repo rate.

The floor is the standing deposit facility, introduced in twenty twenty-two. Banks with spare money can park it with the Reserve Bank at this rate, without the Bank having to give any securities in return.

--- part 5 ---
Before that, the floor was the reverse repo rate, at which the Reserve Bank borrowed from banks against securities. The reverse repo still exists as a tool, but it is no longer the floor.

The ceiling is the marginal standing facility. A bank that is short of funds overnight can borrow at this rate, which is higher than the repo rate. It can even dip into a part of its statutory liquidity holdings to do so. Because it is costly, banks use it only when needed.

At present, both the floor and the ceiling are set a quarter of a percentage point from the repo rate, one below and one above. Together, these windows are called the liquidity adjustment facility. The Reserve Bank also holds variable-rate repo and reverse repo auctions to add or remove money for a few days at a time.

Then there are the reserve ratios. The cash reserve ratio is the share of deposits a bank must keep with the Reserve Bank as cash, earning no interest. The statutory liquidity ratio is the share a bank must hold in safe, liquid assets such as government securities. Lowering either frees up money for lending. Raising either does the opposite.

Finally, there are open market operations. When the Reserve Bank buys government securities, it puts money into the system. When it sells them, it takes money out. These help manage liquidity over longer periods.

From the meeting room to your loan, and a recap.

We are in the last part. How does a decision reach an ordinary borrower? This is called transmission.

--- part 6 ---
For many years, banks set loan rates using their own internal benchmarks, and changes in the repo rate passed through slowly. To speed this up, the Reserve Bank asked banks, from October twenty nineteen, to link new floating-rate loans to retail customers and small businesses to an external benchmark. Many banks chose the repo rate itself. So today, when the repo rate changes, the rate on many home loans changes at the next reset, often within three months.

Deposit rates also respond, but usually more slowly. And the bond market often moves even before the decision, because traders try to guess what the Committee will do. That is one reason the minutes and the stance matter so much.

Transmission is never perfect. If banks have plenty of spare money, they may cut loan rates even without a repo cut. If they are short of deposits, they may keep loan rates high even after a cut. So the Reserve Bank watches liquidity closely, and uses its other tools to help the policy signal travel. When you read about the Bank adding or absorbing liquidity, think of it as clearing the road so the rate decision can reach its destination.

Now a short recap. The repo rate is the rate at which the Reserve Bank lends overnight to banks against government securities, and it is the main policy signal. Since twenty sixteen, a six-member Monetary Policy Committee decides it, with the Governor holding a casting vote.

--- part 7 ---
The Committee aims at a consumer price inflation target of four per cent, within a band of two to six. Missing the band for three quarters in a row means writing to the government. The repo rate sits in a corridor between the standing deposit facility below and the marginal standing facility above. And with external benchmark lending, decisions reach borrowers faster than before.

Next time you read that the policy rate was left unchanged, try to ask: what did the Committee see in inflation, growth and expectations? That one question will take you a long way.

Three questions to check yourself. Pause after each one and choose an answer.

Question one. Who has a casting vote in the Monetary Policy Committee if the votes are tied?

Option one: The Union Finance Minister. Option two: The Chief Economic Adviser. Option three: The Governor of the Reserve Bank of India. Option four: The senior-most external member.

Question two. Which tool now forms the floor of the liquidity adjustment facility corridor?

Option one: The reverse repo rate. Option two: The standing deposit facility. Option three: The marginal standing facility. Option four: The bank rate.

Question three. When is the inflation target treated as missed under the framework?

Option one: When inflation is above four per cent in any single month. Option two: When the repo rate is changed twice in a row. Option three: When average inflation stays outside the tolerance band for three consecutive quarters.

Option four: When core inflation is higher than headline inflation.

Now the answers. Question one: the answer is option three. The Governor chairs the six-member Committee and has a second, casting vote in case of a tie.

Now the answers. Question two: the answer is option two. Since twenty twenty-two, the standing deposit facility, which needs no collateral from the Reserve Bank, has replaced the fixed reverse repo rate as the floor.

Now the answers. Question three: the answer is option three. The rules count a failure only if average inflation is above the upper limit or below the lower limit for three quarters in a row.

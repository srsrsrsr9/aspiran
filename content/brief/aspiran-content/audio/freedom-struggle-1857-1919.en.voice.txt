--- part 1 ---
India's freedom struggle, part one: from eighteen fifty-seven to nineteen nineteen.

The revolt of eighteen fifty-seven.

Welcome. This is the first of two talks that together cover India's freedom struggle. In this one, we go from eighteen fifty-seven to nineteen nineteen. Think of it as watching a river form: small streams first, then a wider current.

Our story begins on the tenth of May, eighteen fifty-seven, at Meerut. Indian soldiers of the East India Company's army, called sepoys, rose up. The immediate spark was the new Enfield rifle, whose cartridges were rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat. That touched the faith of both Hindu and Muslim soldiers. But the anger ran much deeper. There were grievances over pay and service conditions, the annexation of states like Awadh, heavy land revenue, and a growing sense that the old ways of life were being disturbed.

From Meerut, the soldiers marched to Delhi and declared the aged Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, their leader. The revolt soon spread across large parts of north and central India. Rani Lakshmibai led in Jhansi. Nana Saheb and Tantia Tope led around Kanpur. Begum Hazrat Mahal led in Lucknow. Kunwar Singh, an elderly landholder, led in Bihar. Many peasants and artisans joined in their own areas.

The Company's forces recaptured Delhi in September eighteen fifty-seven, and by eighteen fifty-eight most centres had fallen. Historians point to several reasons. The revolt lacked a single plan and leadership.

--- part 2 ---
It did not spread to every region; much of the south and east stayed largely calm. Many rulers and some groups sided with the British. And the British had better arms, communications and resources.

Yet the revolt changed a great deal. In eighteen fifty-eight, the British Parliament passed an Act that ended the rule of the East India Company. Power passed directly to the Crown. A Secretary of State for India was created in London, and the Governor-General also became the Viceroy. Queen Victoria's Proclamation promised to respect Indian princes and religions. The army was reorganised. Later generations of nationalists looked back on eighteen fifty-seven as the first great challenge to British rule.

New ideas and the birth of the Congress.

We are now in the second part. The next three decades were quieter on the surface, but new ideas were spreading.

Modern education, newspapers and the railways connected educated Indians across regions. Reform movements, such as the Brahmo Samaj and later the Arya Samaj and others, asked people to rethink social customs. Regional associations formed to discuss public questions, such as the British Indian Association in Calcutta, the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, and the Madras Mahajan Sabha.

Some events sharpened political feelings. Many educated Indians resented the Vernacular Press Act of eighteen seventy-eight, which restricted Indian-language newspapers.

--- part 3 ---
In eighteen eighty-three, the Ilbert Bill tried to let Indian judges try European offenders in certain cases. The strong opposition from many Europeans in India showed educated Indians how unequal the system was.

In December eighteen eighty-five, the Indian National Congress held its first session in Bombay. Seventy-two delegates attended. W. C. Bonnerjee presided. A. O. Hume, a retired British civil servant, helped organise it. Historians have long debated Hume's role. Many now see the Congress as the result of a desire that already existed among Indians for an all-India platform.

The early leaders are often called the Moderates. They included Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjea and Gopal Krishna Gokhale. Their method was petitions, speeches and resolutions. Their demands were for more Indians in councils and in the civil services, lower military spending, and relief for peasants.

Their greatest contribution was an economic critique. Dadabhai Naoroji argued that British rule drained India's wealth to Britain, through salaries, pensions, interest and the profits of trade. This came to be known as the drain theory. Romesh Chandra Dutt added careful studies of the Indian economy. These ideas helped many Indians see colonial rule not as a blessing, but as a burden.

Partition of Bengal and the Swadeshi movement.

Let us move to the third part, the years of rising temper.

--- part 4 ---
In eighteen ninety-two, an Indian Councils Act allowed a little more Indian participation, but many felt it was too little. A new group of leaders, often called the Extremists, wanted bolder action and greater self-reliance. The best known were Bal Gangadhar Tilak in Maharashtra, Lala Lajpat Rai in Punjab, and Bipin Chandra Pal in Bengal, sometimes remembered together as Lal, Bal and Pal. Aurobindo Ghose was another important voice.

The turning point came in nineteen hundred and five. The Viceroy, Lord Curzon, announced the partition of Bengal. The official reason was that the province was too large to govern well. Many Indians saw a different purpose: to weaken the strong political life of Bengal by dividing it, including along religious lines. The partition came into effect in October nineteen hundred and five.

The response was the Swadeshi movement. Swadeshi means of one's own country. People boycotted British cloth and goods, burned foreign cloth in public, and set up Indian enterprises, from textile mills to banks and match factories. National schools and colleges were started. Rabindranath Tagore wrote songs that became part of the movement, including Amar Sonar Bangla. On the day the partition came into force, people tied rakhis to one another as a sign of unity.

The movement brought new groups into politics, especially students and women, though peasants joined less widely. The government responded with arrests and press controls.

--- part 5 ---
Some young people, disappointed with slow progress, turned to revolutionary methods. Their groups included the Anushilan Samiti in Bengal.

In nineteen hundred and six, the All-India Muslim League was founded at Dhaka. In nineteen hundred and seven, at the Surat session, the Congress split between the Moderates and the Extremists over methods and pace.

In nineteen hundred and nine came the Morley-Minto Reforms, formally the Indian Councils Act of that year. They enlarged the councils, but they also introduced separate electorates for Muslims, which meant Muslim voters would elect Muslim representatives on their own. Many historians see this as a step with long-lasting effects on Indian politics.

Finally, in nineteen eleven, the partition of Bengal was annulled. In the same year, the British announced that the capital would move from Calcutta to Delhi.

The war years and Home Rule.

We are now in the fourth part, the years of the First World War, from nineteen fourteen to nineteen eighteen.

India sent large numbers of soldiers and resources to the war. Prices rose, and taxes and recruitment pressed hard on many people. At the same time, many Indians hoped that loyal support in the war would be rewarded with self-government.

Abroad, Indian revolutionaries in North America formed the Ghadar Party in nineteen thirteen. It tried to spark an uprising among soldiers during the war, but the plans were discovered and many members were punished.

--- part 6 ---
Within India, a new kind of movement emerged: Home Rule. In nineteen sixteen, Tilak set up a Home Rule League in Maharashtra, Karnataka and the Central Provinces. Later the same year, Annie Besant set up another, working in the rest of India. They held meetings, ran newspapers and set up study circles. Their demand was simple: self-government for India within the British Empire.

Nineteen sixteen also brought two acts of unity. At the Lucknow session, the Moderates and Extremists came back together. And the Congress and the Muslim League agreed on a joint set of demands, known as the Lucknow Pact. The Congress accepted separate electorates as part of this agreement.

In August nineteen seventeen, the Secretary of State, Edwin Montagu, declared in Parliament that British policy was the gradual development of self-governing institutions in India, with a view to responsible government. Many Indians welcomed the words, but waited to see the deeds.

Gandhi arrives, and the shock of nineteen nineteen.

We are in the last part.

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi returned to India in January nineteen fifteen, after about two decades in South Africa. There, he had developed satyagraha, a method of struggle based on truth and non-violence. On the advice of Gokhale, he spent his first year travelling India to understand it.

His first experiments in India were local. In Champaran in Bihar, in nineteen seventeen, he took up the cause of peasants forced to grow indigo on unfair terms.

--- part 7 ---
In nineteen eighteen, he supported textile mill workers in Ahmedabad in a dispute over wages, and he backed peasants of Kheda in Gujarat who could not pay revenue after crop failure. These struggles made him known as a leader who stood with ordinary people.

Then came nineteen nineteen. The Rowlatt Act allowed people to be detained without trial in certain cases. Gandhi called for a nationwide satyagraha against it, with a hartal, a day of strikes and closures, in April. The response was large.

On the thirteenth of April, nineteen nineteen, the day of Baisakhi, a crowd gathered in Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar. Many had come for the festival; there was also a meeting in protest against arrests. General Dyer brought troops, blocked the exit and ordered them to fire on the unarmed crowd without warning. Hundreds were killed and many more wounded. Rabindranath Tagore gave up his knighthood in protest. The event deeply changed how many Indians saw British rule.

Later that year, the Government of India Act of nineteen nineteen, based on the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms, introduced dyarchy in the provinces. Some subjects were given to Indian ministers, while others stayed with officials. Many nationalists found the reforms far short of their hopes.

Now a recap. Eighteen fifty-seven ended Company rule. The Congress was born in eighteen eighty-five, with the Moderates' economic critique. The partition of Bengal in nineteen hundred and five sparked the Swadeshi movement.

--- part 8 ---
Separate electorates came in nineteen hundred and nine, the Lucknow Pact and Home Rule in nineteen sixteen, and Gandhi's local struggles in nineteen seventeen and eighteen. Nineteen nineteen brought the Rowlatt satyagraha, Jallianwala Bagh, and dyarchy. In part two, we will see how all this led to the mass movements that followed.
